# You Cannot Gift What You Have Surrendered: The Supreme Court on Gift Deeds That Contradict an Earlier Family Compromise

*A bench of the Supreme Court has set aside concurrent findings and decreed a suit to void a registered gift deed, because the donor had given up his claim to the gifted land in a compromise recorded by the High Court two decades earlier.*

## 1. Introduction

On 14 October 2026, a Division Bench of the Supreme Court of India, comprising Justice Sanjay Kumar and Justice Sanjeev Sachdeva, decided *B. Anasuyamma (died) per LR and another v. B. Madhusudhan Reddy (died) per LRs and others* [2026 INSC 1126]. The judgment, authored by Justice Sanjay Kumar, is short: thirteen operative paragraphs and a one-paragraph order. It is marked non-reportable. It nevertheless repays attention, because it addresses a problem that recurs constantly in Indian property practice. A family fixes its respective shares by a settlement. Years later, one member executes a registered instrument dealing with land that the settlement placed elsewhere. The instrument looks regular on its face, and two courts accept it. The question is what, if anything, survives of the earlier settlement.

The Supreme Court's answer was direct. A man who had surrendered his claim to certain lands in a compromise, recorded by the High Court and made the basis of a decree, could not later gift those same lands. Both the trial court and the Telangana High Court had upheld the gift. The Supreme Court held that they had failed to read the 1959 family settlement and the 1986 compromise correctly, and that their judgments rested on "fundamental errors". It decreed the suit in full.

This article first sets out the facts and procedural history, which span more than six decades and four generations of one family. It then analyses the reasoning. The Court cited no statute and no precedent, so the legal analysis in Section 3 is the author's own: it identifies the doctrines that the result necessarily engages, and says plainly where the judgment is silent. Section 4 draws out what the decision means for conveyancing, due diligence and litigation, and Section 5 concludes. The judgment was delivered within the last 48 hours, so no widening of the search window was needed.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 The family and the 1959 settlement

B. Venkat Reddy was married to Manikyamma. They had three sons: Sudhakar Reddy, Madhusudhan Reddy and Janardhan Reddy. Venkat Reddy and his eldest son Sudhakar died in 1948. Sudhakar's widow, Anasuyamma, adopted Venkatraji Reddy on 25 May 1975.

On 19 April 1959, Manikyamma, Anasuyamma, Madhusudhan Reddy and Janardhan Reddy entered into a family settlement dividing the ancestral properties. Manikyamma received 53 acres 10 guntas in Karkalpahad Village. Madhusudhan Reddy received 62 acres 9 guntas, of which only 1 acre 30 guntas lay in Karkalpahad Village (in Survey No. 15), the balance being in Vasudevapuram Village.

### 2.2 The partition litigation and the 1986 compromise

In 1977, Anasuyamma and Venkatraji Reddy sued in the City Civil Court, Hyderabad, for partition (O.S. No. 555 of 1977), tried together with a suit by Madhusudhan Reddy's vendees. By a common judgment of 21 April 1984, the Trial Court partly decreed the partition suit as to schedules B and C, but dismissed it as to schedule A, which it held to be covered by the 1959 settlement.

Madhusudhan Reddy appealed to the then Andhra Pradesh High Court (CCCA No. 86 of 1984). While the appeals were pending, he compromised with Anasuyamma and Venkatraji Reddy through a document titled "Settlement of Dispute" dated 24 February 1986. In the Court's account, Anasuyamma and Venkatraji Reddy gave up their one-third claim to the schedule B house at Himayatnagar, Hyderabad, which Madhusudhan Reddy took absolutely. He in turn gave up his claim to a share in his mother Manikyamma's properties, which were surrendered to Anasuyamma and Venkatraji Reddy, and paid them ₹2,20,000.

He then applied under Order XXIII, Rule 3 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CMP No. 9728 of 1988). The High Court recorded the compromise on 27 June 1988. By judgment of 7 September 1990 it allowed CCCA No. 86 of 1984 in light of that compromise and of a separate settlement between Madhusudhan Reddy and Venkat Reddy, Janardhan's son. It dismissed a connected appeal by Nirmala, Venkat Reddy's sister, leaving her to work out her rights in a separate suit.

### 2.3 The 2007 gift deed and the suit

On 24 April 2007, Madhusudhan Reddy executed registered Gift Deed No. 4257 of 2007 in favour of Siddhartha Reddy, son of Venkat Reddy and so Janardhan's grandson. The deed covered lands in Survey Nos. 2, 70, 130, 135, 136 and 137 of Karkalpahad Village. The Supreme Court observed that the 1959 allotment showed those lands had not fallen to Madhusudhan Reddy's share at all. They were Manikyamma's lands, and he had given up his claim to them in the 1986 compromise.

On 21 August 2007, Anasuyamma, Venkatraji Reddy and Venkatraji's sister by birth, Gunna Malinidevi, sued in the Court of the Senior Civil Judge, Mahabubnagar (O.S. No. 250 of 2007). They sought a declaration of title over seven parcels, a declaration that the gift deed was null and void, mutation of the revenue records, and a permanent injunction. Anasuyamma died during the suit.

### 2.4 The decisions below

The Senior Civil Judge dismissed the suit on 9 April 2012. The Supreme Court noted with surprise that the judge had held the 1990 compromise decree to have no evidentiary value.

The Telangana High Court dismissed the plaintiffs' appeal (Appeal Suit No. 372 of 2012) on 12 December 2022. The Supreme Court identified two specific errors. First, the High Court had observed that execution of the gift deed "was never disputed", overlooking that the suit itself had been filed within four months of the deed to challenge it. Second, it had held that Manikyamma, having died during the pendency of the suit, was entitled to no share, and so rejected Venkatraji Reddy's claim to be enjoying her property. That finding, the Supreme Court said, proceeded from a failure to note the contents of the 1959 settlement, which Madhusudhan Reddy had himself accepted, and of the 1986 compromise.

### 2.5 The Supreme Court's decision

Leave was granted in Special Leave Petition (C) No. 6930 of 2023. On 16 May 2023, the Court had directed status quo as to the nature and possession of the disputed lands. None of the respondents appeared, despite service and despite that order operating against them; the Court said that their failure "speaks for itself".

The Court held that the judgments of both courts, "[f]ounded on such fundamental errors", could not be sustained. It set aside the High Court judgment and the trial decree, and ordered that O.S. No. 250 of 2007 "stand decreed, as prayed for", with no order as to costs.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 What the Court decided, and what it did not say

The judgment is notable as much for its omissions as for its holding. It does not cite the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, the Registration Act, 1908, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 or any decision of any court. It does not state a ratio in terms. The proposition for which the decision will be cited, that a person who has parted with his share through a compromise cannot later gift the same property, comes from the headline of the LiveLaw report and not from the text, which simply narrates the documents and finds the lower courts' reading of them untenable. Every statement below about the legal basis of the result is therefore the author's reconstruction of the doctrines that the Court's reasoning must be resting on, and should be read as such.

### 3.2 The competence-to-transfer problem

The most direct explanation is that the donor had nothing to give. Section 7 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 makes competence to transfer depend, among other things, on the transferor being entitled to the property transferable. Section 122 defines a gift as a transfer of certain existing property, made voluntarily and without consideration, by a donor to a donee. A gift is an act of transfer, not merely an act of declaration. If the donor holds no interest in the land at the date of the deed, there is nothing existing in him to pass.

On the Court's account of the record, that was the position. The 1959 settlement allotted the gifted lands to Manikyamma, not to Madhusudhan Reddy; and the 1986 compromise recorded his surrender of any claim to what was Manikyamma's. In the Court's view, a registered gift deed executed in 2007 could not revive what had been surrendered in 1986. The registration of a deed, it bears repeating, proves execution; it does not enlarge the donor's title. This is an application of the old rule that a transferor cannot confer a better title than he has. The point is simple, but it is the point that both courts below appear to have missed.

### 3.3 The compromise decree as a bar

The second strand concerns the status of the 1986 compromise. The Supreme Court stresses that the compromise had "the imprimatur of the High Court", and it describes the 1990 judgment as a "compromise decree". Under Order XXIII, Rule 3 of the Code of Civil Procedure, a court that is satisfied that a suit has been adjusted wholly or in part by a lawful agreement or compromise in writing and signed by the parties, is to record it and pass a decree in accordance with it. A compromise recorded in this way is no longer a private contract. It takes the form of a decree, and the parties are bound by it.

The Senior Civil Judge's statement that the compromise decree had "no evidentiary value" is, in this light, the more surprising. Whatever the limits of evidentiary use of a judgment between parties, a decree between the same persons that determines their rights in the same properties is not a collateral piece of evidence. It is the operative disposition of those rights. A donor who was himself a party to the decree is hard put to deny what it records. The Court, notably, did not need to consider whether a plea of estoppel or res judicata was formally raised or applied, and it did not say so. But the result is consistent with both doctrines, and with the broader principle that a party cannot approbate and reprobate: take a benefit under a compromise (here, absolute ownership of the Himayatnagar house) and then act contrary to its burden (here, the surrender of the mother's lands).

### 3.4 The family settlement as an interpretive anchor

The third strand concerns the 1959 settlement. Indian courts have long treated family settlements with favour, as arrangements that bring peace to a family and are to be upheld rather than defeated by technical objection. The Court here did not invoke that line of authority by name, so this article does not attribute any particular case to it. What the judgment does show is how the 1959 document operated in practice: as the evidentiary baseline against which later dealings are tested. Madhusudhan Reddy had accepted it, the Trial Court in 1984 had treated it as covering schedule A, and the Supreme Court used it to demonstrate that the 2007 lands were never his. The High Court's error, in the Supreme Court's analysis, was to hold that Manikyamma "was not entitled to any share" because she had died during the suit. That reasoning treated the question as one of her personal entitlement in 2022, when the real question was what the 1959 settlement and the 1986 compromise had done to the lands and who had taken what under them.

### 3.5 Unresolved questions

A careful reader will find several matters on which the judgment is silent.

First, the position of third parties. The judgment records that third parties to whom Siddhartha Reddy "sought to alienate some lands" were respondents before the High Court and are respondents in the appeal. It does not say whether any of them acquired title, whether they purchased for value without notice, or whether the decree binds them. Because the transfer to Siddhartha Reddy is declared void and the status quo order of 16 May 2023 pre-dates any final decision, the practical consequence for those alienees is left open. Section 41 of the Transfer of Property Act, which protects certain transferees from ostensible owners, was not discussed; whether it could assist them is left open.

Second, the position of Nirmala, whose appeal the High Court dismissed in 1990 while leaving her to a separate suit, and of the other family branches. The 2026 decree operates between the parties to O.S. No. 250 of 2007 only.

Third, the character of the 2007 gift. Siddhartha Reddy was not a stranger: he was the son of Venkat Reddy, who had himself settled with Madhusudhan Reddy before the High Court. The judgment does not examine whether he had notice of the earlier arrangements, and notice was not needed for the result, because the donor's want of title decided the matter. A donee under a gift gives no consideration, so the usual protections available to purchasers for value are in any event unavailable to him.

### 3.6 Assessment

The reasoning is brief, but it is sound in its essentials. The strength of the judgment is that it decides the case on the documents. The decision requires no new principle, and its confidence is justified by the fact that the lower courts' contrary conclusions could be reached only by ignoring the 1959 settlement and the 1986 compromise. Its weakness is that an opinion without statutes or authority offers little to later litigants who wish to rely on it, and, being non-reportable, it carries limited precedential force. Its value is illustrative.

## 4. Practical Significance

### 4.1 Conveyancing and due diligence

The case is a lesson in what a title search must cover. A purchaser or donee in a family-property transaction cannot stop at the registered instrument in the vendor's hand and the mutation entries. The prudent inquiry reaches back to the origin of the vendor's title: any family settlement, partition deed or decree under which the property devolved, and any litigation, compromise or recorded settlement among the family. Here, the 1959 allotment list showed that Madhusudhan Reddy held only 1 acre 30 guntas in Karkalpahad Village, which any donee's advisers could have checked against the gifted survey numbers.

Searches should also extend to the court record. Compromise decrees are not always entered in the revenue or registration records. The 1990 High Court judgment here sat in a case file, not in the sub-registrar's index. Advocates certifying title should ask directly whether any suit among family members concerned the property, and should obtain certified copies of any decree.

### 4.2 Drafting

For family settlements and compromises, the case underlines the value of precision. The 1986 compromise, as the Court describes it, surrendered Madhusudhan Reddy's claim to "the properties of his mother", and the dispute over whether the 2007 lands fell within that description ran for nineteen years through three courts. Parties should schedule every parcel by survey number and extent, state in terms that the surrendering party has no residual right, and, where the compromise is to be made a decree, ensure that the schedule is carried into the decree. Releases should be expressed to bind legal heirs and assigns.

### 4.3 Litigation strategy

Plaintiffs challenging a later transaction should plead the earlier settlement and compromise in the plaint, exhibit certified copies of the decree and put the donor's own participation in it squarely in issue. The plaintiffs in this case did that, and ultimately succeeded. Their success took nineteen years from the filing of the suit. Defendants who rely on a regular registered instrument should expect the court to examine the donor's title first and the formalities second.

On appeal, the decision is a reminder that concurrent findings are not immune where the courts below ignore documents that decide the case. Counsel seeking special leave should frame the grounds as failures to read the record, with page references, and not as a general plea for re-appreciation.

## 5. Conclusion

*B. Anasuyamma v. B. Madhusudhan Reddy* is a modest judgment with a clear lesson. A compromise recorded by a court binds the parties and their successors, and a later registered deed cannot undo it. The gift deed of 2007 failed not because it was irregularly executed, but because the donor had already surrendered his claim to its subject-matter. The courts below went wrong by treating the later document as the starting point of the inquiry, when the earlier documents had already settled who owned what.

For practitioners, the decision does not break new doctrinal ground, and its non-reportable status and silence on authority limit its use as precedent. It is nonetheless a useful, recent illustration that Indian courts will test a registered instrument against the donor's actual title, and that the Supreme Court will correct concurrent findings which overlook a compromise decree. The questions it leaves open, chiefly the position of third-party alienees and the interplay with the protection of transferees under Section 41 of the Transfer of Property Act, are likely to arise in later cases.

A fuller report of the judgment, or a successor decision citing it, may supply the legal reasoning that this judgment leaves implicit. Until then, the safest reading is the narrowest: a person who has given up his claim to property in a court-recorded compromise has nothing left to gift.

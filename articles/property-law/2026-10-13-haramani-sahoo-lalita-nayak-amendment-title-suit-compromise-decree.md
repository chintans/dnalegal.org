# Amending a Title Suit Fourteen Years On: *Haramani Sahoo v. Lalita Nayak* and the Limits of Due Diligence

*On 12 October 2026 the Supreme Court allowed a plaintiff in a Cuttack land suit to challenge a compromise decree he had not known about, by amendment of the plaint, long after trial had begun, while leaving limitation and maintainability open for the trial court.*

## 1. Introduction

Land suits in India age badly. A plaint filed to correct two sale deeds in 2009 can, a decade later, sit amid a chain of further sales, a partition from 1940 and a compromise decree in a different suit that the plaintiff never saw. The question in *Haramani Sahoo v. Lalita Nayak* is what a civil court should do when the plaintiff discovers such a decree late and asks to plead it. Should the court refuse because the suit is old and trial has started, or allow the amendment because the title dispute cannot be settled without it?

A bench of B.V. Nagarathna and R. Mahadevan, JJ., in a judgment written by Mahadevan, J., chose the second course. It set aside the orders of the trial court at Cuttack and of the Orissa High Court, allowed the amendment under Order VI Rule 17 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC), and imposed costs of Rs. 25,000. The judgment is procedural in form. It is nonetheless a property-law decision in substance, because the amendment concerns title, possession and the validity of a decree that affects part of the suit land.

This article makes three arguments. First, the judgment confirms that "due diligence" under the proviso to Order VI Rule 17 is measured from when a party learned of the material fact, not from when the suit began. Second, by declining to decide limitation and the bar in Order XXIII Rule 3A CPC at the amendment stage, the Court keeps a disciplined line between permission to plead and a ruling on merit. Third, the approach has costs for defendants, which the Court meets only partly through a modest award of costs. Section 2 sets out the facts and the holding. Section 3 analyses the reasoning. Section 4 draws out the consequences for practice. Section 5 concludes.

The judgment was retrieved from the AdvocateKhoj reproduction of the text. The reporting citation was not available at the time of writing, so the case is described as not yet reported.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 The suit and the chain of title

The case is Civil Appeal No. 13681 of 2026, arising out of SLP (C) No. 25635 of 2023. The original plaintiff, Rabindranath Sahoo, filed C.S. No. 86 of 2009 before the Senior Civil Judge, Cuttack. He sought a declaration of title, recovery of possession, and rectification of sale deeds dated 15 February 1971 and 28 July 1978, with consequential reliefs. The land lies in Cuttack, in Hal Plots 3370/4045 and 3371/4050.

The judgment records a layered chain of title. For Plot 3370/4045, the land passed from the third defendant to the second defendant in 1976, from the second to the first in 2005, and from the first to the eleventh defendant in 2015. For Plot 3371/4050, the eighth defendant sold to the ninth and tenth defendants in 2007.

The plaint was amended before the application now in issue. After the ninth and tenth defendants filed their written statement, the plaintiff amended to plead that Plot 3371/4050 was vacant land belonging to one Mayadhar Mohapatra under a mutual partition of 1940. The ninth and tenth defendants denied this. The respondents also point to an earlier amendment, in 2017, which was allowed on costs.

### 2.2 The second amendment

The amendment in dispute came later. The plaintiff said that he learned of a compromise decree in T.S. No. 105 of 1998, a suit to which he was not a party. He sought to plead it and to have the final decree declared null and void. According to the judgment, he obtained certified copies of the relevant documents on 22 June 2020. The trial court rejected the application on 21 September 2022. The High Court dismissed the connected civil miscellaneous petition (C.M.P. No. 1091 of 2022) on 10 April 2023. The original plaintiff died while the matter was pending, and his heirs were substituted and became the appellants.

### 2.3 The parties' positions

The appellants argued that the transactions are complex and run back to 1940, which made repeated corrections understandable. They relied on a 2007 order of the Tahsildar that, in their account, showed confusion over multiple sale deeds, and said that the respondents had not disclosed their full source of title. Relying on *A.K. Gupta & Sons Ltd. v. Damodar Valley Corporation* (AIR 1967 SC 96), they contended that the amendment raised no new case and no new cause of action. Relying on *Abdul Rehman v. Mohd. Ruldu* ((2012) 11 SCC 341), they said that limitation must be examined on the facts and not assumed.

The contesting respondents (respondents 1 and 9 to 11) said that the plaintiff had protracted the case through repeated amendments since 2007. They stressed that the application came about fourteen years after institution and that the plaintiff had explained neither the delay nor the date of knowledge. Relying on *Basavaraj v. Indira* (2024 INSC 151), they argued that the amendment would alter the fundamental character of the suit. They added that Order XXIII Rule 3A CPC bars a separate suit to challenge a compromise decree on the ground that the compromise was unlawful, so that the amendment was an attempt to do indirectly what the Code forbids. They also said that the claim was ex facie time-barred.

### 2.4 The Court's holding

The Court allowed the appeal. It set aside both earlier orders, allowed the amendment application subject to costs of Rs. 25,000 to the contesting respondents payable within two weeks, and directed the appellants to carry out the amendment within four weeks. The respondents may file additional written statements within the time the trial court fixes.

The Court expressly confined what it decided. It clarified that allowing the amendment does not accept the averments about the compromise decree, grant any declaration, or confer any substantive right. Limitation, maintainability, the effect of Order XXIII Rule 3A, the validity and binding effect of the decree in T.S. No. 105 of 1998, title, possession and the merits were all left to the trial court.

### 2.5 The reasoning in outline

The Court began from the settled position that Order VI Rule 17 is discretionary and that, once trial has commenced, the proviso permits amendment only if the party could not, despite due diligence, have raised the matter earlier. It surveyed the amendment cases cited by the parties, among them *Rajesh Kumar Aggarwal v. K.K. Modi* ((2006) 4 SCC 385) on the "real controversy" test, *Revajeetu Builders v. Narayanaswamy* ((2009) 10 SCC 84) on the relevant factors, and *Life Insurance Corporation of India v. Sanjeev Builders* ((2022) 16 SCC 1) on a liberal approach. It then held that the diligence proviso was satisfied, that the amendment enlarged the questions but did not change the character of the suit, that arguable limitation need not defeat an amendment at the threshold, and that prejudice could be cured by costs and additional written statements.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory frame

Order VI Rule 17 CPC gives the court power to allow amendment of pleadings "at any stage of the proceedings" on terms that are just, and requires that all amendments necessary to determine the real questions in controversy be allowed. The proviso, added by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 2002, restricts this once trial has begun. The court may not allow the amendment unless satisfied that, despite due diligence, the party could not have raised the matter before the commencement of trial.

The Court's judgment does not pretend that the proviso is absent. Its approach is to apply it faithfully but with attention to what the amendment is about. That is the first point worth noticing. A common failing in amendment disputes is to treat the proviso as if it were a limitation clause on the very idea of amendment. The Court treats it instead as a diligence inquiry tied to the specific fact sought to be pleaded.

### 3.2 Due diligence is fact-specific, not suit-specific

The respondents' strongest argument was delay. A suit instituted in 2009, with an amendment application decided in 2022, invites the thought that the plaintiff has had ample time. The Court did not accept that framing. The new fact was a compromise decree in a different suit from 1998, and the plaintiff said he learned of it when certified copies became available in June 2020. The relevant diligence question is therefore what the plaintiff did, or could have done, about that fact, not how long the suit had been pending.

This is analytically sound. The proviso asks whether the party "could not have raised the matter" earlier. A matter that the party did not know about cannot ordinarily be raised. What the proviso guards against is the party who knew, or with reasonable inquiry would have known, and simply held back. The difficulty lies in the middle ground, where the party's ignorance is itself the product of inattention. The Court's observation that the explanation "was not mere negligence" shows it is alert to that middle ground, but its reasoning on the point is brief, and it declined to fix the date of knowledge finally. A cautious reading is that the Court accepted the explanation as sufficient for the purposes of the proviso without making a finding of fact that would bind the trial court.

One may fairly ask whether that is satisfactory. A plaintiff in a suit about two plots, one of which was the subject of a compromise decree, might be expected to search court records for litigation over the same land. The respondents pressed the point, and the Court's answer is largely an application of the liberal tradition in *Revajeetu Builders* and *Sanjeev Builders* rather than a close inquiry into what a reasonably diligent plaintiff would have found. The strength of the reasoning is that it keeps the inquiry tied to evidence; the weakness is that it leaves little guidance on how much searching of public records the proviso demands in a land dispute.

### 3.3 The character of the suit

The second pillar is that the amendment does not change the nature of the suit. The Court's line of authority here is long. Amendment is refused where it substitutes a different cause of action or converts one kind of suit into another. It is allowed where it adds or alters facts or relief within the same subject matter. The plaintiff's original claim was a declaration of title and recovery of possession; the amendment adds a challenge to a decree that affects part of the same property. The Court treated the subject matter as substantially overlapping, so that the suit remains a title suit.

The treatment of *Basavaraj* is useful. The respondents used it to argue a fundamental alteration. The Court distinguished it on its facts. Since the judgment as available does not reproduce the full factual comparison, readers should consult the certified text before relying on the distinction. What can be said is that the Court draws the boundary by reference to the overlap of subject matter and relief, and that is consistent with *Abdul Rehman*.

### 3.4 Limitation and the separation of stages

The most instructive feature of the judgment is its handling of limitation. The settled principle, repeated in the judgment through *Ragu Thilak* and *Sampat Kumar*, is that an amendment should not be refused merely because a fresh claim would be time-barred if it is arguable, and that the defendant keeps the right to plead limitation. That keeps the amendment application from becoming a mini-trial.

The Court applied this faithfully. The claim to set aside a decree on a suit-based challenge raises obvious limitation questions, and the amendment also touches Order XXIII Rule 3A CPC, which bars a suit to set aside a decree on the ground that the compromise was not lawful. The Court expressly did not decide whether the bar applies to a challenge by someone who was not a party to the compromise suit. That is a real and unsettled question in practice. A non-party claiming that a compromise decree binds his property without his consent invokes ideas of fraud and of the rights of strangers to a decree, which may take the case outside the bar. The Court did not say so. It stated only that the trial court will decide.

The separation of stages is defensible. It also carries a price. The defendants must now file additional written statements and meet a claim that may ultimately fail on limitation or on Order XXIII Rule 3A as a matter of law. A court could have asked, at least in the case of the Rule 3A point, whether the bar was so plain that allowing the amendment would only waste time. The Court's answer is that the question depends on facts and legal argument that should be heard on a full record. Given the stated reliance on arguability, that is a reasonable exercise of discretion, although a more direct engagement with the Rule 3A point would have helped trial courts.

### 3.5 Prejudice, multiplicity and costs

The Court treated the prejudice of delay as compensable in costs, and recognised that refusal might simply lead to a separate proceeding on the same land with a risk of inconsistent findings. The second consideration deserves emphasis. In title disputes, avoiding multiplicity is not a procedural nicety: two courts determining rights in the same plots can produce irreconcilable outcomes affecting every later purchaser. The award of Rs. 25,000 is small against fourteen years of litigation, and is better read as a signal than as compensation.

## 4. Practical significance

### 4.1 For plaintiffs and their advocates

The principal lesson is to document the date and manner of discovery. The Court's reasoning turned on the plaintiff's explanation of when and how the compromise decree came to light, namely certified copies obtained on 22 June 2020. A plaintiff who seeks late amendment should file an affidavit setting out the date of knowledge, the source of the information, and the steps taken after learning. A bare statement that the amendment is "necessary" invites the response that diligence has not been shown, and the respondents will point to the delay.

Plaintiffs should also note what the judgment does not do. It does not hold that a late amendment is available as of right. It rests on arguable explanation, overlap of subject matter and curable prejudice. A plaintiff who has amended several times, as this one had, should expect scrutiny and should expect to pay costs.

### 4.2 For defendants

Defendants cannot assume that age of suit defeats an amendment. The effective resistance is not delay alone but a showing that the plaintiff knew the fact earlier, that the amendment introduces a new cause of action of a different kind, or that the claim is so plainly barred that allowing it serves no purpose. Defendants should plead limitation and Order XXIII Rule 3A in their additional written statement, because the Court has expressly preserved those points for the trial court.

### 4.3 For purchasers and conveyancers

The judgment is a reminder that title in India can be unsettled by decrees that a purchaser never saw. The compromise decree in a 1998 suit became relevant to a 2009 suit and to sales made in 2005, 2007 and 2015. Due diligence on land should include a search for litigation affecting the plot and its predecessors, including suits in which the transferor was a party, and compromise decrees. Title searches commonly examine registered documents and revenue records. They less often examine the civil court dockets of earlier decades. The practical response is to extend the search to the court records of the district where the land lies, to obtain certified copies of any decree affecting the property, and to ask the seller for a disclosure of past and pending proceedings.

## 5. Conclusion

*Haramani Sahoo v. Lalita Nayak* is not a landmark on the scale of a judgment that reshapes a statute. It is a careful application of settled amendment principles to a land dispute with a complicated history. Its holding is narrow: a plaintiff who credibly explains late discovery of a compromise decree affecting part of the suit property may amend to plead it, even after trial has begun, and the court need not decide limitation or the Order XXIII Rule 3A bar at that stage.

The reasoning is persuasive on the central point. Tying the diligence inquiry to the date of knowledge of the particular fact is faithful to the text of the proviso, and the avoidance of multiplicity in title litigation is a weighty consideration. The reasoning is less satisfying in two respects. It gives little guidance on how far a plaintiff must search public court records before claiming ignorance, and it leaves unaddressed the question whether Order XXIII Rule 3A can bar a non-party's challenge, which will now arise before the Cuttack court.

For practitioners, the judgment suggests three habits: record the date and source of discovery, plead limitation and statutory bars at the first opportunity after an amendment is allowed, and extend title due diligence to the civil court records of earlier decades. The next stage of this case, in the trial court, will show whether the amendment strengthens the plaintiff's title claim or simply adds a further layer to a suit already fourteen years old. Either way, the Supreme Court has been explicit that the hard questions are still to be decided.

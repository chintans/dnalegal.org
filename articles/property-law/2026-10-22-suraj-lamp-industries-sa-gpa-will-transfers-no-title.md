# A Power of Attorney Is Not a Sale Deed: *Suraj Lamp & Industries* and the End of SA/GPA/Will Transfers

*No qualifying new property law judgment was found within the 7-day search window, so this article examines a landmark ruling of continuing importance.*

*The Supreme Court's 2011 judgment holds that a bundle of an agreement to sell, a general power of attorney and a will conveys no title to immovable property, however much the purchaser has paid.*

## 1. Introduction

Every property lawyer in northern India has met the file. A buyer paid the full price years ago, took possession, and holds an agreement of sale, a general power of attorney and a will, but no registered sale deed. The seller now refuses to execute one, or has sold again. The buyer asks the court to declare that these documents made them the owner.

*Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana*, decided on 11 October 2011 by a bench of R.V. Raveendran, A.K. Patnaik and H.L. Gokhale, JJ., answered that question firmly. The Court held that "GPA sales" and "SA/GPA/WILL transfers" are not transfers at all. This article sets out what was decided and why, assesses the reasoning, and identifies the consequences that still matter for conveyancing, due diligence and litigation.

## 2. Case summary

**Background.** The proceedings arose from Special Leave Petition (C) No. 13917 of 2009. By an earlier order of 15 May 2009, reported at 2009 (7) SCC 363, the Court had remarked on the ill-effects of GPA sales and SA/GPA/Will transfers. The Court heard Mr. Gopal Subramanium, Senior Counsel, as amicus curiae, and records that the Union and the four States consulted (Delhi, Haryana, Punjab and Uttar Pradesh) agreed such transfers should be curbed (para 4).

**The practice.** The Court described the usual modus operandi (para 2). The vendor receives the full consideration and delivers possession. The vendor then executes an agreement of sale (or an agreement with a separate affidavit confirming payment and possession), an irrevocable general power of attorney in favour of the purchaser or a nominee, and a will bequeathing the property to the purchaser as insurance against the vendor's death. The Court said that both labels are "misnomers", since there can be neither a sale by power of attorney nor a transfer by agreement, power and will taken together. It identified the motives: avoiding stamp duty, registration charges and capital gains, and parking unaccounted money (para 2).

**Holding.** The Court worked through the instruments one at a time.

- *Agreement of sale.* Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (the TP Act) says that a contract for sale "does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on" the property. Relying on *Narandas Karsondas v. S.A. Kamtam* (1977) 3 SCC 247 and *Rambhau Namdeo Gajre v. Narayan Bapuji Dhotra* (2004) 8 SCC 614, the Court held that only a registered deed of conveyance transfers title. An agreement of sale, with or without possession, is not a conveyance. The only protection it offers is the limited shield of Section 53A of the TP Act (part performance), which operates against the transferor and not against third parties (paras 11-12).
- *Power of attorney.* A power of attorney creates an agency, not a transfer. It is revocable unless made irrevocable in a manner known to law, and "even an irrevocable attorney does not have the effect of transferring title to the grantee". The Court quoted *State of Rajasthan v. Basant Nehata* (2005) 12 SCC 77. An attorney may, however, execute a deed of conveyance on the grantor's behalf, which does convey title (para 13).
- *Will.* A will is a posthumous disposition, revocable until death and not a transfer inter vivos. Registering it adds nothing (para 14).

The conclusion is stated in paragraphs 15 and 16. SA/GPA/Will transactions neither convey title nor create any interest in immovable property. Courts will not treat them as completed transfers. They "cannot be relied upon or made the basis for mutations in Municipal or Revenue Records", and the same applies to leasehold property, which can pass only by a registered assignment of lease. The Court disapproved observations in *Asha M. Jain v. Canara Bank*, 94 (2001) DLT 841, and held that decisions treating such transactions as concluded transfers "are not good law".

**Hardship and genuine transactions.** The Court refused to make the ruling prospective, saying it had "merely drawn attention to and reiterated the well-settled legal position" (paras 17-18). It added safeguards. The documents can still be treated as an existing agreement of sale, used to seek specific performance or to defend possession under Section 53A, and used by pre-judgment purchasers to seek regularisation of allotments or leases with development authorities. Mutations already effected by development, municipal or revenue authorities need not be disturbed merely because of the decision. The ruling does not affect genuine powers of attorney, such as one given to a spouse, child or relative to manage affairs or execute a conveyance, or development agreements with builders (para 19).

## 3. Legal analysis

**The statutory core is simple, and that is the strength of the decision.** Sections 5 and 54 of the TP Act define a sale as a transfer of ownership, which for tangible immovable property worth Rs. 100 or more can be made only by a registered instrument. Section 17(1)(b) of the Registration Act, 1908 makes non-testamentary instruments that create or assign title compulsorily registrable. The Court also extracted Section 17(1A), inserted in 2001, under which an agreement to transfer for the purposes of Section 53A has no effect for that section unless registered. The Court refused to let documents that individually convey nothing do collectively what the statute reserves for a registered conveyance.

**Reasoning by elimination.** If an agreement of sale creates only a personal obligation, a power of attorney only an agency, and a will only a revocable testamentary intent, a bundle of the three cannot be more than the sum of its parts. I regard this as correct.

**The prospectivity question.** The most contestable part of the judgment is its treatment of hardship. The Court said it was only restating settled law, so no prospective operation was needed. Yet the paragraph 18 protections, and the decision to leave existing mutations undisturbed, concede that many people had acted on a contrary understanding, which *Asha M. Jain* had arguably encouraged. In substance the Court gave a limited transitional accommodation while declining to call it one. That keeps the doctrine clean but leaves open how far pre-2011 documents may be used for relief other than those listed.

**Shield, not sword.** The Court preserved Section 53A as the one limited respect in which such documents have effect. Following *Rambhau Namdeo Gajre*, it is a shield against the transferor, not a source of ownership or a right enforceable against third parties. A buyer in possession can resist the seller but cannot, on this footing alone, sue strangers as owner.

**Unresolved questions.** The judgment does not map the line between a "genuine" power of attorney and a sham one beyond the examples in paragraph 19, so courts must look at purpose and context: consideration received, possession delivered, and an irrevocable grant to a non-relative. Nor did the Court decide stamp duty; it noted that States differ (paras 4, 8).

## 4. Practical significance

**Due diligence.** A title search must treat any chain that passes through an SA/GPA/Will as broken at that link. Counsel advising buyers or lenders should require a registered conveyance from the person with title, or from an attorney acting under a genuine power that expressly authorises the conveyance. A registered will does not change this.

**Drafting.** For a buyer who has already paid but cannot yet obtain a sale deed, the document must be drafted and treated as what the law says it is: an agreement to sell, to be registered under Section 17(1A) of the Registration Act so that Section 53A can be invoked. Powers of attorney should state the genuine relationship and purpose. A grant that merely recites receipt of the full price and delegates every right of disposal to a non-relative invites exactly the characterisation the Court rejected.

**Litigation strategy.** A plaintiff holding only these documents should plead specific performance and, alternatively, Section 53A protection. A suit for a bare declaration of ownership on the strength of SA/GPA/Will is likely to fail. Defendants can rely on *Suraj Lamp* to challenge the plaintiff's title.

## 5. Conclusion

*Suraj Lamp* is short on novelty and long on consequence. It restates the TP Act and the Registration Act in plain terms: ownership of immovable property passes only through a registered deed of conveyance, and an agency, a bare agreement or a testamentary promise cannot stand in for one. Its guardrails matter as much as the rule. Genuine powers of attorney and development agreements survive, and Section 53A still protects a buyer in possession against the seller. The weak point is the claim that the ruling merely restated settled law while the paragraph 18 accommodations softened its effect, leaving some older transactions with uncertain remedies. The safest course for buyers, lenders and advisers remains the one the Court identified: obtain a registered sale deed, and treat anything less as an agreement that may support specific performance but does not make the holder an owner.

# Sword and Shield: Adverse Possession After *Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur*

*A three-judge bench held that a person who has perfected title by adverse possession may sue on that title, and not merely plead it in defence.*

*No qualifying recent property law development was found within the search window (the 7 days to 17 October 2026), so this article addresses a settled principle of continuing importance.*

## 1. Introduction

Adverse possession lets a person who has occupied another's immovable property, openly and hostilely, for the limitation period acquire the owner's title. Courts have called the rule harsh, yet it remains good law. Its procedural reach was fixed by a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court of India in *Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur* (Civil Appeal No. 7764 of 2014), decided on 7 August 2019 by Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M.R. Shah, JJ.

The question was whether adverse possession is only a shield, available to a defendant resisting eviction, or also a sword, usable by a possessor to sue for a declaration of title or for recovery of possession. Earlier two-judge decisions, including *Gurudwara Sahab v. Gram Panchayat Village Sirthala*, had taken the former view. The bench rejected it.

## 2. Case summary

The reports frame the issue as whether a person claiming title by adverse possession can maintain a suit under Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963. Four suits were in view: for a declaration of title; for a permanent injunction against interference with possession; for restoration after dispossession by a defendant whose own title the plaintiff's possession had extinguished; and for restoration after dispossession by a third party.

The earlier line of authority, as described, treated adverse possession as purely defensive. A possessor could resist ejectment but could not seek a declaration of ownership. A possessor with perfected title could therefore be thrown out and have no cause of action to return.

The bench overruled that line. It held that the plea of acquisition of title by adverse possession can be taken by a plaintiff under Article 65. LiveLaw reproduces the formulation that adverse possession "can be used as a sword by the plaintiff as well as a shield by the defendant", and that "any person who has perfected title by way of adverse possession, can file a suit for restoration of possession". The Court added that nothing in the Limitation Act, 1963 bars a plaintiff from suing on such title.

The reasoning has four strands. A person in possession "cannot be ousted by another person except by due procedure of law". Once twelve years have run, the owner's right to eject is lost and the possessor acquires the right, title and interest of the person against whom he has prescribed. Because title is acquired, it can be asserted offensively. And "By perfection of title on extinguishment of the owner's title, a person cannot be remediless."

Two refinements follow. A possessory suit lies under Article 64 even before title ripens, where someone takes the law into their own hands. And a plaintiff with perfected title can evict the former owner or anyone else, unless that person has himself perfected title against the plaintiff. The Court also noted judicial reluctance to recognise adverse possession over property dedicated to public use.

## 3. Legal analysis

Two provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963 carry the argument. Article 65 prescribes twelve years for a suit for possession of immovable property based on title. Section 27 extinguishes the right to property at the end of the period limited for a suit for possession. The defensive-only view read these narrowly: limitation barred the owner's remedy but vested nothing the possessor could enforce. The bench, on the reported reasoning, treated extinguishment and acquisition as two sides of one event. If the owner's right is extinguished, someone holds what he lost, and that someone is the possessor.

The reasoning is persuasive on coherence. The defensive-only rule made title by adverse possession a lottery, enforceable only if the former owner happened to sue first. The sword-and-shield formulation aligns remedy with right. It also lets a possessor convert an inchoate position into an adjudicated title that can be recorded and eventually sold, which serves the doctrine's own purpose of quieting long-settled possession.

The ruling does not dilute the elements of the plea. The possessor must still prove possession that is open, continuous, exclusive and hostile for the full period, and when it became adverse. The Court's caution about property dedicated to public use signals that the doctrine will not run freely against temples, trusts or public land.

The critique lies in policy, not construction. Allowing the possessor to open the litigation means an owner may first learn of the claim through a plaint. That is an argument for legislative reform of the period or the conditions of the doctrine, not a sign that the bench misread the statute, which it applied as enacted. The reports also leave open how a purchaser should treat an unadjudicated claim of this kind against a registered chain of title.

The Supreme Court has since applied the decision. In *Darshan Kaur Bhatia v. Ramesh Gandhi* (Civil Appeal Nos. 1701-1702 of 2022, reported as 2022 LiveLaw (SC) 246), Sanjay Kishan Kaul and M.M. Sundresh, JJ. set aside the rejection of a plaint under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 in a suit for a declaration of title based on matured adverse possession. The High Court had held the plea purely defensive. The Supreme Court said the position "now stands enunciated to the contrary" in *Ravinder Kaur Grewal*, (2019) 8 SCC 729, and held the suit maintainable. The 2019 ruling therefore operates as binding authority at the pleading stage.

## 4. Practical significance

**Possessors.** A person with twelve or more years of hostile possession has a clear route to a declaration and to recovery if dispossessed. Build the record early: receipts and utility connections in their own name, documentary acts of ownership, and a precise pleading of the date from which possession became adverse. Avoid pleading ownership under a title deed in the alternative, which invites the argument that possession was never hostile.

**Owners.** Limitation is the owner's principal risk. Owners of vacant or inherited land, particularly non-resident owners and joint families, should inspect periodically, keep records of possession and sue within time. A long-silent owner now faces an affirmative suit, not merely a defence.

**Purchasers and lenders.** Due diligence must look beyond the registered chain. Inspect the property, ask who occupies it and on what basis, and consider whether that occupation has run twelve years. A seller's title may have been extinguished though the registered chain is unbroken. Warranties and indemnities on possession help allocate the risk but do not cure a defect in title.

**Litigants and courts.** After *Darshan Kaur Bhatia*, an application to reject a plaint on the ground that adverse possession is only a defence will fail. Defendants must contest continuity, hostility and the starting date. Plaintiffs must plead particulars with precision, since a vague plea of long possession will not carry the burden.

## 5. Conclusion

*Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur* completed a doctrine left half-built. Once limitation was understood to extinguish the owner's right and vest title in the possessor, denying the possessor an offensive remedy could not be justified. *Darshan Kaur Bhatia* has since confirmed that the principle governs at the threshold of litigation.

The decision settles the procedural question and leaves the policy question where it was. Any serious reform will have to come from Parliament. Until then the safest course is vigilance for owners, documentation for possessors and inspection for purchasers.

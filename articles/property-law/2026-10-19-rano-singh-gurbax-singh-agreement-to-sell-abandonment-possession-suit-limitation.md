# An Abandoned Agreement to Sell Cannot Found a Suit for Possession: The Supreme Court on Title, Limitation and Part Performance

*After fifty-one years in the courts, the Supreme Court has held that heirs of an allottee who abandoned his agreement to sell cannot recover a Delhi plot by a bare suit for possession: the agreement conferred no title, the registered sale deed against them was never challenged in time, and neither estoppel nor part performance came to their aid.*

## 1. Introduction

On 16 October 2026, a Division Bench of the Supreme Court of India comprising Justice Aravind Kumar and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi decided *Smt. Rano Singh and others v. Sh. Gurbax Singh (D) through LRs and others* [2026 INSC 1137] in Civil Appeal No. 7025 of 2012. Justice Kumar authored the judgment. The Court dismissed an appeal by the heirs of the original allottee of Plot No. 8, Friends Colony, New Delhi, measuring about 4,132 square yards, and upheld the concurrent dismissal of their suit for possession and mesne profits. That suit had been instituted in 1975.

*Note on the search window.* No qualifying judgment was located within the last 48 hours; this article therefore applies the seven-day window, and the judgment falls three days before the date of writing.

The judgment is worth studying not for a new rule but for the way it assembles four settled strands of property law into a single, practical warning. First, an agreement to sell does not transfer title. Second, a plaintiff whose title is clouded by a registered instrument must sue for the declaratory or cancellatory relief that removes the cloud, and must do so within the three-year period under the Limitation Act, 1963. Third, a person who deliberately tells a promisor that he will not perform, and watches a third party act on that statement, is estopped from later going back on it. Fourth, the shield of Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 is lost by a transferee who walks away from his own obligations.

Section 2 sets out the facts and the Court's reasoning. Section 3 analyses the decision, including where I think the Court's treatment of limitation is open to question. Section 4 draws out the consequences for conveyancing, due diligence and litigation. Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 Facts and procedural history

Sohan Singh joined the Nathu Ram Friends Colony Co-operative Housing Building Society Ltd. (the Society) in 1950. By an agreement to sell dated 14 January 1952, the Society allotted him the suit plot for Rs 11,824. Clause 7 obliged him to build a house within three years, failing which he would lose the right to obtain a sale. On 3 December 1960 the Society executed a registered sale deed of the plot in favour of Gurbax Singh (Defendant No. 1).

The plaintiffs, Sohan Singh's heirs, said that a friend, Sobha Singh, built the house as Sohan Singh's agent, that a Society resolution of 30 August 1959 recorded Sohan Singh as owner, and that the 1960 deed was a collusive sham. They said they learned of it on 23 May 1963.

Defendant No. 1 said that by a letter of 4 October 1954, written before the construction deadline, Sohan Singh confessed he could not build and asked the Society to transfer the plot to Gurbax Singh, whom he called his "nephew". The Society resolved accordingly. Defendant No. 1 took possession in 1954, paid the dues, built a two-storeyed house at his own cost by 1959,. He pleaded adverse possession in the alternative.

The plaintiffs complained to the Registrar of Co-operative Societies in 1964. On the Registrar's direction the Society filed Suit No. 235 of 1966 for a declaration that the 1960 deed was valid; it was dismissed for default. The plaintiffs filed Suit No. 475 of 1975 for possession and mesne profits only. They sought no cancellation, specific performance or declaration. The trial court dismissed the suit on 14 January 2010, holding the plaintiffs estopped. The Delhi High Court affirmed on 2 February 2012 in RFA No. 377/2010, adding that Section 53A was unavailable and that the suit was time-barred.

### 2.2 The Supreme Court's holding

The Court found no ground to interfere.

**Limitation.** A suit for specific performance (Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963) expired in 1958, or at latest in 1966 from the plaintiffs' own date of knowledge. A suit to cancel the deed (Article 59) also expired by 1966. A suit for possession, treated under Article 64 and Section 27, required dispossession; the Court found none, because Sohan Singh's letter showed an intention to part with the plot, and twelve years from 1954 or even from the 1960 deed had run before 1975 (paras 39 to 41).

**No title under an agreement to sell.** Relying on Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, *Rambhau Namdeo Gajre* [(2004) 8 SCC 614] and *Suraj Lamp* [(2012) 1 SCC 656], the Court held that only a registered sale deed conveys title and that a society resolution cannot supply one (paras 44 to 47).

**Bare possession was misconceived.** Applying *Anathula Sudhakar* [(2008) 4 SCC 594], the Court held that where the defendant's registered deed casts a serious cloud on the plaintiff's title, the plaintiff must pray for a declaration (paras 48 to 53).

**Estoppel.** Using the four preconditions of Section 115 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 from *Pratima Chowdhury* [(2014) 4 SCC 196], the Court treated the 1954 letter as the representation, relied on by the Society and Defendant No. 1. It added direct acquiescence under *M.J. James* [(2022) 2 SCC 301], since the letter was never revoked. It declined to decide fraud (paras 54 to 63).

**Section 53A.** The plaintiffs were out of possession and Sohan Singh had abandoned his construction obligation, so neither possession nor readiness and willingness was shown (paras 64 to 67).

The appeal was dismissed without costs (para 70).

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The form of relief

The most instructive passage concerns the prayer (paras 48 to 53). *Anathula Sudhakar* asks whether the defendant's challenge raises a cloud on title, meaning an apparent defect in the plaintiff's title or a *prima facie* right in a third party. A trespasser's bare denial does not; a registered deed from the very vendor does. The Court's reasoning is orthodox and, in my view, correct. The case adds nothing to *Anathula Sudhakar* itself, but shows its test applied to a registered deed.

The logic is practical. Possession follows a decision on ownership. A plaintiff who does not ask the court to decide that question, and to remove the instrument in the way, gives it no decree it can safely pass. The proviso to Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 is the mirror image: a plaintiff cannot sue for a bare declaration when further relief is available, and cannot sue for bare possession when a declaration is needed to clear the way.

The Court added that the plaintiffs could have sued for specific performance (para 53). That sits uneasily with its findings that the agreement had lapsed and been abandoned. It is best read as an illustration of a properly framed suit.

### 3.2 Agreement to sell and the society resolution

The holding that an agreement to sell confers no title restates settled law. Two points deserve emphasis. The Court treated Clause 7 as a condition precedent, and where an agreement says in terms that default forfeits the right to a sale, the promisee's own admission that he cannot comply is powerful evidence that the right has gone. The Court also refused to treat a resolution recognising Sohan Singh's ownership as title (para 46). Housing society records, share certificates and allotment letters evidence a member's rights against the society; they do not replace a registered instrument.

A further point is worth drawing out. The Court's route to dismissal was layered: limitation, absence of title, wrong relief, estoppel and Section 53A each independently sufficed. Alternative holdings of this kind make the decision robust on appeal, but they also invite selective citation. A lawyer relying on the case should identify which strand supports the proposition advanced, because the strands rest on different evidence. The finding on limitation depends on dates; the estoppel finding depends on one letter; the Section 53A finding depends on possession and conduct.

### 3.3 Limitation: a weaker link

The Articles 54 and 59 analysis is straightforward. The treatment of Article 64 is less secure. That Article governs suits "based on previous possession and not on title". The plaintiffs pleaded ownership, and a suit for possession founded on title ordinarily falls under Article 65, which runs twelve years from when the defendant's possession becomes adverse. The judgment does not discuss Article 65.

This probably does not change the outcome. On the Court's findings, Defendant No. 1 took possession in 1954 and any hostile claim dates at latest from the 1960 deed, so twelve years expired in 1972. But the judgment should not be cited as holding that Article 64 governs title-based suits by plaintiffs out of possession; the finding of "no element of dispossession" (para 41) is confined to these facts.

The Court also did not discuss whether time spent before the Registrar could be excluded under Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which concerns bona fide proceedings in a forum without jurisdiction. The Society's suit, which the plaintiffs did not bring, may explain the silence, but that inference is mine; the judgment does not say it.

### 3.4 Estoppel by letter

A written statement to a society, vendor or registry can be used against its maker and his heirs. The Court found each of the four limbs satisfied and noted that the letter was never withdrawn. One observation: the Court treated Defendant No. 1's change of position as both his construction and his registration as owner. The construction at his own cost is the classic detrimental reliance; the deed is the legal result of the Society's decision. It is the construction that does the equitable work, and the finding that the plaintiffs contributed nothing to it supports the point. The Court was right to decline a finding on fraud, since estoppel could be decided on the documents alone.

The acquiescence holding confirms that acquiescence is founded on conduct, knowledge and assent, not lapse of time, so it can operate within the limitation period.

### 3.5 Section 53A

Section 53A is a shield, not a sword. The essentials restated from *Nathulal v. Phoolchand* [(1969) 3 SCC 120], *Shrimant Shamrao Suryavanshi* [(2002) 3 SCC 676] and *Giriyappa v. Kamalamma* [2024 SCC OnLine SC 3849] include continuance in possession and readiness and willingness. A transferee who has surrendered possession and declared himself unable to perform has no equity left to protect. The Court's point that a prayer for possession itself concedes the plaintiff is out of possession (para 66) is neat.

### 3.6 Fairness and the long delay

There is an uncomfortable feature that the Court does not address. The plaintiffs' case was pending for over fifty years, and the appeal alone for fourteen. A reader may ask whether the delay itself, much of it caused by the courts, should have weighed in the equities. The answer in law is that limitation and estoppel run against the claimant regardless of what happened after the suit was filed, and the plaintiffs' difficulty arose in the 1960s, when they did not sue to set aside the deed. The decision is therefore defensible. Yet the case is a reminder that doctrines designed to protect repose operate harshly on heirs who inherit a claim long after the facts have faded.

### 3.7 Open questions

The Court did not decide adverse possession, referred to only as an alternative limitation argument. It did not decide whether the Society's bye-laws allowed transfer to a non-relative, or the agency claim, since it proceeded on the letter and conduct.

## 4. Practical significance

**Conveyancing and due diligence.** A buyer or lender examining title to a plot or flat in a co-operative society should treat an allotment letter, an agreement to sell or a society resolution as evidence of a right to call for a conveyance, not as a muniment of title. The chain should lead to a registered deed. Where the chain depends on an unregistered agreement, the diligence report should identify whether the conditions of that agreement, such as construction deadlines, were performed, and whether the allottee later wrote anything to the society that could be read as relinquishment or nomination of someone else.

**Drafting.** The case shows the force of a forfeiture clause tied to a stated deadline. Societies and developers who wish to preserve the right to reallot should state the consequence of default in terms, as Clause 7 did. Conversely, an allottee who signs a letter "requesting transfer" to another person should understand it may be treated as a binding relinquishment. Drafters of such letters should state whether the request is conditional, for example on payment to the allottee, because the decision shows how unconditional language can be held against heirs half a century later.

**Litigation strategy.** For claimants, the lesson is about the prayer clause and the clock. Where a registered deed stands against the client, the plaint should seek cancellation under Article 59, with specific performance and a declaration of title as alternatives, and should be filed within three years of knowledge. Possession and mesne profits should be consequential reliefs. A plaint that omits the challenge to the deed invites dismissal even if the underlying equities are strong. For defendants who hold a registered deed, the judgment confirms that the cloud-on-title argument is a strong preliminary objection, and that a plea of estoppel based on the claimant's own correspondence can be pleaded alongside it.

**Heirs and long-running claims.** The suit was filed in 1975 and the Supreme Court decided it in 2026. The plaintiffs' stated date of knowledge was 1963. Heirs who inherit a dormant claim should assume that time has been running since their predecessor knew of the adverse transaction, and that a delay that was explained by pursuing a co-operative society's internal dispute resolution may not stop the clock.

**Housing societies and registrars.** Societies should be careful about recording a member as "owner" in a resolution before a conveyance is executed. The Court was unmoved by such a recital, but the resolution still produced thirty years of litigation. Registrars and society managers should also note that a member's written request to transfer a plot is likely to be treated as a representation on which others may rely.

## 5. Conclusion

*Rano Singh* is a faithful application of settled principles to an unusually old dispute. It does not overrule any authority or announce a new test. Its value lies in showing, on a single set of facts, how title, remedy, limitation, estoppel and part performance interlock. A claimant whose predecessor held only an agreement to sell, and who then lets a third party take a registered deed and build on the land, will find each door closed in turn.

The Court's reasoning is strongest on the need for a declaratory prayer where a registered deed raises a cloud on title, and on Section 53A. It is weaker, or at least less fully explained, on the choice between Articles 64 and 65 and on the exclusion of time spent before the Registrar, though neither point appears to change the result. The decision should be cited for the propositions it reaffirms, not for a view that Article 64 governs title-based possession suits.

For the profession, the message is practical. Check the prayer clause before the plaint is filed, read the client's own old letters before asserting a position inconsistent with them, and never assume that an agreement to sell, however fully paid, will carry the client to ownership without a registered conveyance.

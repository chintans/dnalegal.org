# Daughters as Coparceners by Birth: The Continuing Force of *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma*

*A three-judge bench held that a coparcener's daughter takes coparcenary rights by birth, whether or not her father was alive on 9 September 2005.*

*No qualifying recent property law development was found within the search window (7 days to 12 October 2026), so this article addresses a settled principle of continuing importance.*

## Introduction

Few modern decisions have altered the architecture of Hindu family property as sharply as *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma*, decided by a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court of India on 11 August 2020 and reported at (2020) 9 SCC 1. The Court held that a daughter of a coparcener is a coparcener by birth, in the same manner as a son, under Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 as substituted by the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, and that her right does not depend on whether her father was alive on 9 September 2005.

Six years on, the principle sits beneath a steady stream of partition suits, family settlements and title investigations. This article summarises the decision, examines the reasoning on retrospectivity and notional partition, notes the criticism it has drawn, and sets out what it means for conveyancing and litigation.

## Case Summary and Background

The 2005 Amendment rewrote Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 to give effect to gender equality in the Mitakshara coparcenary. Under the substituted provision, a coparcener's daughter becomes a coparcener by birth, in the same manner as a son, with the same rights and liabilities. The difficulty lay in timing. The amendment came into force on 9 September 2005, and many fathers had died before that date. Could a daughter claim coparcenary status in such a family?

Two lines of authority had emerged. *Prakash v. Phulavati*, (2016) 2 SCC 36, read the amendment as prospective, so that the daughter and the coparcener father both had to be alive on the commencement date. *Mangammal v. T.B. Raju*, (2018) 15 SCC 662, followed the same approach. A contrary view in other decisions favoured daughters. The conflict was referred to a bench of three judges. The bench consisted of Arun Mishra, M.R. Shah and S. Abdul Nazeer, JJ., and Arun Mishra, J. wrote the judgment.

The Court answered in favour of daughters. As reported in the secondary sources I consulted, it held that the 2005 Amendment operates retrospectively in the sense that a daughter's claim is not defeated because her father died before 9 September 2005. Coparcenary rights arise by birth, so a predecessor coparcener need not be alive on the amendment's commencement for the daughter to hold them. The Court overruled *Prakash v. Phulavati* and *Mangammal v. T.B. Raju* to the extent they took the contrary view.

A second strand concerned notional partition. Where a male Hindu dies leaving a female heir in Class I of the Schedule, the proviso to the original Section 6 fixed his share by assuming that a partition had taken place immediately before his death. The question was whether that assumption merely quantifies the deceased's share or actually ends the joint family. As reported, the Court adopted the narrower reading. Notional partition is a legal fiction used to determine the deceased's share and does not effect a real partition, so the joint family otherwise continues. This follows the approach of *Gurupad Khandappa v. Hirabai Khandappa Magdum*, (1978) 3 SCC 383, and *Uttam v. Saubhag Singh*, (2016) 4 SCC 68.

One further point of statutory text matters. The substituted Section 6 protects dispositions and partitions effected before 20 December 2004, so the daughter's right does not unsettle genuine earlier transactions. Whether a purported earlier partition counts is therefore the usual battleground. I did not retrieve any source describing what the Court said about proof of such a partition, including oral partitions, and I make no claim on that point. Readers should consult the judgment itself on it.

## Legal Analysis

**Retrospectivity.** The strength of the decision lies in its focus on the nature of the right. If coparcenary status arises by birth, the relevant question is whether the daughter was born into the family, not when the father died. On that footing, applying the amendment to a daughter whose father died earlier does not give the statute retroactive force in any objectionable sense. It recognises a status that the statute now attaches to birth, while the savings in the proviso protect transactions that were completed before the cut-off date. This reading avoids the arbitrary result under *Prakash*, where two sisters in identical families would take different rights depending on the date of their father's death.

The approach also fits the purpose of the 2005 Amendment. A reform designed to remove discrimination would be hollowed out if it benefited only those daughters whose fathers happened to survive until September 2005. I regard the reasoning as sound and the overruling of *Prakash* and *Mangammal* as justified.

**Notional partition.** Here the position is less comfortable. The narrow reading preserves the joint family and the survivorship that characterises coparcenary. That is doctrinally coherent. Its cost falls on certain women. The commentary I read argues that a female heir who inherits an interest in joint family property through notional partition may be left without partition rights if she dies before an actual partition. It also argues that the broader reading, which treats a real partition as having occurred immediately before death, would have protected daughters-in-law without depending on a coparcener seeking partition. That criticism has force. The narrow reading answers the question of status for daughters, but it leaves the position of other female heirs less secure. The Court's choice favours the continuity of the coparcenary over the individual certainty of every heir, and reasonable lawyers can differ on that trade-off.

**A remark and its critics.** The reasoning contains the observation, reported at para 50, that "Once a daughter, always a daughter … son is a son till he is married." The authors of the SCC Online commentary describe this as "romantic paternalism". Critics note that married women may belong to both natal and marital lineages and that this framing sits awkwardly with that reality. They also point to the Law Commission's 174th Report and its 2018 consultation paper, which recommended abolishing the coparcenary altogether. On this view the decision extends an institution that some reformers would prefer to retire.

**Unresolved questions.** Three questions remain open on what I have verified. First, the interaction between the daughter's coparcenary share and the share of women who enter the family by marriage. Second, how far courts will scrutinise pre-2004 partitions and dispositions pleaded to defeat a daughter's claim. Third, whether Parliament will revisit the institution of coparcenary itself.

## Practical Significance

For conveyancing and due diligence, the decision means that a title chain passing through a Hindu undivided family cannot safely ignore daughters. A purchaser of ancestral property from the male members alone takes the risk that a daughter, or her heirs, hold an undivided share that no one conveyed. The risk is real even where the father died decades ago. Due diligence should establish who the coparceners were, whether any genuine partition or disposition predates 20 December 2004, and whether every daughter has joined in the transaction or released her interest by a properly executed and registered instrument.

For drafting, releases and family settlements should name each daughter and recite the basis of her entitlement. A general recital that the property was "partitioned long ago" is weak protection.

For litigation, the decision shifts the burden of effort onto defendants who rely on an earlier partition to defeat a daughter's claim. They must plead and prove that partition with care. Plaintiff daughters, for their part, should plead the family's joint status, their birth into it and the property's ancestral character. Courts and registrars dealing with mutation applications also need to treat daughters as co-sharers when ancestral property is involved.

For lenders and developers, the point is commercial. A mortgage or joint development agreement built on an incomplete family title may be vulnerable to a later claim by an omitted daughter.

## Conclusion

*Vineeta Sharma* settled a conflict in the authorities and gave daughters equal coparcenary status regardless of the date of their father's death. Its core reasoning, that coparcenary rights arise by birth, is persuasive and consistent with the purpose of the 2005 Amendment. Its treatment of notional partition is coherent but leaves some female heirs with weaker protection, and the critics' concerns deserve attention from the legislature.

For practitioners the lesson is practical. Ancestral property titles must be examined with daughters in view, earlier partitions must be proved rather than assumed, and releases must be documented properly. The decision will continue to shape property disputes within Hindu families, and anyone advising on such property should begin the title inquiry with it.

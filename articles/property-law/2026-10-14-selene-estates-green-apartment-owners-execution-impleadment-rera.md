# Execution Cannot Outrun the Order: Tamil Nadu REAT on Impleading Strangers and Directors in RERA Execution

*A homebuyers' association may add a promoter's directors to execution of a RERA order, but it may not add unrelated companies that were never parties to the complaint.*

## 1. Introduction

Winning a regulatory order against a defaulting promoter is only half the battle for a homebuyers' association. The harder half is making the order bite. When the promoter fails to complete a project, associations often discover during execution that the people who actually control the project's assets, land or maintenance are not the entity named in the order. The temptation is then to widen the execution petition to reach them.

On 7 October 2026, the Tamil Nadu Real Estate Appellate Tribunal ("the Tribunal") drew a line through that temptation. In a short common order in Appeal Nos. 88 and 89 of 2026, a bench of Justice M. Duraiswamy (Chairperson) and K. Babu (Judicial Member) held that three companies who were not parties to the original complaint could not be impleaded in execution, but that three directors of the promoter company could be. The order is brief, cites no precedent, and does not engage with any provision of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 ("RERA") beyond the appellate jurisdiction conferred by Section 44. Its value lies in the question it answers and the tension it leaves behind.

## 2. Case summary

### 2.1 Background

The dispute concerns a residential project at Jalladianpettai. The complainant, Green Apartment Owners Welfare Association ("the Association"), filed Complaint No. 29 of 2023 before the Tamil Nadu Real Estate Regulatory Authority ("TNRERA"). The respondents to that complaint were the promoter, M/s Selene Estates Limited ("Selene"), and two other entities, M/s India Bulls Real Estates Ltd. and M/s Ozone Projects Private Limited. The order records that Selene was described in the cause title as represented by its Director, but that no individual director was named.

On 13 September 2023, TNRERA issued directions that, as recorded in the Tribunal's order (paragraph 5), required the promoter to complete pending works and common amenities by 31 December 2023; obtain a Completion Certificate and permanent electricity connections by 31 March 2024; ensure sewerage connections and environmental compliance; and hand over statutory documents, original title deeds and any corpus fund to the Association by 31 March 2024. The Tribunal notes that these directions were issued only against the original respondents.

Selene did not comply. The Association filed Execution Petition No. 15 of 2024. While that petition was pending, and indeed while it stood reserved for orders, the Association filed two applications. Execution Application No. 20 of 2024 sought to implead three companies: M/s Lakisha Real Estate Limited, M/s Airmid Infrastructure Limited and M/s Ozone Facility Management Private Limited. Execution Application No. 5 of 2025 sought to implead three individuals described as directors of Selene: Vasudevan Sathyamoorthy, Vaithiyalingam Mahalingam and Singaram.

By a common order dated 17 June 2026, TNRERA allowed both applications. Selene appealed under Section 44 of RERA. Appeal No. 88 of 2026 challenged the impleadment of the three companies; Appeal No. 89 of 2026 challenged the impleadment of the three directors. By consent of counsel, the Tribunal disposed of both by one order, as the issues were common, the parties the same and the project one (paragraph 4).

### 2.2 The directors

The Tribunal began with the directors, and dealt with them quickly. It observed that a company must be represented by a human representative, and that although the cause title in the original complaint described Selene as represented by a Director, it never named that Director. "In such circumstances," the Tribunal said, "we find justification in impleading the Directors of the promoter Company," and it held that TNRERA had rightly allowed Execution Application No. 5 of 2025 (paragraph 6).

Selene's senior counsel, Mr S. Silambanan, submitted that the ninth respondent, Singaram, had resigned and was no longer a director. The Tribunal recorded that no document had been produced to establish this. It left it open to Selene to file the necessary documents before TNRERA in the execution petition, which was to decide the point in accordance with law (paragraph 7).

### 2.3 The companies

The Tribunal reached the opposite result for the three companies. It reasoned that the Association "had every opportunity to implead all the necessary parties" but "chose not to implead them at the earliest point of time in the original proceedings" (paragraph 8). It then stated the governing principle in paragraph 9: the executing authority cannot go beyond the order passed by the original authority; an order passed against the appellant and respondents 2 and 3 "is not binding on the other respondents who were not parties in the original complaint"; and a decree holder "cannot be allowed to implead new parties in piecemeal in the Execution Proceedings." TNRERA, having failed to consider these aspects, had erroneously allowed Execution Application No. 20 of 2024 (paragraph 10).

### 2.4 Disposition

In paragraph 11 the Tribunal set aside the order on Execution Application No. 20 of 2024 and allowed Appeal No. 88 of 2026. It confirmed the order on Execution Application No. 5 of 2025 and dismissed Appeal No. 89 of 2026. The connected miscellaneous applications were closed. The order does not state what relief the Association sought against the three companies, how they were connected with the project or with Selene, or what directions would follow against the directors. I have not assumed any relationship between the companies and Selene.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory frame, and what the order leaves out

The order's only statutory citation is Section 44 of RERA, the appeal provision. The enforcement scheme sits elsewhere. As reproduced in secondary sources I could access only through search summaries, Section 40(1) of RERA makes interest, penalty or compensation imposed by the adjudicating officer, the Regulatory Authority or the Appellate Tribunal recoverable as arrears of land revenue in the prescribed manner, and Section 40(2) provides that any other order or direction is to be enforced "in such manner as may be prescribed". Execution of non-monetary directions of the kind at issue here, such as completing works and handing over documents, therefore depends on the State's rules and the Authority's regulations rather than directly on the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. I was unable to retrieve the statute or the Tamil Nadu rules, so this paragraph should be read as context rather than as a statement of the Tribunal's reasoning. The Tribunal did not discuss these provisions at all.

That omission matters. The Tribunal's central proposition, that an executing authority cannot go beyond the order it executes, is borrowed from the law of civil execution, where it is usually traced to the idea that an executing court cannot go behind the decree. The order says the proposition is "settled law" but cites no authority for it, and does not explain why it applies with equal force to a statutory regulator exercising prescribed enforcement powers. A reader of the order cannot tell whether the Tribunal regarded the civil-law principle as binding on RERA authorities, or merely as a sound analogy. The bench would have strengthened the decision, and made it more useful to later benches, by saying which.

### 3.2 The companies: a sound result on a thin foundation

The result on the three companies is, in my view, correct. A direction can bind only a person against whom it was made, and a person can be made subject to a direction only after being heard. The order's reasoning tracks that logic: the original directions were issued only against the original respondents, so execution can only be had against them.

The strongest part of the reasoning is timing. The Association could have joined these companies in Complaint No. 29 of 2023 and did not. Execution is not a vehicle for correcting the original pleadings. Adding parties after the order, and after the execution petition was reserved, would deprive them of the chance to contest liability on the merits.

The weaknesses are two. First, the order is silent on whether any recognised exception might have applied, such as a person who is a mere alter ego of the judgment-debtor or a transferee with notice. Its reasoning is categorical: non-parties are not bound. That may be right, but it is stated rather than demonstrated, and the order cites no case law on the point. I have not researched that case law here.

Second, "in piecemeal" suggests a procedural-fairness concern about the sequential widening of the petition (the first application was filed in 2024, the second in 2025). That sits uneasily with the Tribunal's decision to allow the later application, which brings us to the real difficulty.

### 3.3 The directors: the tension with paragraph 9

If a decree holder cannot implead new parties in execution because the original order did not bind them, how can the directors be impleaded? They, too, were not named in the complaint. They, too, were not the subject of the directions of 13 September 2023. The Tribunal's answer is that a company must act through a representative and the cause title mentioned a Director without naming one. On this view, impleading the directors does not add new parties; it names the individuals through whom the existing party, Selene, acts.

That reconciliation is plausible but incomplete, for three reasons.

First, it conflates two things: representing a company in proceedings, and being personally answerable for its default. A director who is named in a cause title as the person through whom a company appears does not thereby assume personal liability for the company's obligations. Whether the three directors have been impleaded in a purely representative capacity, so that execution remains execution against Selene, or in a personal capacity, so that coercive processes could reach them individually, is the crucial question, and the order does not answer it. Counsel for associations will read the order as authority for personal exposure; counsel for directors will read it as a ruling on representation only. Both readings are open on the text.

Second, the reasoning could apply to every corporate promoter, since every company acts through a human being. The Tribunal limited itself to "such circumstances", namely a cause title that referred to a Director without naming one. A narrow reading confines the holding to that pleading feature; a broad reading permits joinder of directors in any execution against a corporate promoter. The order does not say which is intended.

Third, the order's treatment of Singaram shows how little the Tribunal examined the directors' status. The appellant said he had resigned; the Tribunal said no document had been produced and left the matter open before TNRERA. Resignation of a director is a matter of company records, which the Companies Act, 2013 requires to be filed with the Registrar of Companies (I did not retrieve the provision). The Tribunal confirmed the impleadment of all three individuals first and invited proof of resignation afterwards, placing the burden of establishing non-directorship on the person impleaded.

### 3.4 Distinguishing directors from companies

A coherent principle would justify both outcomes. On the Tribunal's account the directors are the human face of a party already bound, whereas the companies are separate juristic persons. Joining a representative adds no new rights-holder; joining a company does. Read that way, paragraphs 6 and 9 do not conflict. But the Tribunal did not articulate the distinction, and personal liability of promoters' directors under RERA is a sensitive question. A BCAJ article, which I could access only through a search summary, describes Section 69 of RERA as dealing with the liability of promoters, directors, partners and officers of realty companies and firms for offences, and notes that its wording mirrors Section 42 of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. That provision concerns offences, not civil execution, and the order does not refer to it. The legislature imposed personal liability on persons in charge of a company in a specific provision with its own safeguards; treating impleadment in execution as opening the door to personal liability for civil directions would bypass them. That is a reason to read the holding narrowly.

## 4. Practical significance

### 4.1 For homebuyers' associations

The lesson is to plead completely at the outset. If the project involves a landowner, a joint-development partner, a maintenance company or an affiliate holding the title deeds or corpus fund, that entity should be a respondent to the complaint. The 13 September 2023 directions included handing over original title deeds and a corpus fund; if those are in the hands of someone other than the named promoter, a direction against the promoter alone may be impossible to perform. Associations should therefore establish, before filing, who holds the documents, the money and the statutory approvals, and should name every such person. They should also name the promoter's directors or authorised signatories, or at least ensure that the person through whom the promoter appears is identified in the cause title.

Associations that already hold orders against a single entity should assess whether a fresh complaint against the omitted parties is available. Whether limitation or a bar on repeated complaints would defeat it is not addressed by this order.

### 4.2 For promoters and their affiliates

Group companies connected to a defaulting promoter gain real protection: their entitlement to be heard before being bound will not be eroded by an execution application alone. But the protection is procedural, and nothing in the order prevents a fresh, properly constituted proceeding against them.

### 4.3 For directors

The more troubling message is for directors of promoter companies. Until a later bench clarifies the capacity in which directors are impleaded, any director of a defaulting promoter should assume that an execution application may name them, and should respond with documents rather than assertions. The treatment of Singaram is instructive. A director who has resigned should be able to produce the filed resignation and the relevant statutory filings immediately. Directors who have left a company should check that the company's public records reflect their departure. Directors named in an execution petition should also ask the Authority, at the first hearing, to record the capacity in which they are impleaded and the nature of the coercive process, if any, that is sought against them.

### 4.4 For counsel and the Authorities

Two drafting points follow. First, cause titles matter. A complaint that describes a company as represented by "its Director" without naming the director invites exactly the application that succeeded here. Respondent companies and their counsel should consider whether the omission is worth exploiting or worth curing. Second, authorities passing execution-stage orders adding parties should record their reasons for treating the proposed parties as bound by the original order. TNRERA's order of 17 June 2026 was set aside in part because it did not consider whether the order bound the proposed companies; a reasoned order would have been easier to defend or to correct.

## 5. Conclusion

The Tribunal's order is a small piece of procedural law with large practical reach. Its core holding, that an executing authority cannot reach persons who were not parties to the original order, is orthodox and, in my view, right. A homebuyers' association that wants relief against a company must sue the company. That discipline protects absent parties and keeps execution proceedings tied to the original adjudication.

Its second holding, that the directors of the promoter could be added, is the more difficult. It may rest on a narrow ground, that the cause title already referred to a Director and the Association was merely naming the representative, or it may rest on a broader proposition that directors of a defaulting promoter are always proper parties to execution. The order does not choose between them, and it does not say what consequences follow for the directors personally. Until a larger bench or the High Court addresses those questions, directors and associations will each read the order to suit their positions.

Two matters call for further judicial attention: the source of the principle that execution cannot go beyond the original order in the RERA scheme, as against the statutory enforcement provisions, and the capacity in which directors are impleaded. A short order with no citations has left both open. For now, the safest course for associations is to plead completely at the outset, and the safest course for directors is to keep their records in order.

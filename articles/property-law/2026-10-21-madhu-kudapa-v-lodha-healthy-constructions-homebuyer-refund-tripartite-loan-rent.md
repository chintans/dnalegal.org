# Whose Money Is Refunded? Tripartite Home Loans, Rent Claims and Unjust Enrichment in *Madhu Kudapa v. Lodha Healthy Constructions*

*When a builder fails to deliver and the buyer takes a refund, the Supreme Court has held that the refund follows the buyer's own money and not the lender's, and that interest on that money, not rent on top of it, is the measure of the delay.*

## 1. Introduction

Most apartments in India are bought with borrowed money. The lender pays the builder directly, in tranches, under a tripartite agreement among builder, buyer and bank, and the buyer starts servicing interest long before possession. When the project stalls, a question that conveyancing practice has rarely had to face becomes unavoidable: if the buyer is entitled to a refund, to whom does the builder return the money that came from the bank?

On 15 October 2026, a Bench of Justice Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe answered it in *Madhu Kudapa (formerly K. Madhusudhan Rao) v. M/s Lodha Healthy Constructions and Developer Private Limited (LHCPL) & Anr.*, 2026 INSC 1131 (Civil Appeal No. 8994 of 2022, with Civil Appeal Nos. 4622-4623 of 2023), in a judgment authored by Aradhe J. The Court held that the buyer is entitled, in his own right, only to the sums he actually paid, with simple interest at the contractual 12 per cent. The balance, which came from the lender, stays in an interest-bearing deposit pending the bank's recovery appeal. The Court also refused years of claimed rent as double compensation and substituted a lump sum of Rs. 15 lakh.

The judgment is dated 15 October 2026 and so falls outside the 48-hour window; because no uncovered property-law judgment within that window was found, the search was widened to seven days. Section 2 sets out the facts and holding; Section 3 analyses unjust enrichment, contractual interest, the rent claim and the bank's deficiency in service; Section 4 draws out the practical consequences; Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Case summary and background

### Facts

In 2009 the builder launched "Lodha Bellezza" at Kukatpally, Hyderabad. On 8 August 2009 the appellant, then a non-resident Indian, booked an apartment for Rs. 2,30,73,876 and paid Rs. 51,11,576 (about 22 per cent) from his own funds; an Agreement for Sale followed on 21 October 2009. The Housing Development Finance Corporation Limited (the Bank) sanctioned a Rs. 2 crore loan, and a Tripartite Agreement dated 30 January 2010 and a Loan Agreement dated 1 February 2010 (with the buyer's wife as co-borrower) were executed. Between February 2010 and July 2011 the Bank released Rs. 1,68,80,531 to the builder in thirteen tranches, so the builder held about 95 per cent of the price.

Clause 24 of the Agreement for Sale promised possession by 30 June 2011, with a six-month grace period. It was not delivered. The builder later offered possession for "fit-outs" while the Fire Department's no-objection certificate was still awaited, and the Bank meanwhile debited pre-EMI interest, of which the buyer paid Rs. 26,23,150.

### Procedural history

On 13 May 2013 the buyer filed a consumer complaint before the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) seeking refund of Rs. 2,19,92,107 with interest, rent of Rs. 7,97,895 for July 2011 to April 2013 and later rent, and Rs. 15 lakh for mental agony. The Bank recalled the loan in January 2015 and sued for recovery before a Commercial Court; the buyer paid it a further Rs. 50 lakh in 2021, taking his payments to the Bank to Rs. 76,23,150.

By its order of 1 July 2022 the NCDRC rejected the builder's force majeure plea, directed refund of the entire amount with 9 per cent interest (12 per cent on default), and found the Bank deficient in service for disbursing without verifying construction progress, awarding Rs. 2 lakh against it. It did not address rent or compensation. The Bank's review was dismissed on 7 December 2022. The builder did not appeal and deposited Rs. 4,43,24,510 with the Commission. The Commercial Court dismissed the Bank's suit on maintainability alone in December 2024; the Bank's appeal is pending before the High Court.

### Issues and holding

The Court asked whether the buyer could recover the whole amount including the Bank's disbursements, and at what interest; whether he could recover rent and compensation; whether the Bank was deficient; and what relief the Bank could have in these proceedings.

It held the buyer entitled in his own right to Rs. 1,27,34,726 (his Rs. 51,11,576 to the builder plus Rs. 76,23,150 to the Bank) with simple interest at 12 per cent from each payment. Paying the entire sum to him while the loan was outstanding would "place in his hands a sum which he has not paid", which the Court termed unjust enrichment. It refused the enlarged rent claim and awarded Rs. 15 lakh in full and final satisfaction of rent, mental agony, harassment and litigation expenses. It upheld the Bank's deficiency and the Rs. 2 lakh award, held that the Bank's contractual claim cannot be decided in a consumer complaint, and directed the remaining deposit to be held in an interest-bearing account abiding the High Court's decision.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Unjust enrichment as the organising principle

The Court examined the refund "on the touchstone" of the principle that no one should be enriched at another's expense (para 22). It located the doctrine in Sections 64, 65, 70 and 72 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and in equity, citing *Mahabir Kishore v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, (1989) 4 SCC 1, *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India*, (2011) 8 SCC 161 and the nine-Judge decision in *Mafatlal Industries Ltd. v. Union of India*, (1997) 5 SCC 536. Restitution, it said, is "a doctrine of correction and not of reward": it returns value to the person at whose expense it was received (para 25).

The analytical step is the description of the transaction as "triangular" (para 27). The builder was plainly enriched by keeping about Rs. 2.2 crore for no return, and conceded liability. But the money had two sources, and the claim belongs to whoever bore the loss. The buyer lost what he paid; the Bank lost what it advanced, and the buyer remained liable to repay it.

The Court read the tripartite deeming clause (Recital (H) and Clause 4), under which disbursal to the builder is disbursal to the buyer, narrowly. It "fastens the obligation to repay the loan on the Borrower" but "does not convert borrowed money, which remains unpaid, into the Borrower's own money" (para 28). That is a sound reading: the fiction gives the lender a clean claim against its borrower and was never meant to decide who may claim restitution from the builder. The contrary view would let a buyer who contributed 22 per cent recover the whole.

The solution is not free of difficulty. The NCDRC's direction had the practical merit of placing the whole fund with the person who owes the Bank, leaving the loan account to be settled between them. The Supreme Court's approach splits the fund: the buyer takes his own money now, and the rest sits in deposit. If the buyer's liability to the Bank is later found smaller than claimed, for example because of the Bank's own deficiency, apportionment is not obvious. The Court left "all contentions", including the buyer's contention about his loan liability, to the High Court (para 45). The judgment resolves who may withdraw now, not who ultimately owns the balance.

Note also what the holding does not say. It does not hold that the lender is entitled to the builder's refund; it holds only that the buyer may not take what he has not paid. Whether the Bank, which argued that cancellation reduced it from secured to unsecured creditor under Clauses 16 and 17 of the Tripartite Agreement, has any direct claim on the fund was not decided.

### 3.2 Interest: the contract controls

The buyer claimed 18 per cent under Clause 19 of the Agreement for Sale and *Rajnesh Sharma v. Business Park Town Planners Ltd.*, 2025 SCC OnLine SC 2061. The Court held that Clause 24 specifically governs the builder's default, providing 12 per cent compensation if the buyer stays or 12 per cent simple interest on refund if he terminates; Clause 19 "does not stand alone" (para 30). *Rajnesh Sharma* was distinguished: it involved a builder who kept the buyer waiting over a decade while charging him 18 per cent for his own delays, and the Court there said the builder's rate is not a rule of thumb. Where parties have agreed the consequence of delay, "strong reasons" are needed to depart from it, citing *DLF Homes Panchkula Pvt. Ltd. v. D.S. Dhanda*, (2020) 16 SCC 318 and *Greater Mohali Area Development Authority v. Anupam Garg*, 2025 SCC OnLine SC 1312.

The NCDRC's 9 per cent therefore becomes 12 per cent, but because the contract says so. This usefully checks any reading of recent cases as creating a de facto 18 per cent default. Yet *Rajnesh Sharma* was distinguished rather than doubted, so a buyer with an unusually low or absent rate clause and a builder whose conduct is egregious keeps the argument for more.

### 3.3 The rent claim

The buyer's rent claim had grown from Rs. 7,97,895 before the NCDRC to Rs. 38,000 a month (to July 2013) and Rs. 65,000 a month (August 2013 to October 2022), raised in a rejoinder before the Supreme Court. The Court gave five reasons for refusing it (paras 36 to 40):

1. The Consumer Protection Act, 1986 has no provision like those in Rent Control legislation giving a tenant a right to rent.
2. Rent in addition to interest on the full refund is double compensation for one default; *D.S. Dhanda* holds that an award "under various heads in respect of the same default is not sustainable".
3. The claim exceeded the pleadings and proof: no lease or rent receipt was before the NCDRC, only a 2021 bank statement.
4. The buyer elected his remedy. Clause 24 offered a choice between staying with compensation and terminating with refund; by seeking refund on 13 May 2013 he chose the latter, after which housing costs were "not a loss caused by the non-delivery of an apartment which he no longer wished to have".
5. The claim was inconsistent with his own letter of 3 November 2012, which valued his loss as rent he could have earned from the flat (about Rs. 1,50,000 a month); one flat cannot produce both rent received and rent paid. Section 73 of the Contract Act, with its Explanation on the means of remedying inconvenience, points the same way.

Reasons 2 and 4 are the doctrinally important ones, and they sit uneasily together. The double-compensation point treats interest as covering the whole consequence of delay; the election point speaks only of loss after termination. A buyer may fairly argue that for 2011 to 2013, before any election, rent paid was a real loss, and that interest on Rs. 1.27 crore does not obviously cover it. *D.S. Dhanda* answers that interest takes into its ambit the consequences of delay in handing over possession, which is persuasive where the refund covers the full price but less so here, where the refund to the buyer was confined to his own contribution while the larger, loan-funded portion was held back.

The Rs. 15 lakh award shows the Court sensed that gap. It noted that the buyer was kept out of both flat and money for over fifteen years and lost the benefit of price escalation, so that compensation "has to be higher than the return of the money deposited" (para 41). The figure is round, is equal to the sum he originally claimed for mental agony alone, and is explained by a wish "to avoid prolonged inquiry, at this distance of time". The rent head is thus rejected for doctrinal tidiness while the underlying hardship is compensated under a general label.

### 3.4 The Bank's deficiency in service

The Court upheld deficiency (paras 42 to 44) through ordinary burden-shifting. The buyer discharged his initial burden: he asked the Bank repeatedly to verify progress, objected in May 2011 that progress did not justify further payment, yet the last tranche was released in July 2011; the Bank itself reported the building only 90 per cent ready in October 2012. The burden then moved to the Bank, since verification was a fact within its knowledge. It produced no inspection report and led no evidence, and its witness in the suit said such reports were not required. That the tranches matched the stage-wise instalments in Clause 6 showed only that the Bank paid what was demanded. The finding was one of fact, neither perverse nor unsupported, and the Rs. 2 lakh award was "modest".

### 3.5 Unresolved questions

The judgment does not mention the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016; the booking dates from 2009 and the case was decided under the 1986 Act. Whether the apportionment principle will carry into refund proceedings before RERA authorities is not addressed, and any suggestion that it does would be speculation. The ultimate division of the deposit is also for the High Court.

## 4. Practical significance

**Lenders.** Expect borrowers in refund proceedings to plead disbursement practice as deficiency and expect the burden to shift quickly. Keep contemporaneous engineer reports and disbursal approvals; the Court gave no weight to unsupported assertions that inspections occurred. Because the deeming clause is only a repayment fiction, a lender with no express assignment or escrow of refund proceeds has no automatic claim on the builder's refund. Tripartite agreements should provide for refunds to be paid to the lender up to the outstanding loan.

**Developers.** Exposure is limited to the contractual interest and a modest lump sum; rent will not be stacked on top. But the principal liability is untouched. A clear remedy clause, offering an election between waiting with compensation and exiting with refund and interest, will be enforced as the measure of loss, and its rate will not easily be displaced.

**Buyers.** First, plead and prove every head of loss before the consumer forum; a rent claim first raised in a rejoinder in the Supreme Court fails. Secondly, treat the election between staying and claiming refund as consequential, since post-election housing costs will be treated as ordinary living costs. Thirdly, do not assume borrowed money passes through: a buyer who has paid little himself and carries a large loan may receive only his own contributions, the rest awaiting the lender's claim.

**Litigation strategy.** Buyers should join the lender and press deficiency in service, while recognising that the lender's recovery claim is outside the consumer forum and proceeds separately. Lenders should resist dismissal of a recovery suit as non-maintainable merely because a consumer complaint has been decided, which is what happened before the Commercial Court here.

**Conveyancing and drafting.** An agreement for sale should state in one clause the consequence of delay, the rate of interest, and whether it is in lieu of or in addition to other compensation. A tripartite agreement should state how a refund is applied as between lender and borrower, and should say so expressly if a refund is meant first to discharge the loan.

## 5. Conclusion

*Madhu Kudapa* is a modest step in doctrine but a useful one in practice. Its central proposition, that a buyer's restitutionary claim extends only to what he has actually paid, is persuasive and well anchored, and its reading of the deeming clause in a tripartite agreement as allocating repayment liability and nothing more is likely to be cited in later disputes. Its treatment of interest confirms that the contractual rate governs absent strong reasons and checks any drift towards an 18 per cent default.

The rent holding is the weaker part. Its five reasons are individually sound but not wholly consistent, and the Rs. 15 lakh lump sum reintroduces, under a general label, compensation for the hardship the rent claim sought to remedy. It is best read as confined to a buyer who elected a refund, received contractual interest and advanced an unproved, escalated rent claim; it does not close the door on proved rent for a pre-termination period.

What remains open is as important as what is decided: how the deposited balance will be divided once the High Court rules, and whether the principle will travel into RERA refund proceedings. Until then, lenders, developers and buyers would do well to document disbursement verification, refund waterfalls and remedy elections with the care this decision shows courts now expect.

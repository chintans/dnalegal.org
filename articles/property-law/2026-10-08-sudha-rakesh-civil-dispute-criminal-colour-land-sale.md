# Cheating Needs Deception at the Moment Money Moves: Sudha Rakesh and the Limits of Criminal Law in Land-Sale Disputes

*The Supreme Court has quashed a cheating prosecution arising from a failed agreement to sell agricultural land, holding that the offence requires deception when the buyer parts with money, and that an attorney who confirmed the later sale cannot be prosecuted when the landowners themselves were discharged.*

## 1. Introduction

On 5 October 2026, a bench of Justice Satish Chandra Sharma and Justice Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh delivered judgment in *Sudha Rakesh v. State of Karnataka & Anr.* (2026 INSC 1086), marked reportable. The Court set aside a Karnataka High Court order that had refused to quash a First Information Report ("FIR") against a general power of attorney holder, and quashed the FIR with all consequential proceedings. The underlying quarrel is familiar to every land-law practitioner. A buyer signs an agreement to sell, pays part of the price, does not complete within the stipulated period, and then finds the land sold to someone else. The buyer sues for specific performance and, in the same breath, sets the police in motion.

The Court's first sentence names its concern: litigants' "tendency to colourize civil disputes into criminal offences", which it called "an open secret". The remark alone would not justify an article. What does is the method. The judgment asks when the buyer's advance was delivered and whether deception existed at that moment, and it then tests the attorney's liability against that of the principals. Both inquiries bear on conveyancing, on drafting agreements to sell and powers of attorney, and on the tactics of buyers who see a criminal complaint as a shortcut to their land.

No reasoned property-law judgment dated within the last 48 hours was found, so the window has been widened to seven days. Section 2 sets out the facts and holding, Section 3 analyses the reasoning, Section 4 draws out practical consequences, and Section 5 concludes. The article rests on the full text of the judgment, which the author read.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 Facts and proceedings

One Ganesh owned survey no. 115, measuring 4 acres 19 guntas, at Channasandra Village, Karnataka. He died intestate, and his wife and other legal heirs (the "landowners") inherited it. On 5 October 2012 the landowners agreed to sell the land to the petitioner, Sudha Rakesh, and executed a General Power of Attorney ("GPA") in her favour. Acting through her, they signed a second agreement on 19 November 2012 with the second respondent and one Kalpana Sure (the "prospective buyers"), who were to pay the balance and complete the sale deed within seven months. On the judgment's account they never came forward. On 11 February 2016 the landowners sold the land to a third party, Gaffar Baig, with the petitioner as confirming party.

The prospective buyers promptly sued for specific performance and, on 13 May 2016, lodged FIR No. 121/2016 at Kadugodi Police Station under Sections 420, 423 and 120-B read with Section 34 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 ("IPC"): cheating, a dishonest statement of consideration in a transfer deed, criminal conspiracy and common intention. The Magistrate took cognizance on 28 September 2017. The High Court quashed the FIR against the landowners on 22 November 2017, calling the matter "purely a civil transaction" given "a criminal colour". The petitioner's own petition went before a different bench and was dismissed on 19 September 2022. That bench distinguished her on five grounds: she received the money, alone signed the 2012 agreement as attorney, was a confirming party to the sale deed, the land fetched a lower price, and it was a triable question whether she passed the money to the landowners.

### 2.2 The parties' submissions

The petitioner argued that she was similarly placed with the landowners, that the dispute was purely civil, and that as GPA holder she was merely a confirming party to a sale deed the landowners executed. The second respondent answered that she was a "principal actor" who herself received the amounts, and that the 2016 sale deed was executed by concealing the pre-existing 2012 agreement. The State filed objections on similar lines, which the judgment does not reproduce.

### 2.3 The holding

The Supreme Court held that the High Court had "fallen into error, and a grave one". The FIR rests on the sale deed, which the landowners themselves executed; if no criminality attached to them, none attached to a confirming party. The money was received collectively, and nothing alleged that she exceeded her authority or kept the landowners in the dark. The lower price was "speculative" and would have incriminated the landowners equally. Whether she remitted the money was outside the allegations, since the landowners had made no such complaint.

On the offences, cheating requires "a deceptive act which leads to inducement for delivery of property/money". The buyers paid in 2012, when the land "could have legitimately been transferred" to them, so there was "no deception"; the sale came almost four years later. Section 423 IPC was "mis-invoked", as no instrument contained a false statement of consideration. Invoking "judicial consistency and propriety", the Court said her culpability "could not have been stretched beyond that of the landowners". It expressly refrained from commenting on the specific performance suit or on readiness and willingness.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The timing test for deception

The judgment runs to twenty paragraphs and cites no precedent. Its authority lies in applying settled principles to a recurring fact pattern, so its reach must be read from its logic. That logic has three layers.

The first, and the most significant for property lawyers, is a timing test. Cheating requires a deception and a resulting inducement to deliver property. The Court fixes the relevant moment as the moment the complainant parted with the advance. On the complainant's own case the landowners then had a subsisting agreement and could have conveyed. A later change of mind, a sale to a third party, or even a breach of contract does not retroactively supply the deception that induced the earlier payment.

This applies the familiar distinction between dishonest intention at the inception of a transaction and mere non-performance afterwards. The Court does not use those words and cites no authority for them, so the proposition should be read as the judgment's own formulation. It does not immunise every land-sale dispute. A seller who has no title, or who has already sold to another, and takes an advance on a fresh agreement, has deceived at the moment of delivery. The decision turns on the admitted fact that the land was available when the money was paid.

The Court also treats the buyers' own default as part of the factual matrix. Paragraph 11 records as "the admitted position" that the agreement was not acted upon within seven months or before the 2016 sale. Relying on undisputed dates keeps the reasoning on the right side of the rule against weighing evidence at the quashing stage.

### 3.2 A limitation point left open

The facts recite that the petitioner and landowners waited "till the expiry of the limitation period for seeking specific performance", yet paragraph 11 "consciously refrain[s]" from any view on the suit. The recital should be read as the case as presented, not as a finding.

The arithmetic suggests why. Secondary sources report that Article 54 of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963 allows three years for a specific performance suit, running from the date fixed for performance or, if none is fixed, from notice of refusal. Seven months from 19 November 2012 ended in June 2013, and three years from then runs to about June 2016. The sale deed of 11 February 2016 and the suit that followed it both appear to fall before that date. That calculation is the author's inference, not the Court's. The suit's fate will depend on reserved questions: continuous readiness and willingness under Section 16(c) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, whether time was of the essence, and whether the ultimate buyer is a bona fide purchaser without notice.

### 3.3 The parity principle and the confirming party

The second layer concerns the attorney. A GPA is routinely used to sign agreements and receive money for absent or numerous owners, as here, where the principals were a deceased owner's heirs. The holding is that the agent's liability for an offence founded on the principals' sale cannot exceed theirs where the allegations are common and she is not alleged to have exceeded her authority. That is sound: an agent's acts within authority are the principals' acts.

The boundary matters. The Court does not hold a GPA holder immune. Protection depended on the absence of any allegation of excess of authority, concealment from the principals or misappropriation. An agent who keeps the proceeds or signs without authority presents a different case, and the principals' remedy would lie in their own complaint. The parity point also carries a message about consistency: the petitioner's case came before a different bench from the one that had discharged the landowners on identical allegations, and the Court insisted that the second bench should have followed the first.

### 3.4 Section 423 and the instrument of transfer

The Court's treatment of Section 423 IPC deserves separate notice because the provision is rarely examined. It reaches a false statement as to consideration in an instrument of transfer. The Court asked a simple question: where is the false statement? Neither the 2012 agreement nor the 2016 sale deed contained one. It then observed that even a false averment in the sale deed would have created a right in the ultimate buyer and not in the complainant. The point is that the section protects those who take under the impugned instrument, and the complainant here claimed under a different, earlier agreement. Complainants who plead Section 423 should therefore identify the instrument, the false recital and their own standing under it.

The related civil point is one on which the judgment is silent but which explains why the complainants chose the criminal route. An agreement to sell does not, of itself, create an interest in the land under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, as that section is generally understood; the buyer's right is contractual, and enforcing it against a later transferee depends on notice and on the equities of the case. That gap between a contractual right and an interest in the land is what makes a buyer who has been overtaken by a sale feel exposed, and it explains the pull towards the police station. This reading of Section 54 is general background and was not retrieved from the bare Act for this article.

### 3.5 Restraint on "triable issues"

The third layer cautions against importing speculative factual questions into a quashing petition. The lower sale price and the question of remittance could not sustain a prosecution, the first because it implicated the landowners equally and the second because it lay outside the allegations. A "triable issue" that does not arise from the FIR and final report is not a reason to let proceedings continue.

### 3.6 Critique and unresolved questions

The decision is correct on its facts, and its treatment of Sections 420 and 423 IPC is orthodox. Three limits deserve mention. First, because it cites no authority, later litigants must supply the doctrinal scaffolding from the wider line of civil-versus-criminal decisions, which this article does not examine. Second, the judgment says nothing on how the pendency of the civil suit should shape criminal proceedings, beyond leaving the remaining questions to the civil court. Third, the FIR was registered in 2016, so the Court applied the IPC. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 has replaced it for later offences with different numbering, and whether the reading of "deception" and "inducement" carries over depends on the new text. This article has not retrieved those provisions, and the point is unverified.

## 4. Practical Significance

**Buyers.** The case warns against the criminal complaint as a litigation tactic. An agreement to sell is a contract, and the usual remedy for non-completion is specific performance or damages. A buyer who lets the completion date pass, without tendering the balance or calling for the deed, should expect a court to notice the delay, as the High Court did for the landowners and the Supreme Court did here. To plead cheating, a buyer must identify deception operating when the money was paid: a false claim of title, an undisclosed prior sale, a forged document or an undisclosed encumbrance. The safer sequence is to document readiness and willingness, tender the balance in writing within time, call upon the seller to execute, and sue promptly, seeking interim protection against alienation. The Court's remark that the buyers "woke up" only after the sale shows how silence colours a case.

**Sellers and attorneys.** An agreement that has lapsed should be put to rest in writing, by termination notice or by the buyer's acknowledgment, and the later sale deed should disclose the earlier agreement and its status. The complainants alleged concealment of the 2012 agreement; a recital would have foreclosed the argument. A GPA holder should act strictly within the instrument, record that she receives money for named principals, issue receipts in that capacity and account to them in writing. Where the attorney also holds her own agreement for sale, as here, the documents should state the capacity in which each act is done, since the double role invites suspicion.

**Purchasers.** The ultimate buyer was not before the Supreme Court, but the specific performance suit still threatens the title. Due diligence should include searches for pending suits and earlier agreements to sell, written inquiry of the vendor, and public notice. An indemnity and a retention from the price pending known litigation are sensible additions. These points are the author's practice commentary, not findings of the Court.

**Litigators and the courts.** The judgment offers a template for a quashing petition in a land-sale case: state the dates of agreement, payment and sale; show the land was transferable when the money was paid; show the civil suit is pending; and identify the elements of Sections 420 and 423 that the allegations do not disclose. An agent's petition should add the parity point where the principals have obtained relief. Complainants should note that the civil court is the forum for a genuinely contested sale, and that police and Magistrates should scrutinise an FIR lodged after, and alongside, a civil suit on the same transaction.

## 5. Conclusion

*Sudha Rakesh* delivers a clear message. A failed land sale is a civil dispute unless the allegations show that the seller deceived the buyer when the buyer paid. The Supreme Court tied cheating to the moment the money was delivered, rejected a prosecution built on a later sale to a third party, and held that an attorney acting within her authority and confirming the principals' sale could not be treated more harshly than the principals, who had already been discharged. It also reminded the High Courts that a coordinate bench's reasoning on the same FIR is not to be avoided through peripheral distinctions.

The limits matter as much as the holding. The judgment decides nothing on the buyers' entitlement to specific performance, on readiness and willingness, or on the ultimate purchaser's position. Its limitation recital is not a finding, and the dates suggest the civil suit was not obviously out of time. It cites no precedent, and its application under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita is untested.

For practitioners the lesson is practical. Draft agreements to sell with clear completion dates, tender and termination mechanics and disclosure of prior commitments. Document the capacity in which an attorney acts. File the civil suit promptly. Treat a criminal complaint as a remedy for fraud at the inception of a transaction, not as leverage in a contract dispute. The Court's phrase, "casting the shadow of criminality on a pure civil dispute", will probably be quoted often; its value will depend on how carefully the High Courts apply the timing test beneath it.

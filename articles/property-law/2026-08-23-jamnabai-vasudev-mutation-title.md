A three-decade-old family dispute over 12.41 acres in Indore has given the Supreme Court fresh occasion to restate a foundational, if perennially litigated, principle: a mutation entry in revenue records is not a title deed, and no amount of administrative acquiescence can substitute for a document of conveyance.

## 1. Introduction

On 20 August 2026, a Bench of Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice Augustine George Masih delivered judgment in *Jamnabai and Others v. Vasudev and Others*, arising out of Special Leave Petition (C) No. 39 of 2026. The Court set aside a Madhya Pradesh High Court decision that had, in a second appeal under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 ("CPC"), overturned concurrent findings of two courts below and denied the appellants — the widow and children of one co-owner — their declared share in ancestral agricultural land. In doing so, the Supreme Court restated two propositions that recur with striking regularity in Indian property litigation: first, that revenue mutation is an administrative and fiscal exercise incapable of creating, transferring or extinguishing proprietary rights; and second, that a High Court exercising second-appellate jurisdiction has no license to re-open concurrent findings of fact absent perversity or an error of law.

This piece proceeds in four parts. It first sets out the facts and procedural history that brought the dispute to the apex court. It then undertakes a legal analysis of the two principal holdings — on the evidentiary status of mutation entries and on the constricted scope of Section 100 CPC — situating them within the settled jurisprudence they reaffirm rather than reinvent. It considers the practical consequences of the ruling for conveyancers, litigators, and revenue authorities dealing with unregistered family arrangements. It closes by asking why, given how frequently this principle is restated, mutation-based claims to title continue to reach the Supreme Court at all.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

The property in dispute is agricultural land measuring approximately 12.41 acres bearing Survey No. 307, together with a house, situated at Village Kanadia, Tehsil and District Indore, Madhya Pradesh. The land originally belonged to one Bhagwansingh. On his death it devolved jointly on his two sons, Ramprasad and Vasudev (the first respondent), and revenue records were mutated to reflect their joint holding.

According to the case set up by the appellants — Jamnabai, the widow of Ramprasad, and their children — Ramprasad held an equal proprietary share in the land notwithstanding subsequent events within the family. The appellants' pleaded case, as recorded in the reporting on the judgment, was that Ramprasad suffered from alcoholism and was subjected to humiliation and physical mistreatment by Vasudev, which led Jamnabai to relocate him to her parental village, even as the family continued to receive a share of the agricultural produce from the land and repeatedly sought a formal partition that was never carried through.

Against this backdrop, the Naib Tehsildar passed a mutation order dated 24 April 1990, in Revenue Case No. 3A/6A/1989–90, recording the names of Vasudev and one Jaswant in the revenue records in place of Ramprasad. Ramprasad died two years later, in 1992. His widow and children thereafter instituted civil proceedings seeking a declaration of co-ownership, partition and separate possession, contending that the 1990 mutation did not — and legally could not — extinguish Ramprasad's proprietary interest, since no instrument of relinquishment, gift or sale had ever been executed or registered in respect of his share.

The trial court and the first appellate court both found for the appellants. On the evidence, both courts concurrently held that the mutation entry recording Vasudev and Jaswant in Ramprasad's place did not reflect any valid relinquishment of Ramprasad's title, and decreed that the appellants — as Ramprasad's legal heirs — were entitled to be recognised as co-owners of the property and to a partition of their share. Vasudev's side carried the matter to the Madhya Pradesh High Court in second appeal under Section 100 CPC. The High Court reversed the concurrent findings, apparently attaching significance to the fact that the 1990 mutation had gone unchallenged for decades, and held against the appellants' claim to co-ownership.

The appellants approached the Supreme Court by special leave. The questions before the Court were, in substance: (i) whether a revenue mutation entry — however long unchallenged — can operate to extinguish an existing co-owner's title in the absence of a registered instrument of transfer or relinquishment; and (ii) whether the High Court, sitting in second appeal, was within its jurisdiction under Section 100 CPC in disturbing concurrent findings of fact recorded by the trial court and the first appellate court.

The Supreme Court answered both questions in the appellants' favour. It held that a revenue entry neither creates nor extinguishes title, and that mutation cannot operate as a conveyance or as a relinquishment of proprietary rights; and it held that the High Court had exceeded the limited jurisdiction conferred by Section 100 CPC by substituting its own appreciation of the evidence for concurrent findings that were neither perverse nor vitiated by any error of law. The Bench accordingly set aside the High Court's judgment and restored the concurrent decrees of the trial court and the first appellate court declaring the appellants co-owners entitled to partition.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The evidentiary — not dispositive — character of revenue records

The central holding in *Jamnabai* sits within a long and largely unbroken line of authority on the legal character of revenue mutation. Indian land revenue administration requires the maintenance of records of rights — registers that note, among other things, the name of the person in possession or recorded as the "occupant" or "Bhumiswami" for purposes of assessment, collection of land revenue, and administrative convenience. These records are compiled under state land revenue legislation (in Madhya Pradesh, the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code, 1959) and serve fiscal and administrative ends. They are emphatically not instruments of title. This distinction — between a record that is probative of possession for revenue purposes and a document that operates in law to create, transfer, limit or extinguish an interest in immovable property — is one the Supreme Court has articulated repeatedly, most influentially in *Sawarni v. Inder Kaur*, (1996) 6 SCC 223, where the Court held in unambiguous terms that mutation of a property in revenue records does not create or extinguish title, nor does it have any presumptive value on title; it only enables the person in whose favour mutation is ordered to pay land revenue.

*Jamnabai* applies that settled doctrine to a specific and commonly recurring factual pattern: a mutation entry that substitutes one family member's name for another's, unaccompanied by any registered deed, and left unchallenged for a long period — here, roughly three decades. The temptation for a court, faced with such prolonged inaction, is to treat acquiescence as a species of informal relinquishment. The Supreme Court's holding forecloses that inference as a matter of law. Extinguishment of an existing proprietary right in immovable property — whether by sale, gift, exchange, or release — is governed by the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and, where the value of the right exceeds the statutory threshold, requires a registered instrument under Section 17(1)(b) of the Registration Act, 1908. A family understanding, however long-standing, that is never reduced to a registered release deed simply does not effect a transfer in the eyes of the law. The mutation order that followed such an informal understanding is, at most, an administrative record of what the revenue authority was told; it cannot cure the absence of the legal formality the statute requires.

This has an important corollary for limitation. If mutation cannot extinguish title, then a suit for partition and declaration brought by a co-owner (or the co-owner's heirs) is not, without more, defeated merely because the plaintiff waited years before challenging the mutation entry itself. What matters for limitation purposes is when the plaintiff's title was actually extinguished or when a hostile, adverse claim to exclusive title was asserted and known to the plaintiff — not the date of an internal revenue reclassification that never purported to be, and could not in law operate as, a transfer. This is consistent with the broader principle that adverse possession or ouster must be established independently, through possession that is open, continuous and hostile to the true owner's knowledge, rather than inferred wholesale from an unchallenged revenue entry.

### 3.2 The constricted compass of Section 100 CPC

The second, and arguably more consequential, limb of the judgment concerns appellate discipline. Section 100 CPC confines a second appeal to the High Court to cases involving a "substantial question of law." This threshold was inserted, and has been consistently policed by the Supreme Court, precisely to prevent High Courts from converting what is meant to be a narrow error-correcting jurisdiction into a second round of fact-finding. The Supreme Court's own jurisprudence — reflected in decisions such as *Kondiba Dagadu Kadam v. Savitribai Sopan Gujar*, (1999) 3 SCC 722, and *Hero Vinoth (Minor) v. Seshammal*, (2006) 5 SCC 545 — holds that concurrent findings of fact by the trial court and the first appellate court cannot be disturbed in second appeal merely because the High Court would have weighed the evidence differently; interference is permissible only where the findings are shown to be perverse, based on no evidence, or vitiated by the application of a wrong legal test.

In *Jamnabai*, the High Court's error, as the Supreme Court characterised it, was to reverse concurrent findings on relinquishment and on the legal effect of the mutation without identifying any substantial question of law that could justify disturbing them. Framed this way, the Section 100 holding is not merely procedural housekeeping; it operates as a structural safeguard for the mutation principle itself. If High Courts were free to revisit factual findings on the strength of long, unchallenged possession or mutation, the doctrinal clarity of *Sawarni* and its progeny would erode in practice even while remaining formally intact in precedent. By insisting on jurisdictional discipline at the second-appeal stage, the Supreme Court closes off a route by which the "mutation is not title" principle might otherwise be diluted case by case.

### 3.3 Unresolved questions and a note of critique

The judgment, at least as reported, leaves certain questions understandably unaddressed because they were not squarely before the Bench. First, the precise legal characterisation of the family's conduct — decades of acceptance of a share of produce alongside repeated but unfulfilled requests for partition — sits uneasily between acquiescence and estoppel. The Court's holding correctly forecloses reliance on mutation alone as a substitute for a registered relinquishment, but a harder case would arise if the respondent's side had pleaded, and proved, an oral family settlement coupled with part-performance or possession referable to that settlement, engaging doctrines distinct from mutation as such. That case was not this one, and the judgment should not be read as foreclosing arguments of that different kind in a future dispute.

Second, the judgment does not appear to engage with the interaction between mutation and the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code's own provisions on recording of rights following succession, which is understandable given that the core vice identified was the absence of any valid relinquishment rather than any defect in the mutation procedure itself. A more granular treatment of when a mutation proceeding under state revenue law can itself be collaterally attacked in a civil suit — as opposed to merely being denied evidentiary weight — would have been a useful addition, though its absence does not detract from the correctness of the result.

On balance, the reasoning is unremarkable in the best sense: it applies settled law faithfully to a recurring fact pattern, and it uses the discipline of Section 100 CPC to prevent that settled law from being eroded by appellate second-guessing. Its significance lies less in doctrinal innovation than in the persistence of the problem it addresses — three decades after the mutation, and well over a century after the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the Registration Act, 1908 laid down the formalities for a valid transfer, disputes of exactly this character still make their way to the apex court.

## 4. Practical Significance

For conveyancers and due-diligence practitioners, the ruling is a reminder — not a novelty, but one worth restating precisely because it is so often forgotten in practice — that a clean revenue record in a vendor's name is necessary but never sufficient proof of marketable title. Title verification in any transaction involving agricultural or ancestral land should trace the chain of registered instruments (sale deeds, registered release or relinquishment deeds, probated wills, or registered partition deeds) independently of what the current *khatauni* or record of rights shows. Where a purchaser's diligence reveals that a vendor's name entered the revenue record through mutation following an unregistered family arrangement rather than through a registered conveyance, that should be treated as a red flag warranting further inquiry into outstanding co-owners' rights, not as a fact cured by the mere passage of time.

For drafting practice, the judgment reinforces the importance of formalising family settlements and relinquishments through registered instruments at the time they are made, rather than allowing them to be evidenced — years later, and often in litigation — only by an administrative mutation entry. A registered release deed executed under Section 17(1)(b) of the Registration Act, 1908 costs comparatively little at the time of a family arrangement; the alternative, as this case illustrates, is protracted litigation stretching across generations, with the original parties to the arrangement often dead before the dispute is finally resolved.

For litigation strategy, the decision offers two levers. Plaintiffs seeking to displace an adverse mutation entry can rely on the settled principle that the entry alone does not extinguish their title, shifting the evidentiary burden onto the party invoking mutation to independently establish a valid transfer. Defendants who genuinely hold registered title, by contrast, should ensure their pleadings and evidence at trial establish the underlying conveyance itself rather than resting on the revenue record, since an appeal built solely on the weight of an unchallenged mutation is now, if it was not already, a fragile one. Litigators appealing adverse concurrent findings should also take note of the reaffirmed rigour around Section 100 CPC: a second appeal that cannot identify a substantial question of law — as opposed to a disagreement with how the evidence below was weighed — is unlikely to survive scrutiny at the Supreme Court even if it succeeds before the High Court.

For revenue authorities and registrars, the judgment is a caution against treating mutation proceedings as a mechanism for resolving competing title claims. Naib Tehsildars and other mutation authorities exercise a limited, summary jurisdiction that is not equivalent to civil adjudication of title; parties with disputed claims should be directed to the civil court, and mutation orders should not be represented — to litigants or in subsequent proceedings — as having settled ownership. For homebuyers and lay purchasers of agricultural or family land, the practical lesson is blunt: insist on seeing the registered deed behind the revenue entry, not merely the entry itself.

## 5. Conclusion

*Jamnabai v. Vasudev* breaks no new doctrinal ground, and its significance is, in a sense, precisely that it did not need to. Nearly three decades after a disputed mutation order and over a century after the formalities for transferring immovable property were codified in the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the Registration Act, 1908, the Supreme Court found it necessary to say again, and plainly, that a revenue entry is not a conveyance. The judgment's twin holdings — on the fiscal, non-dispositive character of mutation, and on the narrow compass of second-appellate jurisdiction under Section 100 CPC — reinforce each other: the first supplies the substantive rule, and the second protects it from erosion through appellate re-appreciation of facts.

The forward-looking lesson for practice is less about the law, which remains as settled as it was after *Sawarni v. Inder Kaur* three decades ago, than about documentation discipline. Every unregistered family arrangement left to be "evidenced" only by an administrative mutation entry is a latent partition suit waiting for the generation that inherits it. Until registered instruments become the invariable practice for every relinquishment, gift, or family settlement affecting immovable property — and not merely the counsel of textbooks — cases with the shape of *Jamnabai* will keep reaching the Supreme Court, decades after the events that gave rise to them.

# Severance Damages Are Measured on the Land Left Behind: Surinder Ahuja v. State of Punjab

*No qualifying property law development was found within the 48-hour or seven-day search window, so this article addresses a recent Supreme Court judgment of continuing importance, delivered on 29 September 2026.*

## Introduction

Compulsory acquisition rarely takes a whole holding. More often it cuts a strip through it, and the owner is left with two fragments and a barrier between them. In *Surinder Ahuja v. State of Punjab* (2026 INSC 1068), a Bench of Justice Dipankar Datta and Justice Sheel Nagu dealt with exactly that injury. The Court held that severance damages under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 are measured on the land left behind, not on the land taken, and it restored a 50 per cent rate on the market value of the remaining land.

The Court also rejected a "radial" step-down valuation model and reaffirmed that solatium attaches only to land market value.

## Case summary

The 47 appeals arose from acquisition for the Aspal Extension Drain, an 18.42 km channel, 264 to 340 feet wide, across six villages of Fazilka district, Punjab. Notifications under Section 4 of the 1894 Act issued in November 2000, and the Section 6 declaration followed on 2 March 2001. The Collector's award of 6 August 2001 fixed Rs 2,25,000 per acre for irrigated land and awarded nothing for severance.

On references under Section 18, the Reference Courts enhanced the market value to Rs 5,00,000 per acre in three villages and to lower figures elsewhere. They also awarded severance charges at rates between 10 and 50 per cent. The Punjab and Haryana High Court, by a common judgment of 29 March 2019, replaced these with graded rates of Rs 2,99,000 to Rs 3,39,000 per acre. It fixed severance at a uniform 40 per cent, calculated on the value of the acquired land.

The Supreme Court framed five issues and decided them as follows.

- **Market value.** The Court restored a uniform Rs 5,00,000 per acre. It held that the Reference Court rightly relied on an earlier 1996 award at village Sultanpur, on the same road and for a comparable drainage purpose, and on evidence that the villages were classified as "special villages" attracting the urban 9 per cent registration rate. It held that the High Court's step-down, which cut Rs 20,000 per acre for each village further from the centre, was arbitrary.
- **Fruit trees.** The Court upheld the High Court's limit of compensation to the 280 trees pleaded in the reference petition (Rs 5,38,300), because evidence cannot travel beyond pleadings.
- **Severance.** The Court restored 50 per cent of the market value of the remaining unacquired land.
- **Solatium and additional interest.** The Court affirmed that these are not payable on trees, tubewells or severance.
- **Tubewells.** The Court affirmed the flat Rs 50,000 per tubewell, since the private valuation reports behind a higher award were never proved through their author.

The Court directed that additional compensation be paid within six months, with any recoveries made within nine months after notice.

## Legal analysis

### The severance holding

Section 23(1) of the 1894 Act directs the court to consider, "thirdly", the damage sustained by the person interested "by reason of severing such land from his other land". The text points at the other land. The compensation is for what happens to the remainder. The High Court nonetheless applied a formula calculated on the acquired strip, drawing on a Division Bench rule in *Tehal Singh v. State of Punjab* (1987 RRR 495) that fixed severance at 40 per cent of the value of the land acquired where the severed portion is two acres or less and lies away from the village abadi.

The Supreme Court found a "fundamental legal error" in that shift of baseline. Its illustration was concrete: the lead appellant lost 79 Kanals 2 Marlas, but the remaining 249 Kanals 17 Marlas were bifurcated. A percentage of the narrow strip's value bore no relation to the loss suffered by the far larger remainder. The Court relied on *Smt. Tribeni Devi v. Collector of Ranchi* ((1972) 1 SCC 480) for the proposition that physical separation causes diminution in the value of what is left, for which separate and robust compensation must be allowed.

The Court's treatment of *Tehal Singh* is the most useful part for practitioners. It did not overrule the case. It held that the High Court had applied it as a "geometric rule of thumb" based on distance from the abadi, while ignoring the physical reality on the record. The State's own irrigation officer admitted that the drain was about 100 feet wide with banks 10 to 12 feet high, that it could not be crossed without a bridge, and that the bridges were about 2 km apart. The Court noted that one bridge was seven feet wide with a nine-tonne capacity, so a combine harvester could not cross it. Land on the far bank was, in the Court's words, left in "absolute, practical isolation". It cited *Walchandnagar Industries Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra* ((2022) 5 SCC 71) for the principle that where acquisition destroys the functional integrity of the owner's remaining property, the State must compensate the actual loss of utility.

On a fair reading, the reasoning is strong on the choice of baseline and on the refusal to treat a precedent formula as a rule of law. It is less satisfactory on quantum. The judgment restores 50 per cent of the remainder's market value without explaining why that figure, rather than 40 or 60, matches the loss. The Reference Court's figure is endorsed as "fully justified" on the facts, but the opinion offers no method by which a later court could test a different case. The risk is that the figure hardens into a new rule of thumb, replacing the one the Court has just criticised.

### Parity and the radial model

On market value, the Court drew on *Ali Mohammad Beigh v. State of Jammu & Kashmir* ((2017) 4 SCC 717) and *Union of India v. Harinder Pal Singh* ((2005) 12 SCC 564) for project-wide parity, and on *Krishan Kumar v. State of Haryana* (2025 INSC 638) for the principle that similarly situated lands should be compensated equitably unless objective distinctions justify otherwise. It also noted that the High Court's model gave a village ten kilometres from the border Rs 2,79,000 but a border village Rs 3,19,000, an anomaly that justifies rejecting it.

### Solatium and the limits of Section 23

The Court's affirmation of *State of Punjab v. Amarjit Singh* ((2011) 4 SCC 734) is orthodox. Solatium under Section 23(2) and additional amount under Section 23(1-A) are payable on the market value under the first factor of Section 23(1) alone. Trees fall under the second factor, severance under the third, and tubewells under the fourth or fifth. The Court held that an older High Court decision treating trees as land cannot survive *Amarjit Singh*. The consequence is that a generous severance rate is not enhanced further, which tempers the practical effect of the 50 per cent holding.

## Practical significance

For landowners and their counsel, the judgment gives a clear instruction on pleading and proof. Severance should be claimed and computed on the remaining land, supported by site plans and by the acquiring authority's own admissions about access. Evidence that a barrier cannot be crossed by ordinary agricultural machinery proved decisive here. Claimants should also plead every item of loss at the reference stage: the Court refused compensation for 482 further trees because the reference petition, which it treated as the pleading, was never amended.

For the State and acquiring bodies, the decision removes the safety of fixed percentages. A distance-from-abadi test will not defend a low severance award where the record shows real loss of access. Collectors who award nil severance, as here, will find the point reopened on reference.

For valuation, the rejection of mechanical step-down models strengthens the case for uniform rates across contiguous land acquired for a single scheme, provided registration rates, comparable awards and development evidence support it.

The case arose under the 1894 Act. Its force for acquisitions under the 2013 Act is untested, and this article has not examined that Act.

## Conclusion

*Surinder Ahuja* is a useful correction to formula-driven valuation. Its central point, that severance damages answer the loss suffered by the remaining land, follows directly from the words of Section 23(1) and from earlier authority, and the Court applied it to a factual record that made the loss obvious. Its weaker point is the absence of any principled method for fixing the percentage, which leaves future courts either to follow 50 per cent by habit or to depart from it without guidance. Counsel will do best to build the evidentiary record that justified the figure here: physical barriers, access constraints, machinery limits and the actual diminution in the usability of the remainder.

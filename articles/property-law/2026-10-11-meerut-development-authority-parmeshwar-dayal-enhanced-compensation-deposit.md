# Paying the Landowner Before the Appeal Ends: Meerut Development Authority v. Parmeshwar Dayal and the Mechanics of Interim Release of Enhanced Compensation

*A two-judge bench of the Supreme Court has used a batch of special leave petitions to restructure how a development authority must deposit, and landowners may draw, a very large enhanced land acquisition award while the authority's appeals remain pending before the Allahabad High Court.*

## 1. Introduction

On 8 October 2026, the Supreme Court of India disposed of a batch of fifteen special leave petitions arising from the acquisition of land for the Shatabdi Nagar Housing Scheme at Meerut. Dipankar Datta and Sheel Nagu, JJ. did not decide whether the enhanced compensation is correct. That question belongs to the Allahabad High Court, where the Meerut Development Authority's first appeals are pending. The bench instead decided how money should move while those appeals wait: the Authority must deposit the whole enhanced sum, a fixed fraction goes to the landowners at once, the rest earns interest in a bank, and the High Court is asked to decide the appeals within six months.

Because the judgment is a disposal order on interim arrangements, it does not announce a new rule of land acquisition law. Its value lies elsewhere. It shows how the Supreme Court now manages one of the most persistent problems in compensation litigation: awards that are enhanced decades after the acquisition and then sit under appeal for years, leaving the acquiring body exposed to a huge liability and the landowner without the money. It also fixes a numerical template, 8.33 per cent of each enhanced award, that other benches and High Courts may adopt.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### The acquisition and the award

The acquisition dates to 1987 and was made under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 for the Shatabdi Nagar Housing Scheme, Meerut (para 3). The Special Land Acquisition Officer announced his award on 22 February 1990, fixing compensation at Rs. 20 and Rs. 27 per square yard for the lands in two villages. Dissatisfied landowners sought references to the civil court for enhancement.

Those references took more than three decades. On 30 March 2024, the Reference Court, the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority at Meerut, enhanced compensation to Rs. 690 and Rs. 720 per square yard for the two villages respectively (para 3). Measured against the original award, that is roughly thirty-five times the earlier figure for one village and nearly twenty-seven times for the other. Different landowners received different sums, and counsel placed a chart before the Court as Appendix-I (para 4).

### The appeals and the interim regime in the High Court

The Authority challenged the enhancement by first appeals under Section 54 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894, which provides for appeals to the High Court from an award of the Reference Court. These appeals, a batch known as the Shatabdi Nagar group, remain pending before the Allahabad High Court (para 5).

In First Appeal No. 717 of 2025, by order of 17 September 2025, the High Court stayed the award on conditions. The Authority was to deposit 50 per cent of the enhanced amount in cash and furnish security for the remaining 50 per cent. The claimants could withdraw the cash deposit, with the balance to abide by the final decision (para 5).

### The earlier Supreme Court order

The Authority approached the Supreme Court. An earlier coordinate bench, which included Datta J., had on 24 April 2026 passed an order in SLP (C) No. 15370 of 2026 and connected matters. In that group, on an enhanced sum of about Rs. 6 crore, the Court directed release of only Rs. 50 lakh to the landowners, about 8.33 per cent, subject to conditions (para 6). The present batch of fifteen petitions followed the same approach.

### The directions of 8 October 2026

The Court disposed of the petitions with these directions (paras 7 to 14):

- The Authority must deposit the entire enhanced sum with the High Court Registry within four weeks.
- The Registrar General is to set apart 8.33 per cent of each respondent's enhanced compensation. The balance is to be placed in interest-bearing fixed deposits with a nationalised bank, on auto-renewal, subject to further orders of the High Court.
- The set-apart sum is payable to the respondents in accordance with law, without prejudice to the rights of the parties in the pending first appeals.
- If the Authority succeeds in the first appeals, the respondents must refund the amount released to them.
- The High Court is requested to take up the Shatabdi Nagar batch on priority and, preferably, decide it within six months of communication of the order.
- If the High Court cannot decide within six months, the respondents may apply for release of such further sum as the High Court thinks appropriate.
- A copy of the order goes to the Registrar General of the High Court for placement before the Chief Justice, and pending applications stand disposed of.

### The Court's stated reasons

The reasoning is brief. Because the petitions arose from appeals still pending in the High Court, the Court saw no useful purpose in keeping them alive (para 7). It followed its earlier order to keep outcomes consistent across the batch. And it expressed regret that the High Court had not heard the appeals on priority despite an earlier request, recognising the heavy workload of the High Court but stressing that a roster bench should make a sincere effort to expedite such matters (para 11). The Court also observed that an unusually large enhancement imposes a heavy financial burden on the Authority and leaves the respondents uncertain of the final figure. In para 11 it said that it "request[s] the High Court once again to assign some priority" to the first appeals.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 What the order does and does not decide

The order engages one statutory provision by name: Section 54 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. It says nothing on the merits of the enhancement, the correct market value, or the interpretation of any provision of the Act. It cites no precedent by name and relies solely on the earlier order of 24 April 2026. In doctrinal terms, therefore, it is an exercise of the Court's power to mould interim relief rather than a decision on the law of compensation.

That matters for how far the order can be cited. It is persuasive evidence of how the Court approaches a particular situation, a long-delayed enhanced award under appeal, but it does not lay down a binding principle under Article 141 of the Constitution on the amount that must be released in every case. A High Court deciding a stay application in a similar batch will be entitled to look at the figures before it. A litigant who cites the order for the proposition that 8.33 per cent is the "correct" release will meet the obvious answer that the Court was disposing of fifteen petitions on their own facts and in deference to its earlier order.

### 3.2 The fixed fraction: a defensible default, an imperfect rule

The most striking feature is the move from the High Court's regime to a fraction. The High Court had ordered the Authority to deposit half the enhanced amount in cash and secure the other half, with withdrawal allowed from the cash deposit. Read literally, that regime lets the claimants draw up to 50 per cent of the award. The Supreme Court replaced it with a regime in which the Authority deposits all of the money, but the claimants draw one-twelfth.

The two regimes are strikingly different in whom they favour. The High Court's order protects the landowner's access to money and asks the Authority to bear the cost of cash and security. The Supreme Court's order protects the Authority against a large unrecoverable outflow if the appeals succeed, while still ensuring that the Authority cannot use a bank guarantee or other security in place of cash and so keep the funds. Full deposit means the money is available at the end of the appeal whoever wins, and the fixed-deposit direction means it earns interest in the meantime.

The choice is defensible. A refund obligation is only as good as the recipient's capacity to refund. Some respondents in this batch are cooperative housing societies and an industrial estate cooperative, with decretal amounts running into crores of rupees on the Authority's own chart (Appendix-I, as it appears on the source page). Those are not ordinary individual landowners, and the chance of recovering large sums from them after a successful appeal is a real concern for a public body. A low release percentage limits the downside.

It is also imperfect. A single percentage applied to every respondent treats the claimant who is an elderly individual dependent on the compensation in the same way as a society with other resources. The order contains a safety valve, which is the right to seek further release if the High Court does not decide within six months, but that mechanism makes the claimant's access to more money depend on the High Court's delay, and delay is exactly what the order is trying to cure. The Court also gave no explanation of why 8.33 per cent, and the earlier order derived it from a Rs. 50 lakh payment on about Rs. 6 crore. The figure looks like a product of that particular sum, not a principled ratio.

### 3.3 Appellate delay as the real subject

Read carefully, the order is less about compensation than about time. The acquisition took place in 1987 and the award in 1990. The Reference Court decided the references in 2024. The appeals were filed after that and are still pending in the High Court at the time of the order. Where the claimants receive the sum only at the end, a very large part of any enhancement is, in practical terms, a delayed payment on land that was taken more than thirty-five years ago.

The bench's language in para 11 is notable for its tone. It records that it had earlier requested priority hearing, that the request had not been honoured, and that it "request[s] the High Court once again". The Court acknowledged workload, which is realistic, and held back from fixing a mandatory deadline, preferring "preferably within six months". That restraint is consistent with the Supreme Court's general reluctance to dictate the High Courts' rosters. The direction that a copy be placed before the Chief Justice is the instrument that gives the request some force: it moves the matter from the individual roster bench to the person who controls the roster.

The six-month trigger, however, carries an implicit allocation of risk. If the High Court does not decide in six months, the respondents may seek further release. In other words, the cost of High Court delay is shifted, at least in part, from the claimants to the Authority. That is a sensible incentive. An acquiring body that wishes to keep the funds locked in until the appeal is decided now has a reason to press for an early hearing.

### 3.4 Parity and the batch approach

The order explicitly follows the earlier order of 24 April 2026 to maintain parity. This is a recognisable judicial technique: where many petitions raise the same question, the Court prefers one template to many bespoke orders. It has costs and benefits. Consistency across the Shatabdi Nagar batch is obviously desirable, since landowners in the same scheme should not be treated differently because their petitions were listed before different benches. But consistency can be achieved at the price of attention to differences. The appendix to the order shows that earlier payments, settlement payments and balances vary widely between respondents. Appendix-I, as reproduced, contains visible inconsistencies (for example "FA No. 718 (sic, 717)"), possibly transcription errors, which illustrate the risk of resolving fifteen matters through one chart.

### 3.5 Questions left open

Several questions remain unanswered, and practitioners should not assume the order resolves them.

First, the order does not say whether interest on the deposited sum accrues to the Authority or the claimants. It directs fixed deposits "subject to the orders of the High Court", which suggests the High Court will allocate the interest at the end. Parties should expect to argue the point.

Second, the order does not address the interaction between the released 8.33 per cent and amounts already paid in earlier rounds. The source reproduces a table with columns for earlier payment, settlement payment, assessed decretal amount, payment made and balance. How the new release is to be netted against payments already received is something the parties will have to work out in the Registry, and the lack of explicit instruction may generate disputes.

Third, the refund direction places the risk of a successful appeal on the claimants who receive the released sum. The order does not say whether refund carries interest, or from which date. Silence suggests the point is reserved.

Fourth, and most importantly for future cases, the order does not state any criteria for departing from the 8.33 per cent default. A claimant with demonstrable hardship may reasonably say that the Court's own safety valve should apply. A High Court bench will have to decide that without guidance.

### 3.6 Assessment

On balance, the reasoning is thin but the result is sound. A court that has before it a thirty-five-fold enhancement, a pending first appeal and a public body that has already been directed to deposit half in cash must make a pragmatic call. The Supreme Court's call protects both parties' principal interests: the money is secured, the claimants get something immediately, and the High Court is pressed to decide. What the order lacks is explanation. A short paragraph on why one-twelfth, and why the claimants' hardship does not warrant more, would have made the template more usable. The reader is left to infer the logic from a figure borrowed from a prior order.

## 4. Practical Significance

### For acquiring authorities and development bodies

The order is a warning that an appeal does not buy time at the claimant's expense. A development authority that appeals a large enhancement should expect to deposit the whole sum, not secure part of it with a guarantee, and should expect interim release to the claimants. Budget provisions should reflect the full enhanced liability from the date of the Reference Court's award, not from the date of the appeal's dismissal. Counsel should advise clients that a stay of the award, where obtained, is now likely to be conditional on full deposit if the matter reaches the Supreme Court.

### For landowners and their counsel

Claimants gain a modest but immediate payment and a mechanism for more. Counsel should note three tactical points. The six-month trigger gives a date from which an application for further release can be filed, so the calendar should be diarised from the date the order is communicated to the High Court. A further release application should be supported by evidence of hardship, since the order gives no automatic entitlement. And claimants who receive money should keep it traceable: the refund obligation makes the released sum, in effect, a conditional advance, and dissipating it creates a collection problem that the Court has plainly anticipated.

### For High Courts and registries

The order puts administrative tasks on the Registrar General, who must compute each respondent's share, open auto-renewing fixed deposits and track releases. Placing the order before the Chief Justice signals that listing of the batch is expected to be handled administratively.

## 5. Conclusion

The order of 8 October 2026 is modest in doctrine and meaningful in practice. It does not interpret the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. It does not decide whether the enhancement to Rs. 690 and Rs. 720 per square yard is right. What it does is put the money in a safe place, give landowners one-twelfth of it now, and ask the Allahabad High Court to decide the appeals in six months.

Its strengths are pragmatism and protection of both sides. Its weaknesses are a fixed fraction with no stated rationale, a release mechanism that depends on the High Court's own speed, and unresolved questions on interest, netting of earlier payments and refund. The practical message to acquiring bodies is that appealing a large enhancement means depositing the full amount. The message to landowners is that interim relief is available but is measured, and further release must be earned by the appeal's delay and by evidence of need.

The real test is whether the High Court decides the Shatabdi Nagar batch within the period asked for. If it does, the order will count as effective case management. If not, the six-month trigger will produce a second round of applications, and the question of how much interim money is enough will return to court.

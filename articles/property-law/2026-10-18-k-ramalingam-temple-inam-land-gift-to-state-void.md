# Custodian, Not Owner: The Supreme Court Voids a Gift of Temple Inam Land to the State

*The Supreme Court has held that a trustee cannot gift temple inam land to the Government, that registration cannot cure what the endowments statute declares void, and that the State, as statutory custodian, holds religious property for the deity and not for itself.*

## 1. Introduction

On 16 October 2026, a Division Bench of the Supreme Court of India comprising Justice Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe decided *K. Ramalingam and others v. Government of Tamil Nadu (represented by its Secretary) and others* [2026 INSC 1144]. Justice Aradhe authored the judgment. The Court set aside a Madras High Court order and declared null and void a registered gift deed by which a temporary trustee of a village temple had transferred a little over an acre of temple land to the State for a Taluk Office.

The case matters for three reasons. It settles, at least for Tamil Nadu, that a gift of inam land granted for the support of a religious institution falls under Section 41 of the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act, 1959 (the "HR&CE Act"), which prohibits such gifts outright, and not under Section 34, which permits alienations with sanction. It holds that registration under the Registration Act, 1908, as applicable in Tamil Nadu, cannot validate a transfer the endowments statute declares void. And it states directly that the State is a custodian and regulator of temple property, not its owner, whatever the public purpose.

The Court did not simply restore the status quo. Because a Taluk Office had been built with public funds, it preserved the State's occupation as a lessee on enhanced rent. That remedy deserves scrutiny.

This article summarises the facts and reasoning, analyses what the judgment settles and leaves open, and considers its consequences for conveyancers, temple administrators, registering officers and government departments. It is based on the judgment text as reproduced on AdvocateKhoj and on LiveLaw's report; no authoritative reported version had appeared at the time of writing.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 The land and the gift

The land measures 1 acre 10 cents and lies in Survey Nos. 36/4, 36/5 and 41/2 of Kodumudi Village, Erode District. It belongs to the Nagapalayam Mariamman-Ponnachiamman Temple. On 30 August 2017, the Tehsildar certified, for registration purposes, that S. Bhoopathy, described as the temporary trustee, was the trustee of the temple. The same day, the temporary trustee executed a gift deed in favour of the Government of Tamil Nadu, represented by the District Collector and the Tehsildar, and the Tehsildar accepted it. The stated purpose was the construction of a Taluk Office.

### 2.2 Procedural history

Villagers first challenged the gift in W.P. No. 26913 of 2017, which they withdrew on 7 March 2018. The hereditary trustee then filed W.P. No. 7075 of 2019, invoking Section 34 of the HR&CE Act. On 19 March 2019, the Madras High Court dismissed the petition. It reasoned that Section 34 speaks of exchange, sale, mortgage and lease and does not mention gift or settlement, and that whether the land fell within the Act was a disputed question of fact. It gave the petitioner liberty to sue for a declaration of title.

The hereditary trustee did not pursue the matter further. On 19 August 2019, the Supreme Court permitted devotees to file special leave petitions, and notice was issued the same day. The State filed a counter affidavit on 23 October 2019 defending the gift, and later sought vacation of a status quo order. The turning point came on 21 February 2022, when the Court directed the Secretary in charge of Endowments to state by affidavit whether a trustee can gift temple property. In an affidavit of 23 March 2022, the Principal Secretary of the Tourism, Culture and Religious Endowments Department stated that the land was inam land granted to the deity under Inam Title Deed No. 388, that Section 41 and not Section 34 applied, and that a High Level Committee had agreed to cancel the gift and take the land on lease. A committee fixed rent at Rs. 13,235 per month for three years.

### 2.3 The arguments

The appellant devotees argued that the land was temple property, that the temporary trustee lacked authority, that the gift was void and that Section 22-A of the Registration Act barred its registration. The temporary trustee, claiming to be the hereditary trustee, said the transfer served the public and had the villagers' approval. The State and the HR&CE Department did not dispute that the land belonged to the temple or that the gift could not be sustained, but pointed to the building already erected. The amicus curiae submitted that any alienation had to satisfy Section 34 and that no statutory process had been followed.

### 2.4 The holding

The Court framed the central question as whether the State, as statutory custodian of religious and charitable property, can accept a gift of such property from a temporary trustee of a temple. It answered in the negative. Its reasoning, in summary, ran as follows.

First, the State's own affidavit established that the land was inam land. Section 34(5) excludes inams referred to in Section 41 from Section 34. Section 41(1) declares any exchange, gift, sale, mortgage or lease for more than five years of inam land granted for religious support to be null and void, allowing Government to sanction transactions other than gifts. A gift of such land is therefore void from the start, and no sanction can validate it.

Second, registration did not cure the defect. The Court reasoned that registration under one statute cannot validate what another statute declares void, and that Section 22-A of the Registration Act, as applicable in Tamil Nadu, directs the registering officer to refuse registration of such transfers unless the competent authority's sanction is produced.

Third, and in the alternative, the gift would have failed even under Section 34, because the Commissioner's sanction and the prior approval of Government were absent.

Fourth, a trustee must administer property as carefully as a man of ordinary prudence would administer his own, as Section 28(1) requires. A gratuitous transfer confers no benefit on the donor institution and cannot be prudent management.

Fifth, the State is a custodian and regulator, not an owner. Its sovereign status does not release it from the obligations of a custodian.

On remedy, the Court declared the land to be inam property vested in the temple, held the gift deed null and void and its registration of no legal effect, and set aside the High Court's order. It did not annul the lease. It enhanced the rent to Rs. 31,000 per month from 8 March 2019, to be refixed every three years under Section 34-A but never below that figure, ordered arrears within three months and costs of Rs. 1,00,000 within eight weeks, and left lease terms to the temple trust. It also issued general directions that the State and the endowments authorities must strictly follow Sections 34, 34-A and 41 and the Rules before any exchange, sale, mortgage or lease of religious property, that decisions must be guided by the institution's interest and not administrative convenience, and that the State cannot unilaterally appropriate religious property whatever the label or public purpose.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Why Section 41 and not Section 34

The High Court's reasoning illustrates a familiar interpretive trap. Section 34 of the HR&CE Act lists exchange, sale, mortgage and lease of more than five years. If one reads that list as exhaustive, a gift appears to fall outside the prohibition. The High Court took that view, in the context of a petition under Section 34.

The Supreme Court's route around the difficulty was not to read gift into Section 34. It was to identify the correct provision. Section 41(1) names gift expressly, and Section 34(5) removes inams of that description from Section 34 altogether. The choice between the provisions therefore turned on a question of characterisation, namely whether the land was inam land granted to the deity, and the State's affidavit settled that question by admission.

The classification therefore decides which regime applies, and the regimes differ sharply. Under Section 34 an alienation can be sanctioned if necessary or beneficial, after publication, objections and prior Government approval. Under Section 41(1) a gift cannot be sanctioned at all. The Inam Title Deed is the first document to look for.

The alternative holding, that the gift fails under Section 34 as well, protects the result against a different characterisation of the land, which matters because the High Court had treated the land's status as disputed.

### 3.2 Void, not voidable

The Court described the gift as void from the start. A merely irregular transfer can be ratified, but Section 41(1) permits sanction of non-gift transactions only, so no sanction could validate this one. Two consequences follow. Delay is a weaker defence against a void transaction: the State's affidavit came five years after the gift, and the Court treated the lapse as no obstacle. And a void gift confers no title on the donee, so mutation of the land, its reclassification as "Sarkar Poramboke" and the construction of the Taluk Office created no rights in the land.

### 3.3 Registration cannot cure illegality

Section 22-A of the Registration Act, as applicable in Tamil Nadu, requires the registering officer to refuse registration of transfers of religious institution property unless the competent authority's sanction is produced. The gift was nevertheless registered, and the Court held the registration of no legal effect.

The principle is orthodox: registration does not decide the validity of the transaction a document records. The signal to registering officers is clear: registration without sanction protects no one. A conveyancer who treats registered status as comfort, without checking for sanction, assumes the risk that the instrument is void. The judgment's treatment is brief, however, and does not examine what a registering officer must do where the character of the property is uncertain. It should not be read as a general rule about every statutory prohibition.

### 3.4 The trustee's authority and the prudence standard

The Court's reliance on Sections 27 and 28 supplies a second, independent ground. Section 28(1) requires trustees to administer property with the care of an ordinarily prudent person managing his own affairs. A gift is gratuitous, so it cannot be prudent management and cannot benefit the institution. The reasoning generalises beyond inam land: even for non-inam property, a gratuitous transfer will rarely satisfy the necessary-or-beneficial standard in Section 34. It closes the gap the High Court's reading would have opened, namely that gifts, being unmentioned in Section 34, escape its controls.

One question remains open. The temporary trustee claimed to be the hereditary trustee, and the Tehsildar certified him as trustee for registration. The Court's reasoning assumes that even a validly appointed trustee could not have made the gift, which makes the dispute over status immaterial to the result. It does not address how far a counterparty may rely on a Tehsildar's certificate of trusteeship.

### 3.5 The State as custodian

The most quotable part of the judgment concerns the State's position. The Court drew on *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* [(1954) 1 SCC 412], the seven-judge Bench decision holding that Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution protect religious belief and practice but do not immunise the secular and economic administration of religious property from regulation. It also relied on *A.S. Narayana Deekshitulu v. State of Andhra Pradesh* [(1996) 9 SCC 548], which treats the administration of temple funds and offices as a secular function distinct from worship.

Those authorities are usually cited to uphold State regulation of temples against religious freedom challenges. The Court here turned the same principle round. If management of temple property is a secular function that the State may regulate, then the State's role is that of a regulator and custodian, with duties that run to the deity and the worshippers. Regulation is a trust and not a title. The Court said that a custodian does not cease to be bound merely because it is sovereign.

The Court also looked to comparative law: Section 117 of the Charities Act 2011 of England and Wales, the New York Religious Corporations Law and the Ontario Religious Organizations' Lands Act, each of which requires court or official oversight before religious land is disposed of. The comparison is illustrative, not binding, and shows that supervision of such dispositions is common in mature legal systems. The text reviewed contains a minor inconsistency in the New York section reference, which a reported version may correct.

### 3.6 The remedy and its tension

The weakest part of the judgment, in my assessment, is the remedy, although it is defensible. Having held the gift void, the Court did not order the State to vacate. It preserved the State's occupation as lessee because the building was constructed with public funds and serves the area. The temple gets market-referenced rent back-dated to March 2019 and control of the lease terms. The calculation is transparent: a market value of Rs. 167 per square foot, a multiplier of 225 per cent under G.O. (MS) No. 200 dated 2 November 2018, a total value of Rs. 3,04,38,040, and rent at 0.1 per cent of that value, rounded to Rs. 31,000 per month.

The tension is that the remedy accommodates a party that defended an unlawful gift for years. The Court criticised the State for defending the gift, mutating and reclassifying the land and building on it, and conceding only when an affidavit was required. It marked that with costs of Rs. 1,00,000, which are modest against the land's value. A department that builds on temple land without sanction may expect, at worst, to pay market rent and costs. The directions in paragraph 40 aim to counter that inference, but they direct compliance with the statute and create no new sanction for departure from it. The judgment also does not examine whether the preserved lease complied with Section 34's requirements for leases of more than five years, leaving lease terms to the temple trust. A later case on a differently structured lease may need to answer that.

## 4. Practical significance

### 4.1 Conveyancing and due diligence

For any transaction touching endowment land in Tamil Nadu, the judgment reinforces a due diligence sequence. Establish the institution's status under the HR&CE Act. Establish whether the land is inam, from the Inam Title Deed and revenue records. Identify the regime, Section 34 or Section 41. Where Section 34 applies, confirm the Commissioner's sanction and prior Government approval. Where the land is inam and the transaction is a gift, do not proceed, because no sanction can cure it. A registered deed and a Tehsildar's certificate of trusteeship are not substitutes for these steps.

Practitioners elsewhere should note that the judgment rests on the Tamil Nadu statute. Other States have comparable endowments legislation, but the sections differ and the analysis must be done under local law. The reasoning on custodianship and on the primacy of the statute over registration is likely to be persuasive.

### 4.2 Government departments

Accepting a gift of religious land from a trustee, whatever the public purpose, exposes the State to a declaration of voidness. Where land is genuinely needed, the lawful routes are a sanctioned lease under Section 34, the statutory process for other transactions, or compulsory acquisition where the law permits. Departments should also note the Court's criticism of the State for defending a transaction its own endowments arm regarded as unlawful.

### 4.3 Temple administrators, devotees and registering officers

Trustees should treat gratuitous disposals of institutional property as outside their authority. Devotees should note that enforcement may fall to those who are not trustees: the hereditary trustee dropped out after the High Court's order, and the devotees, permitted to file special leave petitions, carried the case. Registering officers should demand proof of sanction before registering any transfer of property that appears to belong to a religious institution. The Tehsildar's certificate in this case was obtained to facilitate registration and did not cure the absence of sanction.

### 4.4 Litigation strategy

Challengers should plead the character of the land, inam status in particular, and seek the State's position by affidavit early. The decisive step here was the direction to the Secretary to state whether a trustee can gift temple property. Once the State answered, the case was effectively decided.

## 5. Conclusion

*K. Ramalingam* decides a narrow question, whether a gift of temple inam land to the State is valid, and answers it firmly. The gift is void because Section 41(1) of the HR&CE Act prohibits it, registration does not rescue it, and the same transaction would fail under Section 34 for want of sanction. Beneath the statutory analysis sits a principle that will outlast the facts: the State regulates religious property as a custodian, and a public purpose does not convert custody into ownership.

The judgment's strengths are its identification of the governing provision, its alternative holding, and its refusal to let registration or administrative convenience override a statutory prohibition. Its limits are that it is tied to Tamil Nadu legislation, leaves open how far certificates of trusteeship can be relied on, and preserves the State's occupation of land it should never have accepted, at a rent and cost that may not deter repetition.

The questions to watch are whether other High Courts adopt the custodian reasoning under their own endowments statutes, whether the Tamil Nadu authorities issue guidance to registering officers and departments following the directions in paragraph 40, and whether a later case tests the lease arrangement that the Court preserved. Until then, the safe reading for practitioners is this: temple land is held for the deity, and any transaction that disregards the statutory procedure, however well-intentioned, is exposed.

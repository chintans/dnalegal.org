# Wrong Provision, Right Claim: Supreme Court on Third-Party Objections to Possession Decrees in Execution

*A stranger's objection to a possession decree cannot be rejected merely because it cites Order XXI Rule 58 instead of Rule 97, and the decree-holder must first establish the identity of the property.*

## 1. Introduction

A decree for specific performance is only as valuable as the possession it ultimately delivers, and the stage at which that possession is delivered, execution, is where strangers to the suit most often appear. On 14 October 2026, a Bench of S.V.N. Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria, JJ. of the Supreme Court of India decided *Alladin Charities and Zakat Wakf v. Mohammed Saleem alias Mohd. Wajid Ali and Another*, an appeal arising out of a Hyderabad execution petition. A trust claiming the land as waqf property had objected to delivery of possession under an ex parte decree for specific performance. The executing court rejected its objection, and the Telangana High Court agreed. The Supreme Court set both orders aside and remitted the matter for a fresh inquiry.

The decision is short on new doctrine and long on discipline. It applies the Court's March 2025 ruling in *Periyammal (Dead) Through LRs v. V. Rajamani* to hold that a third-party objector does not lose because the application cites the wrong provision of Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC). It also holds that the identity of the property must be established from old and contemporaneous revenue and municipal records, and that the burden of proof cannot be placed on the objector. Both propositions matter to anyone who buys, finances or occupies land that has been the subject of a decree to which they were not party.

This article first sets out the facts and the Court's reasoning, drawing on the text of the judgment as reproduced on a legal-research portal. It then analyses the decision against the scheme of Order XXI Rules 35 and 97 to 103 CPC and *Periyammal*, and identifies the questions the Court left open. It closes with the practical consequences for conveyancers, decree-holders, litigants resisting execution, and lenders. A note on sources is necessary at the outset: the full certified text of the judgment was not available to the author, and the account below depends on a portal reproduction that carries an AI-generated summary. Paragraph references follow that reproduction and should be checked against the certified text before being cited in court.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 The decree

The first respondent filed O.S. No. 2204 of 2012 in the Court of the VII Senior Civil Judge, City Civil Court, Hyderabad, seeking specific performance of an agreement dated 23 February 2001. The second respondent did not contest the suit, and it was decreed ex parte on 18 March 2015, with a direction to execute a sale deed and deliver possession. According to the reproduction, the first respondent's title narrative runs through a 1943 sale deed in favour of the second respondent's father, Ali Ahmed, and an agreement by Ali Ahmed to sell 375 square yards for Rs 8,00,000, said to have been paid in cash. The reproduction is internally inconsistent on who executed the 2001 agreement, attributing it to the second respondent in one paragraph and to Ali Ahmed in another. That discrepancy is unexplained and is flagged here as unresolved.

### 2.2 The appellant's claim

The appellant, a trust, claimed the land through a farman of the then Nizam of Hyderabad, an entry in a 1949 Muntakhab (a register of endowments) and a trust deed of 1976 covering about 3,151.51 square yards. It relied on a history of earlier proceedings: a declaration by the Chief Executive Officer of the Andhra Pradesh State Waqf Board dated 15 May 2000 that the property formed part of Municipal No. 21-1-1098; a writ petition that set that order aside and a writ appeal, W.A. No. 983 of 2001, allowed in the appellant's favour on 18 October 2001; dismissal of a special leave petition filed by others; and a notification in the Andhra Pradesh Gazette dated 20 November 2003 bringing certain properties under the Waqf Act, 1995. A separate suit, O.S. No. 59 of 2002, was dismissed, and that dismissal was upheld in a civil revision petition decided on 22 November 2011.

### 2.3 Execution and the objection

On 5 January 2016 the decree-holder filed E.P. No. 4 of 2016 for delivery of possession. The appellant resisted by filing E.A. No. 94 of 2016, a claim petition. The executing court dismissed it on 29 January 2019 on three grounds: that ownership was not proved because the relevant municipal numbers were absent from the gazette notification and the farman, the survey report and the trustee's authorisation had not been filed; that a petition under Order XXI Rule 58 CPC was not maintainable because the execution petition sought possession and not attachment; and that the claimant had not proved that the property was declared waqf property or was the same as that in O.S. No. 59 of 2002.

The Telangana High Court, in C.C.C.A. No. 94 of 2019, dismissed the appeal. It noted that the witness for the trust had no document authorising him to depose, that the farman and plan were not produced, that the trust had not challenged a 1353 Fasli sale deed, and that Rule 58 governs attachments so that the proper remedy lay under Rule 97. A search of public sources confirms that a Telangana High Court judgment in this appeal, decided on 11 July 2025 by a single judge, exists; its text was not retrieved, so the account of its reasoning rests on the Supreme Court judgment as reproduced.

### 2.4 The issue and the holding

The Supreme Court framed the question narrowly: whether an executing court may dismiss a third-party objection to a possession decree solely because it was filed under Rule 58 rather than Rule 97. For the appellant, Mr C. Aryama Sundaram relied on *Periyammal* and argued that citing a wrong provision cannot defeat a claim, that a detailed inquiry was mandatory and, alternatively, that the trust should be allowed to prove its claim on the documents already on record. For the decree-holder, counsel opposed interference but, asked whether the schedules in the 1943 sale deed, the agreement and the court sale deed had been correlated, accepted that they had not, and suggested a remand with liberty to both sides to plead and lead evidence.

The Court recorded the principles in *Periyammal* as these: Rule 35 permits removal of a person bound by the decree; Rules 97 to 101 deal with obstruction by "any person", including a stranger asserting independent title; Rule 97 operates before possession is delivered and Rule 99 after a stranger has been dispossessed; a decree-holder faced with obstruction by a stranger must proceed under Rule 97 and cannot sidestep it by seeking a fresh warrant under Rule 35; and Rule 101 requires a full inquiry into questions of right, title or interest. On that footing it held that "the quoting of the wrong provision is not a ground for rejecting the prayer of the Third Party Objector" (para 10). It observed that the identity of the property was the decisive factor, that the decree-holder's documents were "sketchy", that the boundaries in the 1943 sale deed and the decree schedule differed, and that "the burden is wrongly fastened on the Third Party/Appellant" (para 10.1). Both lower orders were set aside; the executing court was directed to allow fresh pleas from both sides, hold a fresh inquiry and decide afresh (paras 12 and 13). No costs were awarded. The Court did not decide whether the land is waqf property.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The architecture of Order XXI

Order XXI of the CPC governs execution of decrees. For decrees for possession of immovable property, Rule 35 authorises the court to deliver possession to the decree-holder and, if necessary, to remove any person bound by the decree who refuses to vacate. Rules 97 to 103 then provide a self-contained code for resistance and obstruction. Under Rule 97, a decree-holder obstructed in obtaining possession may apply to the court for a direction to remove the obstruction. Rule 98 lets the court order possession in favour of the applicant, or punish the obstructor, after adjudication. Rules 99 and 100 give a person dispossessed by the decree-holder, or by a purchaser, a remedy to complain and to obtain restoration. Rule 101 requires all questions of right, title or interest between the parties to the application, including their representatives, to be decided by the executing court itself, and not by a separate suit. Rule 103 deems the resulting order a decree for purposes of appeal.

Rule 58, by contrast, sits in the part of the Order dealing with attachment. It prescribes how a claim or objection to attached property is investigated. It has nothing to do with delivery of possession as such. The executing court's conclusion that Rule 58 could not be invoked against a possession decree was, in that narrow sense, probably correct. Its error lay in treating the mismatch as dispositive. A claim that the property belongs to a stranger is the same factual claim whether labelled under Rule 58 or Rule 97, and Rule 101 obliges the court to decide it.

### 3.2 The reliance on *Periyammal*

*Periyammal* is the engine of the judgment. The Supreme Court in that case, decided on 6 March 2025 by a Bench of J.B. Pardiwala and Pankaj Mithal, JJ. and reported as 2025 INSC 329, read "any person" in Rule 97 to include a stranger to the decree and held that the Rule offers a remedy to a stranger who resists delivery by claiming derivative or independent title, and that Rule 99 is available to a claimant already dispossessed. It also held that questions of right, title and interest in the decretal property must be decided under Rule 101 after a full inquiry. It reiterated the mandatory direction that execution be completed within six months of filing and directed High Courts to gather data on pending execution petitions. The present execution petition, filed in January 2016, is ten years later still at the stage of an objection.

*Alladin Charities* does not extend *Periyammal*; it enforces it against a lower court that, on the facts reproduced, had not followed it. The step the Court takes is a modest one: where the substance of the objection is that a stranger holds independent title, the court must treat it as an application under the Rule that fits, whatever label the objector used. The reproduction cites only *Periyammal* by name; whether further authorities were discussed in the certified text is unverified.

### 3.3 Identity of property and burden of proof

The more interesting analytical move is in paragraph 10.1. The executing court had faulted the trust for not proving ownership by producing the farman, the survey report and a trustee's authorisation, and the High Court repeated those points. The Supreme Court looked instead at the decree-holder's side of the ledger. It treated identity of the property, established through old and contemporaneous revenue or municipal records, as the decisive factor, and found that the decree-holder's papers did not correlate the schedule properties: the boundaries in the 1943 sale deed differed from those in the decree schedule.

This reallocates the burden in a way that deserves approval. A decree-holder invoking the coercive power of the court to remove occupants must show that the land to be delivered is the land the decree describes. An ex parte decree, where the judgment-debtor did not contest, carries no judicial finding on identity tested by adversarial evidence. To require a stranger to prove title to the whole parcel before the decree-holder has demonstrated that the parcel is covered by the decree inverts the logical order of proof. The Court's use of the word "wrongly" signals that this is an error of law and not merely a difference of appreciation.

At the same time, the reasoning has limits. The Court did not hold that the decree-holder bears the burden throughout. Under Rule 101 and the general law of evidence, a claimant who asserts independent title must still establish it, and the remand expressly allows both sides to adduce evidence. The better reading is that the decree-holder must first discharge the burden of identifying the decretal property and that the objector's burden on title arises only after, and in proportion to, that showing. The judgment does not state the rule in those terms, and the point is therefore an inference, not a holding.

### 3.4 Collusion, ex parte decrees and the objector's standing

The objector's case is that the decree was collusive and went undefended. The reproduction records that the second respondent was ex parte but shows no finding on collusion, and none should be attributed to the Court. It routes the objector into a full Rule 101 inquiry, which is the correct forum for such allegations. Because *Periyammal* confines Section 47 CPC to matters arising after the decree, collusion is best deployed as evidence that the decretal land is not the objector's land, not as a collateral attack on the decree. The reproduction does not draw that distinction; it is offered here as the likely consequence.

### 3.5 Waqf and the question left open

The land is claimed as waqf property under the Waqf Act, 1995, and the Court deliberately did not decide whether it is. The reproduction available shows no discussion of the Act's provisions on tribunal jurisdiction, so no view is expressed on how a Waqf Tribunal's jurisdiction would interact with an Order XXI objection.

### 3.6 Critique

The outcome is correct and the principle sound, but one matter troubles. The executing court dismissed the objection in 2019, the High Court in 2025, and the Supreme Court has now ordered a fresh inquiry in 2026. Remand is the right remedy because identity was never properly examined, yet the delay, set beside the six-month direction in *Periyammal*, shows how far practice remains from the stated norm. The reproduction records no timeline for the fresh inquiry, so the remand risks repeating the delay it seeks to cure.

## 4. Practical Significance

### 4.1 For decree-holders

A decree-holder who obtains an ex parte decree for specific performance should assume that the decree will be tested at execution. The schedule to the decree must be reconcilable with the title documents, and the boundaries, survey numbers and municipal numbers should match across the agreement, the plaint, the decree and any earlier conveyance. Where they do not, the safer course is to seek correction or clarification from the trial court before filing for execution, and not to await a stranger's objection. An application styled under Rule 35 will not avoid Rule 97 if there is obstruction by a stranger. *Periyammal*, applied here, forecloses that shortcut.

### 4.2 For third parties resisting execution

A third party who learns that possession of land it claims is about to be delivered should file promptly and should plead facts and not just provisions. The label will not defeat the claim, but a poorly documented claim will still fail on the merits at the Rule 101 stage. The objector should assemble revenue and municipal records, survey material, earlier orders concerning the same land and any authorisation for the person who will depose. The lower courts in this litigation faulted the trust for precisely these omissions, and although the Supreme Court did not endorse that approach, it did not suggest that the omissions are irrelevant once the inquiry resumes.

### 4.3 For purchasers and lenders conducting due diligence

Buyers and banks examining a chain of title should not treat a decree for specific performance as conclusive evidence that the land described in it is the land they are being offered. A search should extend to execution proceedings, objections pending under Order XXI, and any waqf, endowment or inam declarations. Where the property has a history of notification under the Waqf Act, 1995, or of earlier litigation over the same municipal number, the gazette entries and the municipal register should be checked against the boundaries in the deed. Mismatched boundaries, as in this case, are a warning that a later claimant may be able to show a different parcel.

### 4.4 For executing courts

An objection by a stranger is a claim requiring adjudication under Rules 97 to 101, with an opportunity to plead and lead evidence. Summary dismissal on grounds of nomenclature is likely to be set aside, with the loss of years.

## 5. Conclusion

*Alladin Charities* is not a landmark, but it is a useful corrective. It confirms that *Periyammal* is the governing authority on obstruction of execution by strangers; that Rule 97 and not Rule 58 is the proper home for an objection to a possession decree; that mislabelling does not defeat an objector's claim; and that the decree-holder, whose coercive remedy is being invoked, must first show that the land it seeks is the land decreed. The Court's emphasis on identity through old and contemporaneous records will be welcomed by practitioners who know that title disputes in older cities often turn on boundaries recorded in documents of 1943 or earlier.

The judgment leaves open the substantive question of whether the land is waqf property, the interaction between Order XXI and the specialised waqf jurisdiction, and the precise allocation of burden between decree-holder and objector once identity is established. Those gaps are not criticisms of a brief judgment that decides only what it needs to decide. They are, however, the points on which the next round of litigation in this case, and in others like it, is likely to turn.

For practitioners, the lessons are plain. Describe the decretal property precisely and consistently. File objections early and support them with records. Treat a decree for specific performance as the start of the enforcement process and not its end. And when an executing court rejects an objection for citing the wrong rule, remember that the Supreme Court has said again that the section number is not the substance.

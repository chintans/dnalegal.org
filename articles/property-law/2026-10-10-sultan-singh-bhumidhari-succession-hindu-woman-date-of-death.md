# The Personal Law Is Not Frozen in 1954: Succession to a Hindu Woman's Bhumidhari Holding After Sultan Singh v. Financial Commissioner

*A Division Bench of the Supreme Court has held that when a Hindu woman who inherited land before 1954 dies after 1956, her Delhi holding devolves under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 as it stood at her death, not under the limited estate she held when the land-reform statute began.*

## 1. Introduction

Land-reform statutes and personal-law statutes were drafted by different legislatures, for different purposes, within a few years of each other. Where they meet, the result is often a succession dispute that waits decades for resolution. On 9 October 2026, in *Sultan Singh (D) through LRs v. The Financial Commissioner, Govt. of NCT of Delhi* (Civil Appeal No. 9146 of 2012), the Supreme Court resolved one such dispute over about 45 bighas in Bhawana village, Delhi.

A Hindu widow, Khajano, inherited agricultural land with only a limited estate. The Delhi Land Reforms Act, 1954 (the DLR Act) then made her a bhumidhar, a heritable tenure-holder. Two years later the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (the HSA) came into force. Khajano died in 1973. Who takes her holding: the male descendants of her first husband's family, who would take if her estate stayed limited, or her own children, who would take if the HSA made her an absolute owner? The answer turns on Section 51(2)(a) of the DLR Act, which sends a female bhumidhar's holding down one of two routes depending on "the personal law applicable to her".

The Court held that personal law means the law in force at death, so the holding devolved under Section 53. This article summarises the decision, analyses its interpretive moves and open questions, and identifies its consequences for practice. It relies on the judgment text as reproduced on a third-party database; paragraph references should be checked against the official report. The decision falls squarely within the 48-hour window and matters because it settles how a recurring fact pattern, a pre-1954 limited estate and a post-1956 death, is to be handled in Delhi.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 Facts and procedural history

The land is Khata No. 273/222, Bhawana village. Mauji Ram died leaving a widow, Khajano, and a son, Ram Chander. Ram Chander died unmarried in 1937, and Khajano inherited a limited estate, one the holder enjoys for life without full power of disposal, reverting on her death to the heirs of the last male owner.

The DLR Act commenced on 20 July 1954, and Khajano was recognised as a bhumidhar under Sections 5 and 11. The HSA commenced on 17 June 1956. Khajano remarried Bal Kishan and had two sons by that marriage (Respondents 2 and 3). She died in 1973.

In 1997 her sons sought mutation. The Sub-Divisional Magistrate allowed it on 25 April 1997, and the Additional Collector (31 July 2000) and Financial Commissioner (17 November 2000) upheld that order. The appellants, male lineal descendants of Mauji Ram's brothers and thus reversioners, filed a writ petition. A single judge of the Delhi High Court quashed the mutation on 9 December 2004, relying on *Ram Mehar v. Dakhan* and *Ram Jivan v. Phoola* (AIR 1976 SC 844). A Division Bench reversed on 9 August 2012 (LPA No. 92 of 2005). The Supreme Court bench was headed by S.V.N. Bhatti, J.; the judgment is signed by Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria, JJ., and the source does not say which wrote the reasoning.

### 2.2 Issue and contentions

At paragraph 17 the Court framed the issue: whether succession to the bhumidhari holding of a Hindu female who inherited before the DLR Act but died after the HSA falls under Section 51(2)(a)(i) or (ii). As quoted in paragraph 36, clause (i) applies where her personal law gave her only a life estate, and the holding goes to the nearest surviving heir of the last male proprietor under Section 50. Clause (ii) applies where she held absolutely, and the holding devolves under the table in Section 53.

The appellants said Khajano's rights were fixed in 1954 as a limited estate, so clause (i) governed, and distinguished *Bajaya v. Gopikabai* ((1978) 2 SCC 542) because the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code, 1954 (MPLRC) differs from the DLR Act. The respondents said the law at death governs and that Section 14(1) HSA had made Khajano an absolute owner.

### 2.3 Holding and reasoning

The Court dismissed the appeal. It treated the DLR Act as a complete code on devolution, approving *Hatti v. Sunder Singh* ((1970) 2 SCC 841) (paragraph 24). It read "personal law" as a general referential incorporation, following *Bajaya*, so that the law in force at death applies (paragraphs 18–19, 38–40). It read Section 51(2) literally: "inherited" is past tense and records how she acquired the holding, while "dies" is present tense and fixes the trigger (paragraphs 32, 38, 42–43). It held that "the personal law is not frozen on the enactment of the Act, 1954" (paragraph 37.1, as extracted in the source).

Applying Section 14(1) HSA, the Court found that Khajano was alive on 17 June 1956 holding a limited estate, which became absolute before her death (paragraph 40). Section 15 HSA was inapplicable because she inherited from her son, not a parent (paragraph 12). *Ram Jivan* was distinguished because the woman there died in 1952, before the HSA (paragraphs 10, 11A). No costs were ordered (paragraphs 44–46).

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The timing gap in Section 51(2)

Section 51(2)(a) classifies a female bhumidhar by the estate she held but does not say as of when. Three dates are plausible: inheritance, commencement of the DLR Act, and death. The appellants needed the second; the respondents the third. The stakes are stark: assessed in 1954, Khajano was a limited owner and the reversioners succeed; assessed in 1973, she was a full owner and her children succeed.

### 3.2 Literal construction and its limits

Resting on text is defensible because Section 51(2) supplies a temporal anchor: it applies where the woman "inherited" before the Act and "dies" holding as proprietor. The tense observation identifies two functions, one describing the past event that created her entitlement, the other fixing the occasion for working out devolution.

Tense arguments, however, prove less than they appear to. A legislature using "dies" in 1954 speaks of a hypothetical future death; it does not follow that it meant the personal law of that future date to apply. The tense argument shows when the question arises. Which version of personal law answers it depends on the doctrine of referential incorporation. The reasoning is strongest when the two are read together.

### 3.3 Referential incorporation and Bajaya

A statute that incorporates a specific provision of another enactment freezes it as it stood; one that refers generally to a body of law takes that law as it develops. *Bajaya* placed the MPLRC in the second category, and *Sultan Singh* extends the reasoning to the DLR Act. The appellants' distinction failed because the operative expression, "the personal law applicable to her", is general in both. The legislature could easily have written "as in force at the commencement of this Act" and did not.

A structural point supports this. The DLR Act has no rules of its own on Hindu women's estates; it borrows them. It would be odd for a borrowing statute to freeze the lender's rules just as Parliament was about to transform them. The Court's remark that legislation "looks to the future" (paragraph 40) captures this: a death between 20 July 1954 and 17 June 1956 would attract clause (i), but a later death attracts clause (ii).

### 3.4 Section 14(1) HSA and remedial purpose

Section 14(1) provides that property possessed by a Hindu female, whether acquired before or after the HSA, is held as full owner, and its Explanation gives "property" a wide meaning. The Court surveyed cases including *V. Tulasamma* (1977), *Eramma* (1966) and *Jagannathan Pillai* (1987) and read them as confirming a remedial purpose; paragraph 41 states that restricting a Hindu woman's rights is disfavoured unless Section 14(2) applies. Nothing in the account consulted suggests Section 14(2), which preserves restricted estates created by instrument, decree or award, applied here, since Khajano's estate arose by operation of law.

The layers fit together. Section 14(1) makes Khajano an absolute owner; the referential reading of Section 51(2) lets that fact enter the DLR Act's devolution scheme. Without the second, the first would be irrelevant; without the first, the second yields nothing.

### 3.5 Ram Jivan

The single judge relied on *Ram Jivan*. A woman who died in 1952 could never be affected by a later change in personal law, so the distinction is close to conclusive. The Court's own reasoning in paragraphs 17–46 does not analyse *Ram Jivan* separately, so the point rests mainly on the Division Bench's earlier treatment.

### 3.6 The anomaly the contrary reading would create

The appellants' reading carries a cost that the Court's approach avoids. If Section 51(2)(a) froze the woman's estate as it stood in 1954, then Hindu women in Delhi who held bhumidhari rights would, for decades after 1956, have been absolute owners of every other item of property under the HSA while remaining limited owners of their agricultural holdings under the DLR Act. A single woman's estate would have been split by the type of asset. Nothing in the account of the judgment suggests the DLR Act was meant to produce that fragmentation, and the Court's remark that restricting a Hindu woman's rights is disfavoured unless Section 14(2) applies (paragraph 41) points the same way. This coherence argument is not a substitute for the textual one, but it explains why the Court was comfortable that the literal and purposive routes converged.

### 3.7 Precedential weight

*Bajaya* was a three-Judge Bench decision, and *Sultan Singh* was decided by a Division Bench. The Division Bench therefore did not depart from binding authority; it applied it to a differently worded statute and explained why the difference did not matter. That is the safer course, and it makes the decision a straightforward extension rather than a new doctrine. A later bench wishing to confine it would have to find a textual difference in the statute before it, not question the principle.

### 3.8 Critique and open questions

**Reversioners' reliance.** The appellants in effect said the 1954 Act fixed rights and that a reversionary expectation should survive later legislation. On the account consulted, the Court did not meet that argument directly, observing instead that restricting a Hindu woman's rights is disfavoured unless Section 14(2) applies (paragraph 41). The judgment leaves open how far a reversioner's expectancy can be protected once Section 14(1) has operated.

**Unadjudicated points.** The respondents argued that the objection based on Khajano's remarriage was untimely and referred to a partition suit. These were argued but, on the account consulted, not decided (paragraph 15). The decision is not authority on the effect of remarriage on a widow's estate.

**Section 48(2).** The judgment notes that Section 48(2) bars a widow or mother from bequeathing her holding, and that the bar attaches to holdings under clause (i). Whether women whose holdings devolve under clause (ii) enjoy testamentary freedom is left open. I could not verify the text of Sections 48(2), 50 or 53 from the source, so this observation carries that limit.

**Method.** The Court says the literal rule applies because the words are clear, yet also cites the golden rule and purposive construction through *Grid Corporation of Orissa* ((2011) 11 SCC 334). That is sound technique, showing every route reaches the same place, but it makes the true ratio harder to isolate.

**Other statutes.** *Sultan Singh* decides a question under the DLR Act. Other state land-revenue and tenancy statutes may contain comparable provisions, but each must be read on its own words.

## 4. Practical Significance

### 4.1 Mutation and revenue practice

Revenue officers dealing with the death of a female bhumidhar must establish the date of death and the personal law then in force before choosing between clauses (i) and (ii). For a Hindu woman dying after 17 June 1956, subject to Section 14(2) and the facts, clause (ii) will ordinarily apply and Section 53 governs. An officer who applies Section 50 merely because she inherited a limited estate before 1954 now risks reversal.

### 4.2 Due diligence and conveyancing

Where a chain of title in rural Delhi runs through a Hindu female bhumidhar who inherited before 1954 and died after 1956, purchasers and lenders can rely on her Section 53 heirs as the proper successors. Searches should still collect the death certificate, mutation record and any will, because Section 48(2) and testamentary questions remain live, and should check for any instrument that might attract Section 14(2) HSA.

### 4.3 Litigation strategy

For reversioners, the decision closes a line of argument for deaths after 1956. Counsel should advise accordingly. The facts also show the cost of delay: mutation was sought twenty-four years after death, and the contest ran almost thirty years more. For heirs of a female bhumidhar, the personal-law argument is strong, but they should apply for mutation promptly and preserve proof of the date of death and her status.

### 4.4 Beyond Delhi

The decision is a guide to method. Where a land statute refers to a female holder's personal law, the first question is whether the reference is general or specific. If general, the date of death governs; if the statute names a particular provision or fixes a date, a different result may follow. Compare the local wording with Section 51(2) and the MPLRC provision in *Bajaya* before applying the reasoning.

### 4.5 Coherence with recent authority

The decision accords with *Ganapati (Dead) by his LRs v. Prabhakar* (22 September 2026), reported by LiveLaw, in which the same Bench held that a widow's limited interest from before the HSA was enlarged by Section 14(1) without any partition. I have read only the news report of *Ganapati*, not the judgment, and cite it only for that general proposition.

### 4.6 Drafting family settlements and wills

Families holding agricultural land in Delhi that traces to a pre-1954 female inheritance should record the date of death, the nature of the estate and the basis for devolution in any settlement deed or mutation application. A recital that the holding devolved under Section 53 by operation of Section 51(2)(a)(ii) read with Section 14(1) HSA, supported by the death certificate, gives revenue officers and later purchasers a clean basis to act on and reduces the room for a reversioner's late challenge.

## 5. Conclusion

*Sultan Singh* answers a question that Section 51(2) of the DLR Act left open. By treating the reference to personal law as general and fixing the date of death as the moment it applies, the Court aligned the devolution of bhumidhari holdings with the evolution of Hindu succession law. This gives effect to Section 14(1) HSA in the land-reform setting and avoids the anomaly of a woman being an absolute owner under one statute and a limited owner under another.

The reasoning is persuasive, though its force lies in the combination of arguments. The tense argument shows when the question arises; referential incorporation, drawn from *Bajaya*, shows which law answers it; and Section 14(1) supplies the content. Each element already existed, and the Court's contribution is to join them for the DLR Act. Open questions remain on remarriage, the testamentary rights of women under clause (ii), and the reach of the reasoning to other state statutes.

For practitioners, the message is to read the local statute closely, decide whether its reference to personal law is general or specific, establish the date of death and the nature of the estate at that date, and act early. The judgment is not yet reported, and paragraph references should be verified against the official text before citation in a pleading.

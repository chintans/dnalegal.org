# Poramboke Land, Void Pattas and the Innocent Flat Buyer: The Supreme Court in S. Gururaj

*The Supreme Court has held that a ryotwari patta granted over land that was never ryoti land is void under Section 14-A of the Tamil Nadu Estates (Abolition and Conversion into Ryotwari) Act, 1948, and that municipal approvals and zoning cannot cure the defect, but it has also ordered the State to rehouse the innocent flat owners before any demolition.*

## 1. Introduction

On 16 October 2026 a Bench of Justices S.V.N. Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria decided *S. Gururaj v. The Additional Chief Secretary / Commissioner of Land Administration* (2026 INSC 1146). The appeals arose from a Madras High Court order directing demolition of a residential block built on land that the revenue records had always described as Government poramboke. Poramboke is the Tamil Nadu term for land reserved for communal or public use, such as tanks, watercourses and grazing grounds, and not available for private ownership. The land had nonetheless been granted a ryotwari patta, mortgaged to a bank for Rs. 3.40 crores, developed into twenty flats, and sold to buyers who relied on a building permit, a zoning classification and the revenue entries.

The Court dismissed the appeals and upheld the cancellation of the patta. It then did something less common in encroachment litigation: it made demolition of the flats conditional on the State first allotting a commensurate vacant site to the flat owners. The judgment therefore speaks to two questions that rarely sit together in a single ruling. The first is doctrinal: when is a patta void and not merely voidable, and what evidence fixes the character of the land? The second is remedial: who bears the loss when public officers wrongly confer title on land the State never relinquished?

This article sets out the facts and the Court's holding, analyses the statutory scheme and the reasoning on each issue, and assesses the consequences for conveyancing, mortgage lending and litigation. It argues that the Court was right on the substantive point, that its treatment of zoning is the most useful part of the judgment for practitioners, and that its remedy, though humane, leaves unresolved who must finally pay.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 The land and its chain of title

The dispute concerned 0.72 acres out of 1.68 acres in Survey No. 572 of Thirumullaivoyal Village, Ambattur Taluk (now Avadi), Tiruvallur District. The 1911 RSR "A" Register, the oldest record in the case, classified the land as "Government Poramboke - Kanakkan Kuttai". The judgment records that later entries consistently described it as tank or Government poramboke (paras 6 and 23).

Despite that classification, the land passed through private hands. Sales in 1980 and 1988 led to one A. Palanichamy Nadar, whose heirs sold to P. Ramadoss in 2004. Avadi Municipality granted a building permit in 2005, and the Chennai Metropolitan Development Authority (CMDA) showed the survey number within a Mixed Residential Zone (para 7).

### 2.2 The patta

On 18 October 2010 Ramadoss applied for a ryotwari patta. A patta is the record of title issued to a landholder under a ryotwari settlement. The District Revenue Officer (DRO), Tiruvallur, granted it on 21 January 2011, reasoning that the land was "never a Kuttai Poramboke but only a ryoti land" (para 7). Ramadoss then mortgaged the property to Tamil Nadu Mercantile Bank Ltd. for Rs. 3.40 crores. In 2012 he settled 0.22 acres on his wife, who gave a power of attorney to a builder. The builder put up twenty flats and sold them, one buyer being the appellant S. Gururaj (paras 8 and 9).

### 2.3 Cancellation and the High Court

By an order of 26 December 2019 the Commissioner of Land Administration set the patta aside. The Commissioner found that the application had been entertained decades after the limitation periods in the governing Government Orders and the estate abolition statutes; that the land was recorded as watercourse poramboke; that the DRO had decided without calling for reports and had misdescribed the file; that subordinate officials had failed to flag the defect; and that the survey number had been irregularly subdivided, creating third-party rights. The Commissioner directed restoration of the land to Government poramboke and ordered disciplinary and criminal action (para 10).

The Madras High Court, in orders of 11 July 2024 and 2 July 2025 in W.P. No. 8661 of 2020, adopted those findings. It held that communal water bodies vest in the State, that Section 14-A of the 1948 Act bars a patta even over a private tank, and that the mortgagee obtained no valid charge against the State. It directed eviction, demolition across the whole 1.68 acres, and restoration of the water body within sixteen weeks. It preserved the bank's remedies under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI Act) and excluded the period of the writ proceedings from limitation under Section 14 of that Act (para 11). A flat owner and the bank appealed (Civil Appeals 13629 to 13631 of 2026).

### 2.4 The Supreme Court's holdings

As reported, the Court decided the following.

First, the State need not first adjudicate that land is a tank before the statutory bar applies; the nature of land is determined from enjoyment, the period of enjoyment and entitlement to patta (para 17). Second, land that is not ryoti land cannot receive a ryotwari patta, and its nature is fixed by the earliest revenue, survey and settlement records, which the appellants failed to show classified the land as ryoti (paras 18 and 19). Third, zoning as "Mixed Residential" does not remove the bar and cannot take away Government rights in poramboke, forests or water bodies (paras 20 and 21). Fourth, the objection that the mortgagee and flat owners were not heard failed, because notice went to the patta holder who claimed the interest (para 22). Fifth, the argument that the records showed private names was "far removed" from the extracts, which consistently showed tank or Government poramboke (para 23). Sixth, Section 14-A prohibits the grant and makes the order void; the DRO acted beyond the Act (paras 24 and 26).

On relief, the Court recognised the hardship of the flat owners. It directed the Chief Secretary and the Collector, Tiruvallur, to allot a vacant, commensurate site to the flat owners, represented by their Society, preferably within six months, and only then permitted demolition of the 0.22 acres built upon. It allowed development of the vacant area, preserved remedies against the developer, owner and mortgagor in the civil court, and left the State free to take disciplinary action against officers (paras 27 and 28). The appeals were dismissed without costs (para 29).

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory architecture

The 1948 Act abolished intermediary estates and converted the land into ryotwari holdings, with the State dealing directly with the cultivator. Under it, entitlement to a ryotwari patta is confined to ryoti land, a category defined by reference to the Tamil Nadu Estates Land Act, 1908. Section 14-A, inserted by Amendment Act 49 of 1974, provides in terms quoted by the Court that "no ryotwari patta shall be granted in respect of any private tank or oorani" (para 4). The effect is twofold. A patta cannot be issued over such land, and an earlier patta over such land is rendered inoperative.

The Court's holding that non-ryoti land lies wholly outside the patta-granting jurisdiction is the foundation of its conclusion that the DRO's order was void. This matters because the appellants' best arguments were all of a kind that applies to voidable orders: delay, reliance, hearing and third-party rights. If the DRO had power to decide the question wrongly, those arguments would have weight. If the DRO had no power to grant a patta over this category of land at all, the order is a nullity and, as the Court put it in substance, the State's officers rather than the purchasers should bear the consequences of the error (para 26).

### 3.2 No prior adjudication of "tank" status

The first issue is the most consequential doctrinally. The appellants argued that the land could not be treated as a tank until some authority had so adjudicated. The Court rejected that, holding that the Act does not demand adjudication to protect communal poramboke, and that the nature of the land is a matter of evidence: how it was enjoyed, for how long, and whether a patta entitlement existed (para 17).

That reasoning is sound. A rule requiring a prior declaration would invert the burden. The State would have to litigate to protect land that was never available for grant, while a claimant could rely on a single mistaken patta to shift the onus. The better view, which the Court adopts, is that the claimant seeking a patta must establish the land's ryoti character, and that the earliest records are the primary evidence. The appellants "did not show" that the survey and settlement records classified the land as ryoti (paras 18 and 19). The decision thus places the burden where the statute's structure places it.

I have not read *Salem Municipality* independently and cite it only as the judgment describes it. The reported judgment does not separately analyse whether every species of poramboke, such as the "Kanakkan Kuttai" land here, falls within Section 14-A; the safer reading is that the Court rests on the broader proposition that non-ryoti land cannot attract a patta, with Section 14-A as the clearest instance.

### 3.3 The weight of the earliest records

The second holding, that the character of land is fixed by the earliest revenue, survey and settlement records, has strong doctrinal support in the logic of land administration. Settlement records were prepared when the State was classifying land for the purposes of revenue, and they preceded the commercial pressures that later produce disputed reclassification. A rule giving primacy to them promotes stability.

The chain here ran from 1980 to 2004, yet no sale could improve the land's original character, since a purchaser takes no better title than the vendor had. The Court found reliance on private names in later records "far removed" from the extracts (para 23).

### 3.4 Zoning and municipal approvals

The most practically significant holding is that the CMDA's Mixed Residential zoning does not remove the statutory bar. In the Court's words, zoning "cannot be construed as taking away the Government's rights in Poramboke lands, forests, water bodies/tanks, etc." (para 21).

The reasoning is correct in law and consistent with the structure of planning legislation. Zoning regulates the permitted use of land that can lawfully be privately held. It is a statement about what may be built where, not a determination of who owns the land. A master plan frequently colours large areas uniformly and cannot be assumed to have verified title to each survey number. Treating zoning as a source of title would allow a planning document to override the Estates Abolition Act, which would invert the hierarchy of norms.

### 3.5 The remedy: rehousing before demolition

The remedial part of the judgment departs from the High Court. The High Court ordered demolition of everything on the 1.68 acres within sixteen weeks. The Supreme Court narrowed the order to the 0.22 acres actually built upon, permitted development of the vacant remainder, and made demolition conditional on prior allotment of an equivalent site to the flat owners (paras 27 and 28).

This is an equitable direction and, in my view, principled. The purchasers had no reason to doubt the title: a municipal permit existed, zoning favoured residential use, and the revenue records, as the Court itself observed, are the place a "bona fide purchaser for consideration" looks (para 26). The Court shifted the cost to the State, which created the appearance of title by issuing the patta in the first place, and left the State to recover from erring officers through disciplinary action.

One difficulty remains: the direction says "preferably within 6 months" but does not say who values commensurability or what follows if no site is available. The civil remedies preserved against the developer, owner and mortgagor mean the flat owners may receive a site but must litigate separately to recover construction cost.

### 3.6 Unresolved questions

The judgment as reported leaves four matters open. It does not state a test for when a purchaser is so far on notice that equitable relief should be refused. It does not explain whether the rehousing direction creates an enforceable entitlement or is a one-off accommodation tied to these facts. It does not address whether the State's long delay, from 2011 to 2019 before cancellation, bears on equitable relief, though the void character of the order makes delay largely irrelevant to the patta's validity. And it does not discuss whether the mortgagee could ever have taken a valid charge, leaving the High Court's holding that no valid charge arose against the State undisturbed.

## 4. Practical significance

### 4.1 Conveyancing and due diligence

Advocates conducting title searches in Tamil Nadu, and by analogy in other States with poramboke or comparable public-use categories, should treat the earliest available settlement register as a primary document. A search that begins with the vendor's patta is not enough. The practical steps are to obtain the RSR "A" Register extract and the Field Measurement Book entry for the survey number; to check the current Town Survey or Adangal classification against it; to ask for the file under which any patta was granted, including the date of application and the reports called for; and to treat any patta issued many years after the abolition of the relevant estate, particularly on application by a private person, as a red flag.

Opinion letters should not treat municipal permission or a CMDA zoning colour as confirming title. A buyer's advocate who writes that title is "clear and marketable" on that footing exposes the client to the very risk this case illustrates.

### 4.2 Lenders

The bank took a mortgage over a Rs. 3.40 crore facility and found, per the High Court, that it held no valid charge against the State. The case is a reminder that banks should not rely solely on a patta and a search certificate. Legal scrutiny reports should record the classification in the earliest settlement record, and valuation should be conditional on that verification.

### 4.3 Developers and flat buyers

Developers who sell flats on land with a doubtful classification carry the civil liability preserved by the Court. Buyers should require, as a condition of purchase, a developer's warranty on classification and an indemnity, and should check whether the land's patta rests on a recent order. For buyers already in possession, the decision offers partial comfort: the Court's rehousing direction shows that equity will be applied to protect innocent purchasers, but it is not a rule of law and cannot be assumed.

### 4.4 Litigation strategy

For the State, the judgment confirms that cancellation of a void patta is available despite long delay and that no prior declaration of tank status is required. For occupants and lenders, the lesson is to raise equitable relief early and specifically. The appellants' arguments on hearing, limitation and third-party rights failed, whereas the plea for hardship succeeded. Counsel should therefore frame a fallback prayer for rehabilitation, rather than rely only on the validity of the patta, which the evidence here made unsustainable.

## 5. Conclusion

*S. Gururaj* decides that a ryotwari patta over land that was never ryoti land is outside the authority of the granting officer and void, that the nature of the land is proved by the earliest revenue records and not by later entries, and that zoning and municipal permission cannot supply what the statute withholds. Each of these propositions is sound and follows from the structure of the 1948 Act and the Court's earlier reading of Section 14-A.

The remedial direction is the more interesting contribution. By requiring the State to allot a commensurate site before demolition, the Court allocated the loss of a defective grant to the party that made it. That is fair, though its mechanics are thin: no timetable binding on the State, no standard for commensurability, and no relief for the construction cost of the flats.

The wider lesson is one of diligence. A long chain of registered sales, a building permit and a favourable zoning map did not protect any link in this chain, because none of them could improve the land's original character. Practitioners should read the case as authority to go behind the patta, to the settlement register, before advising any client to buy, lend or build. The open questions identified above, in particular the reach of Section 14-A to poramboke that is not a tank, and the legal footing of the rehousing direction, are likely to be tested in future appeals.

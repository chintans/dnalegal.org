# Sanctity Is the Reward of Legality: Sterling Holiday Resorts and the Limits of SARFAESI Auction Finality

*The Supreme Court has set aside a confirmed sale of a mortgaged hill resort after a fifteen-year dispute, holding that the finality courts accord to auction sales presupposes an auction conducted in accordance with law.*

## 1. Introduction

On 30 September 2026, a bench of Justice Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe delivered judgment in *Sterling Holiday Resorts Limited v. M/s P.M. Associates & Ors.* (2026 INSC 1071), allowing the borrower's appeals against a Madras High Court judgment that had upheld a sale certificate issued under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 ("the SARFAESI Act"). The Court quashed the sale, dismissed the purchaser's cross-appeals and left the resort, "The Fernhill" at Ooty, with the borrower that had by then repaid the secured creditor in full. The judgment is marked non-reportable, but its reasoning merits attention from anyone who buys, finances or defends title acquired at an enforcement sale.

This article is written within a widened window. No property law judgment dated within the last 48 hours met the selection criteria after a search of the sources available to this run, so the window was extended to seven days; the judgment discussed here is seven days old.

The decision matters for three reasons. First, it restates, in firm terms, that Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 ("the Rules") are mandatory and that the protection of auction finality is conditional on compliance. Second, it identifies a cluster of concrete defects, namely a bid received while a tribunal restraint was in force, a premature sale, an unnoticed revival of a dormant auction, and a sale certificate issued to a non-bidding "nominee" firm, any one of which a purchaser's counsel will now have to anticipate. Third, by expressly declining to decide several contested questions, it leaves open points that will recur: whether a secured creditor's authorised officer can cancel a sale certificate once issued, whether such a certificate requires registration, and when the right of redemption is extinguished.


## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 The facts

The borrower, Sterling Holiday Resorts Limited, secured 1991 loans from the Industrial Finance Corporation of India Limited ("IFCI") and the Tourism Finance Corporation of India Limited by a joint equitable mortgage over its Ooty resort. After default, the lenders sued in the Debts Recovery Tribunal, Delhi in 2000, and IFCI separately issued a Section 13(2) demand notice in 2007 and a Section 13(4) notice of symbolic possession in 2009. Following litigation before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, Chennai, IFCI issued an auction notice dated 25 March 2010 with a reserve price of Rs. 20 crore.

On 7 April 2010 the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal ("DRAT") restrained the Authorised Officer "from in any way proceeding further" under the SARFAESI Act, on the borrower depositing Rs. 1 crore, which it did the next day. IFCI nevertheless received bids, though it did not open them. The High Court set aside the DRAT's order on 6 September 2011. Six days later IFCI opened the bids and declared Ms. Rukmani Khemchand the successful bidder. The full price of Rs. 20,00,10,000 was paid by M/s P.M. Associates, a partnership between her and her brother constituted that very day, and a sale certificate dated 16 September 2011 issued to the firm.

The borrower meanwhile cleared IFCI's dues under the tribunal's decree and a settlement. In February 2012 IFCI cancelled the certificate and refunded the purchaser with interest, which the purchaser encashed. By judgment of 23 August 2013 the Madras High Court nonetheless upheld the certificate, held that the Authorised Officer could not cancel it, and held that it required registration under Section 17(2)(xii) of the Registration Act, 1908.

### 2.2 The holding

The parties argued the officer's power to cancel, registration, and redemption. The Court treated none as necessary. It framed the "pivotal question" as whether the auction was conducted in accordance with law (para 24), and found five infirmities (para 31):

1. **Bid received despite the DRAT restraint.** Receiving bids and earnest money was a step "under the provisions of the SARFAESI Act". Not opening them did not cure it, and the High Court's later reversal could not validate it retrospectively.
2. **Sale within thirty days.** Rule 9(1) of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 ("the Rules") bars sale before thirty days from notice. Excluding the restraint period under the maxim *actus curiae neminem gravabit*, seventeen days remained until 23 September 2011; the sale closed on 12 September.
3. **No notice of the bid opening**, seventeen months after the original date.
4. **Certificate to a non-bidder.** The firm neither bid nor existed on the auction date, and Rules 9(2) and 9(6) look to the highest bidder.
5. **No auction records.** IFCI described the sale as private treaty and then as tender, and never produced the original file or any record of inter se bidding.

On finality, the Court held that the principle "presupposes an auction conducted in accordance with law" and that "[s]anctity is the reward of legality, not a substitute for it" (para 33). It added that the borrower had repaid the dues with possession retained, the collusion plea failed, the purchaser had been refunded, and Article 300A of the Constitution permits deprivation of property only by authority of law (para 34). The High Court judgment was quashed, the purchaser's appeals dismissed, and the contempt petitions and a connected special leave petition on the borrower's merger were not pursued.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The statutory frame

Section 13(4) of the SARFAESI Act empowers a secured creditor, after notice under Section 13(2), to take possession of and sell the secured asset without the intervention of a court. Section 13(8), as it stood before its substitution by Act 44 of 2016 with effect from 1 September 2016, barred sale or transfer if the creditor's dues, with costs, were tendered at any time before the date fixed for sale. The Court quoted that version because the events pre-dated the amendment, and it noted that the thirty-day notice under Rules 8(6) and 9(1) exists to give the borrower "a real and final opportunity to redeem" under Section 13(8) (para 30).

The Court treated the Rules as a code. It cited the old maxim that where a statute requires a thing to be done in a particular manner it must be done in that manner or not at all (para 29), and tied it to the constitutional justification for the Act. The safeguards, it said, were "the very condition upon which the power exists", a reference to *Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311, which upheld the Act's validity partly because of the borrower protections built into it. Rules 8 and 9 are, on this view, not directory machinery but the constitutional price of extra-judicial enforcement.

### 3.2 The finality doctrine reframed

The most doctrinally significant passage is paragraph 33. Purchasers at enforcement sales have long invoked decisions such as *Valji Khimji and Company v. Official Liquidator of Hindustan Nitro Product (Gujarat) Limited* (2008) 9 SCC 299 and *Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) (P) Ltd.* (2024) 2 SCC 1, which caution against lightly unsettling confirmed sales. The Court accepted the principle but disciplined its use. Relying on *Divya Manufacturing Co. Pvt. Ltd. v. Union Bank of India* (2000) 6 SCC 69 and *J. Rajiv Subramaniyan v. Pandiyas* (2014) 5 SCC 651, it held that the same line of authority recognises that a sale "vitiated by material irregularity, fraud, or non-compliance with the mandatory statutory procedure" does not attract that protection.

This is less a departure than a clarification of what the cases always meant. Finality rules exist to protect bidders who participate in sales that look regular; they were not designed to launder illegality. The reasoning is persuasive, but it exposes a tension. A bidder cannot ordinarily inspect the lender's internal file, so a rule that unwinds sales for process defects shifts diligence onto bidders and, in cases like this one, onto a purchaser who was arguably blameless for some of the defects. The Court answered this partly on the facts. The purchaser here was not an innocent stranger: the firm was the nominee of the declared bidder, it was created on the day bids opened, and it was refunded in full with interest within months. The reasoning therefore rests on features that will not be present in every challenge, and later benches will have to decide how far paragraph 33 reaches when the purchaser is genuinely uninvolved in the defect.

### 3.3 Restraint orders and the thirty-day clock

The treatment of the DRAT order is the most practically important element. The Court held that soliciting and receiving a bid is itself a "step" under the SARFAESI Act. This reads a restraint order generously, and rightly so. A restraint against "proceeding further" would be hollow if the creditor could continue gathering bids and merely defer opening them; the bidder's commitment, earnest money and the creditor's own expectations all build momentum towards a sale. The Court's refusal to treat non-opening as a cure follows from the settled rule, which it cited through *Surjit Singh v. Harbans Singh* (1995) 6 SCC 50 and *Delhi Development Authority v. Skipper Construction Co. Pvt. Ltd.* (1996) 4 SCC 622, that acts done in violation of an order of a court or tribunal are without legal effect.

Equally important is the observation that the later High Court judgment did not retrospectively validate the earlier acts. A reversal of a restraint operates prospectively. A creditor who acts during the restraint, hoping to be vindicated on appeal, bears the risk of that gamble.

The exclusion of the restraint period from the thirty days is a sensible application of *actus curiae neminem gravabit*, but it raises a drafting question the Court did not address. The arithmetic assumes the notice period was suspended and then resumed, leaving seventeen days. The Court did not consider whether a fresh notice should instead have been required after so long an interregnum, a position the borrower's counsel urged. Paragraph 31.3, which treats the absence of any notice of the bid opening as an independent infirmity, suggests the Court would have been receptive. A cautious creditor will treat any restraint of material duration as a reason to re-notify.

### 3.4 The nominee problem

The fourth infirmity is the cleanest in law. Rule 9(2) ties confirmation to the person who "has offered the highest sale price", and Rule 9(6) ties the certificate to the purchaser. Nothing authorises substitution after the fact. The Court added that an entity "which did not exist when bids were invited and received could neither have been assessed for eligibility nor have participated in the auction", and invoked the principle that one may not do indirectly what cannot be done directly.

This passage has a wider reach than SARFAESI. Nomination of a bidder's assignee is common in commercial auctions, and some tender documents expressly permit it. The Court's emphasis on the text of this auction notice, whose clause 2.1 defined the "Purchaser" as the successful bidder and whose clause 3.1 prescribed eligibility, means the holding turns on the instrument. Where the notice permits nomination within stated limits, the analysis may differ. Without an express provision, however, a nominee certificate is vulnerable.

### 3.5 What the Court left undecided

Paragraph 35 deserves close reading. The Court recorded that the purchaser's argument on the officer's lack of power to cancel a sale certificate had been advanced "with some force", and that the borrower had relied on Section 21 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, which allows a power to issue an instrument to be exercised to rescind it. It also noted the debate on registration and on the extinguishment of the right of redemption. It decided none of these.

That restraint is defensible, because the illegality of the auction disposed of the case. But the High Court's findings on these points were set aside along with its judgment, and nothing in the Supreme Court's reasoning endorses them. Practitioners should therefore not assume that an Authorised Officer may cancel a certificate unilaterally; the question remains open, and the safer course for a creditor who discovers a defect is to approach the Debts Recovery Tribunal. The registration question, which the High Court answered by reference to Section 17(2)(xii) of the Registration Act, 1908, also remains unsettled in this litigation. The High Court's direction that officers disclose stamp duty liability in the sale notice is attractive as practice, but it too fell with the judgment.

## 4. Practical Significance

**For secured creditors and authorised officers.** The judgment is a compliance checklist. Obtain a valuation and fix a reserve price before sale. Serve the thirty-day notice and publish the public notice. Hold the sale only after the period expires. Maintain contemporaneous records of bids and any inter se bidding, since the Court drew an adverse inference from IFCI's failure to produce the original file. Treat a tribunal or court restraint as suspending the entire process, including the receipt of bids, and re-notify the borrower before reviving a stalled sale. Document the mode of sale consistently from the outset; describing the same sale as private treaty and then as tender, as IFCI did, is damaging.

**For auction purchasers.** Diligence must extend beyond the property to the process. Before bidding, check whether any restraint order is in force in the borrower's pending proceedings, and whether the public notice and borrower notice were duly served. Bid in the name of the entity that will take title. A bidder who intends to take title through a company or firm should ensure that the entity exists and is registered before the bid is submitted, and should confirm that the notice permits it to participate. Counsel should also keep in mind that a sale certificate is not a safe harbour: the judgment shows that a certificate can be quashed years after issue.

**For borrowers.** The judgment confirms that the thirty-day notice has substantive value, since it is the borrower's window to redeem. A borrower facing enforcement should record any restraint orders and the dates on which they take effect and lapse, and should write to the Authorised Officer asserting that the notice period is suspended. Where dues are cleared, as here, the borrower's position is strengthened if possession has been retained and the certificate remains unregistered. The result does not mean that every defect will unravel a sale. The borrower here succeeded with multiple, independently serious defects and a full repayment.

**For litigators.** Marshal the documentary record early: the public notice, bid forms, dates of restraint orders and the creditor's own communications. Here the Court relied on IFCI's letter of 8 February 2012 to establish the nominee point, even though the purchaser said it had not been argued before the High Court. Defendants should not rely on the finality maxim alone.

**Precedential weight.** The judgment is non-reportable, and it applies settled principles to an unusual chronology. It will be cited, but a court may distinguish it as fact-specific. Its value lies in the clarity of its reasoning on the five infirmities and on the proposition that finality follows legality.

## 5. Conclusion

*Sterling Holiday Resorts* does not announce a new doctrine. It applies the established view that enforcement sales under the SARFAESI Act must follow Rules 8 and 9, and it refuses to let the sanctity of auction sales shield a process that broke the rules at several points. Its clearest contributions are the holding that receiving bids is a prohibited step during a restraint, the exclusion of the restraint period from the thirty-day clock, and the rejection of a nominee certificate issued to a firm that neither bid nor existed on the auction date.

The decision is notable as much for what it leaves open. Whether an Authorised Officer may cancel a sale certificate, whether the certificate must be registered, and when exactly redemption ends under the pre-2016 Section 13(8) will await another case. The judgment applies Section 13(8) as it stood before the 2016 substitution and says nothing on how the current text would operate on comparable facts, so sales conducted after 1 September 2016 will need separate analysis.

The broader lesson is about risk allocation. Creditors cannot rely on speed, purchasers cannot rely on certificates, and both must treat the procedural record as part of the title. The Court's formulation, that sanctity is the reward of legality, will likely be quoted often. Its force will depend on how future benches handle the harder case, in which a genuinely innocent purchaser acquires title through a sale that the lender, and not the purchaser, has tainted.

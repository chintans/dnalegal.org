**Quick answer:** In *Manjit Singh & Anr. v. Nirmal & Ors.*, decided on 16 September 2026, the Delhi High Court dismissed a first appeal against a trial court decree that had rejected a suit for partition. Two sons claiming their late father's estate had been out of possession of the disputed plot since 2003, when their father's second wife, acting under a registered power of attorney, sold it by a registered agreement to sell to a third party who paid full consideration and took possession. The sons could not prove the power of attorney was forged, and having admittedly lost possession, could not maintain a suit for partition that never asked for possession to be restored.

## 1. Introduction

Family property disputes in India routinely combine two different questions: who, as a matter of succession law, is entitled to inherit, and who, as a matter of procedure, is entitled to bring a particular suit at all. The second question is often decisive well before the first is reached, because Indian civil procedure treats possession as a threshold condition for certain reliefs rather than a fact established only after title is decided. A suit for partition is a recurring example: it proceeds on the assumption that the plaintiff already holds, or is deemed in law to hold, some possession over the property to be divided, and it can fail on that assumption alone, whatever the strength of the plaintiff's underlying claim to a share.

On 16 September 2026, the Delhi High Court applied that principle to a dispute stretching back more than two decades. Two sons of a man who died in 2003 sued for partition of what they said was his intestate estate, including a plot in Surya Niketan, Delhi. Their father's second wife and her son resisted the claim, producing a Will in the wife's favour, a registered power of attorney, and a registered agreement to sell under which the wife, acting as attorney, had already sold the plot to an unrelated buyer before the father's death was even relevant to the analysis. The trial court dismissed the suit, and the sons' appeal — by the time it was finally decided, narrowed to the one plot alone — met the same result.

This article sets out the background to that decision, the two doctrinal planks on which it rests — the evidentiary presumption attaching to registered instruments, and the possession-based maintainability of a partition suit — and what each means for anyone drafting, defending or advising on a family property dispute where a co-parcener's share has, in fact if not in law, already been sold to someone outside the family.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

Late S. Swaran Singh had two sons, Manjit Singh and Harjinder Singh, from his first marriage to Swinder Kaur, which was dissolved in 1961. He subsequently married Nirmal (defendant no. 1), with whom he had a son, Ravinder Singh (defendant no. 2). Swaran Singh died on 20 June 2003. Manjit Singh and Harjinder Singh, as the appellants, filed a suit for partition of his estate, asserting that he had died intestate and that they, along with Nirmal and Ravinder Singh, were his legal heirs. The suit concerned two properties: a flat at Shakuntala Apartments, Nehru Place ("the Nehru Place Property"), and Plot No. 27, Surya Niketan, Delhi, admeasuring 330 square yards ("the Surya Niketan Property").

Nirmal and Ravinder Singh's defence, set out in their written statement, was threefold. First, Swaran Singh had executed a Will dated 8 May 2002 bequeathing all his movable and immovable property, including both suit properties, to Nirmal. Second, the Nehru Place Property had already been agreed to be sold, by an Agreement to Sell dated 9 October 2001, to Kapil Kumar Chopra and Narinder Pal Singh, who had been put in possession. Third, Swaran Singh had executed a registered General Power of Attorney ("GPA") dated 1 February 2003 in Nirmal's favour over the Surya Niketan Property, under which Nirmal, as his attorney, had executed a registered Agreement to Sell dated 26 May 2003 in favour of Shobha Sharma, who had also been put in possession. The plaintiffs amended their suit to implead Shobha Sharma, Kapil Kumar Chopra and Narinder Pal Singh as defendants, and sought cancellation of the Will, both agreements to sell and the GPA, alleging that Swaran Singh's signatures and thumb impressions on each were forged.

The trial court framed eight issues, the most consequential of which asked whether the plaintiffs were in actual physical possession of any part of the suit properties at the time the suit was filed, whether the GPA was valid, and whether Shobha Sharma was a bona fide purchaser of the Surya Niketan Property. On the evidence, the trial court held that the Will had not been duly proved, but that the Nehru Place Agreement to Sell, the GPA and the Surya Niketan Agreement to Sell had all been validly executed, that full consideration had passed under each, and that possession of both properties had passed to the respective purchasers before Swaran Singh's death — meaning he held no right, title or interest in either property when he died. Since the plaintiffs were consequently neither in actual nor constructive possession, and had not sought a decree for possession, the trial court held the suit for partition not maintainable as framed, and dismissed it on 11 July 2017.

On appeal, the plaintiffs confined their challenge to the Surya Niketan Property, expressly abandoning any claim to the Nehru Place Property. Because the trial court had found Shobha Sharma in possession of the Surya Niketan Property, the High Court held in 2017 that the appeal had to be valued, for court-fee purposes, on the market value of the property as a claim for possession — not merely as an undervalued partition suit — and the appellants duly paid the additional fee. They then applied in 2018 to amend the plaint to formally add a prayer for possession, but withdrew that application in March 2026, telling the court they believed they remained in constructive possession and so did not need to seek it. Justice Amit Bansal, who reserved judgment on 13 August 2026 and delivered it on 16 September 2026, rejected that position, upheld the trial court's findings in full, and dismissed the appeal.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The evidentiary weight of a registered document

The appellants' central substantive case was that the GPA and the second Agreement to Sell were forgeries — that Swaran Singh's thumb impressions on the GPA were not his, and that his advanced age and hospitalisation in 2002–2003 made execution of either document implausible. The Court's analysis of this claim turns entirely on the evidentiary consequence of registration. Both the GPA and the Agreement to Sell had been registered before the concerned Sub-Registrars, and the defendants proved that registration by summoning and examining the Lower Division Clerks who produced the original documents from the Sub-Registrars' offices at Seelampur and Geeta Colony. An attesting witness to the GPA, cross-examined without being discredited, identified Swaran Singh's thumb impressions and his own signature as an attesting witness before the Sub-Registrar.

Against that record, the Court invoked the Supreme Court's holding in *Prem Singh v. Birbal*, (2006) 5 SCC 353, that "there is a presumption that a registered document is validly executed," that such a document is "prima facie ... valid in law," and that "the onus of proof ... would be on a person who leads evidence to rebut the presumption." Applying that allocation of the burden, the Court found the appellants had not discharged it. Their principal evidence — a handwriting expert's report comparing thumb impressions — was already disbelieved by the trial court because the comparison had been made from "a dim and light photocopy" and identified no specific markers of forgery, a finding the High Court did not disturb. Their medical evidence fared no better on close reading: a discharge summary from January 2003 showed Swaran Singh was in "satisfactory condition" on discharge, which did nothing to suggest he could not visit a Sub-Registrar's office the following month, and letters the appellants themselves had obtained under the Right to Information Act and put to a defence witness in cross-examination showed Swaran Singh was of sound mind through the period the second Agreement to Sell was executed. The Court also drew a sharper doctrinal point from the structure of the transaction itself: because the Agreement to Sell was executed by Nirmal as Swaran Singh's duly constituted attorney under a validly registered GPA, his own hospitalisation at the time was legally irrelevant to its execution — an attorney's authority to act does not lapse merely because the principal is unwell, and there was accordingly "no requirement for the buyer ... to meet Late S. Swaran Singh" at all.

The reasoning is unremarkable as doctrine — the presumption of due execution attaching to registered documents is long settled — but the case illustrates how thin an evidentiary challenge to a registered instrument typically is if it rests on inference (advanced age, illness, family suspicion) rather than direct proof that the document was falsified. A forgery allegation requires evidence, ideally an expert opinion based on originals rather than photocopies and medical evidence that speaks to incapacity at the precise moment of execution, capable of actually displacing the statutory presumption.

### 3.2 Possession as a threshold for partition, not an afterthought

The second and more procedurally significant strand of the judgment concerns why the appellants' suit failed even independently of the forgery allegations: they had, as a matter of admitted fact, no possession of the Surya Niketan Property to divide. The Court's reasoning proceeds from a doctrine explained in an earlier order in the same appeal (quoted in the judgment) about the ordinary rule that a co-owner's suit for partition need not be valued, for court-fee purposes, at the market value of the property, because "every co-owner is deemed to be in possession for and on behalf of all the co-owners" — the plaintiff is treated as being in constructive possession even without physical occupation. But that presumption, the order continued, "extinguishes and comes to an end when the physical possession of the suit property is no longer with one or more family members," at which point a suit for partition or declaration cannot be maintained "without the plaintiff in the suit seeking possession of the suit property and paying court fees as per the market value of the suit property."

Once the trial court found — and the High Court affirmed — that Shobha Sharma, a stranger to the family, held registered documents of title and had been put in physical possession of the Surya Niketan Property in 2003, the presumption of constructive possession in the plaintiffs' favour was displaced. The plaintiffs' own conduct compounded the difficulty: having initially amended their appeal valuation to reflect a claim for possession, they then applied in 2018 to add a formal prayer for possession to the plaint, only to withdraw that application in 2026 on the stated ground that they believed themselves still in constructive possession. The Court treated this as simply incorrect on the facts already found, holding that "it is manifest that plaintiffs were not in actual or constructive possession of Surya Niketan Property at the time of institution of the suit," and that "in the absence of possession and without seeking the relief of possession, the suit for partition was not maintainable as framed."

For this proposition, the judgment relies on this Court's own precedent in *Manmohan Batra v. Bharat Bhushan Batra*, 2012 SCC OnLine Del 2663, which held that "a suit for partition only lies when the plaintiff is either in physical possession of the property or in constructive possession of the property," and that once the defendant's documents show possession was handed over to a third party, "ouster was clearly pleaded and it was incumbent on the appellant/plaintiff to amend his plaint to seek the relief of possession." Batra went further, on its own facts, to hold that a belated attempt to add such a relief would in any event be time-barred under Article 65 and Section 27 of the Limitation Act, 1963. That limitation reasoning belongs to Batra's own facts, where ouster was traced to a specific date in 1996; the present judgment does not undertake an equivalent limitation computation of its own, since the appeal was disposed of on the narrower ground that the suit as framed omitted the relief of possession altogether.

### 3.3 The bona fide purchaser's position, reinforced rather than newly stated

Although the Court did not frame its conclusion in these terms, upholding both the GPA's validity and the possession-based bar to partition had the practical effect of insulating Shobha Sharma's purchase on facts that made out a conventional bona fide purchaser defence: a registered instrument of transfer, consideration recorded as fully paid and independently traceable through a banker's cheque deposited into the seller's own bank account, and corroborated possession, evidenced by a letter from the housing society acknowledging her as the person paying its security and maintenance charges. None of these elements is individually novel, but together they form a compact template of what a purchaser will need to show, years later, to resist a challenge from a seller's disappointed heirs: not merely a registered document, but proof that money actually moved and possession was actually taken.

### 3.4 What the judgment leaves undecided

Two points are worth flagging as outside the judgment's actual holding. First, because the trial court found the Will unproved, this appeal did not turn on, and the High Court did not need to revisit, any question of testamentary succession; the case was decided entirely on the antecedent question of whether Swaran Singh held any interest in the Surya Niketan Property to pass on at all once the GPA and Agreement to Sell were found valid. Second, the appeal's scope was itself narrowed by the appellants' own concession in 2017 that they no longer claimed any interest in the Nehru Place Property, so nothing in this judgment should be read as a fresh appellate finding on that property; the High Court's disposal addresses the Surya Niketan Property alone.

## 4. Practical Significance

For litigators drafting a partition suit on behalf of an heir who suspects, or knows, that a family member has already parted with possession to a third party, this judgment is a pleading checklist: verify at the outset whether the plaintiff can plausibly claim actual or constructive possession, and if a third party holds possession under a registered document, plead and value the suit for possession as well as partition from the start. Waiting to add that relief by amendment — as the appellants here first attempted and then abandoned — risks a substantive dismissal, as this case shows, if the amendment is not pursued to completion.

For conveyancers and prospective purchasers, particularly where a seller acts under a power of attorney rather than executing a sale deed personally, the judgment usefully sets out what a court will look for years later if the transaction is challenged by the principal's heirs: registration of both the power of attorney and any subsequent agreement to sell before the appropriate Sub-Registrar; consideration paid through a traceable instrument credited to the seller's own account, supported by bank records; and documentary evidence of possession maintained after the transaction, such as society or utility records in the purchaser's name. Buyers relying on a power of attorney should retain and preserve exactly this class of record, since the burden of proving a negative — that the transaction did not happen as the documents record — will fall on whoever challenges it later, not on the purchaser.

For advocates instructed to challenge a registered instrument on the ground of forgery or the executant's incapacity, the judgment is a caution against evidence that gestures at doubt without displacing the statutory presumption. A handwriting expert's opinion needs originals or high-quality reproductions and specific, articulated markers of forgery, not general reservations about a photocopy; and medical records offered to suggest incapacity must actually speak to the executant's condition on the date of execution, not merely to a period of illness that surrounds it. Advocates should also anticipate the attorney-execution point: where a document is executed by a power-of-attorney holder rather than the principal, the principal's contemporaneous ill health will ordinarily be irrelevant unless the challenge is instead to the validity of the power of attorney itself.

For revenue and registration authorities, and for anyone advising on how a registered document's authenticity is proved in later litigation, the case again confirms that producing the registering official as a witness, together with the original register entry, remains the standard and generally decisive route to establishing due execution once a challenge is raised.

## 5. Conclusion

*Manjit Singh v. Nirmal* does not add a new principle to Indian property law; it applies two settled ones — the evidentiary presumption favouring a registered instrument, and the possession-based maintainability of a suit for partition — to a family dispute where both cut decisively against the plaintiffs. What makes the judgment worth reading is less its doctrine than its illustration of how these two strands interact in practice: an heir who cannot rebut the presumption attaching to a power of attorney and a subsequent registered sale will, in the ordinary case, also be unable to show the possession a partition suit requires, because the very documents that defeat the forgery challenge are usually the same documents that record the transfer of possession to a third party. For practitioners advising on family property disputes involving power-of-attorney sales, the lesson is to treat the possession question and the title question as inseparable from the moment a suit is drafted, rather than as sequential hurdles to be addressed one at a time.

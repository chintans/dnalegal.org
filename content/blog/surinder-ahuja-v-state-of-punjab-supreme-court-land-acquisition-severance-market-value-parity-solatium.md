**Quick answer:** In *Surinder Ahuja v. State of Punjab*, decided on 29 September 2026 (2026 INSC 1068), a Bench of Dipankar Datta and Sheel Nagu, JJ. disposed of 47 connected appeals on compensation for land acquired in Fazilka, Punjab, under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. The Court restored a uniform market value of Rs. 5,00,000 an acre, set aside the High Court's distance-based valuation model, restored severance damages of 50 per cent calculated on the value of the land left behind, and held that solatium and additional interest attach only to the market value of the land. It confined the claim for fruit trees to the 280 trees pleaded and fixed tubewell compensation at Rs. 50,000 each. The judgment is marked reportable.

## 1. Introduction

*Surinder Ahuja* resolves 47 appeals at once, and could be dismissed as a fact-bound correction of the High Court's arithmetic. It does three things beyond arithmetic. It treats the valuation of a long linear project as a question of parity across contiguous land, and rejects a formula that priced each village by its distance from town and border. It moves the baseline for severance damages from the acquired strip to the land that was cut off. And it restates, against a High Court decision that had treated trees as "land", that solatium and additional interest are tied to the first limb of Section 23(1).

It also holds that a claim cannot exceed the reference petition, and that a private valuer's report is not proved by being marked as an exhibit.

This article sets out the facts and the five issues the Court framed, analyses its reasoning on each, identifies the questions the judgment leaves open, and ends with what claimants, the State and their advisers should take from it.

## 2. Case summary

### 2.1 Background

The acquisition was for the Aspal Extension Drain, an 18.42-kilometre channel of 264 to 340 feet in width through six villages in Fazilka district: Karni Khera, Odian, Awa, Kotha, Alamshah and Salem Shah. Notifications under Section 4 of the 1894 Act issued on 14 and 24 November 2000, the Section 6 declaration followed on 2 March 2001, and the Collector made his award on 6 August 2001 (para 1.3).

The Collector fixed a single rate of Rs. 2,25,000 an acre for irrigated land across all six villages, on the basis of a departmental committee's recommendation, gave nothing for severance, and awarded solatium at 30 per cent on market value (paras 3.1A and 3.1D). The landowners sought references under Section 18. Between 2006 and 2008 the Reference Court enhanced the rate to Rs. 5,00,000 for Karni Khera, Odian and Alamshah, to Rs. 3,00,000 for highway-facing and Rs. 2,56,000 for interior land in Awa, and to Rs. 2,56,000 in Kotha. It left Salem Shah at Rs. 2,25,000 (para 3.2A). It also awarded severance, compensation for 946 trees in Karni Khera, Rs. 1,00,000 for each tubewell in some villages, and solatium on the trees and structures as well as on the land.

By judgment of 29 March 2019, the Punjab and Haryana High Court disposed of 195 appeals and five cross-objections, of which 47 came to the Supreme Court. The Court reworked the valuation on a "hub-and-spoke" model, treating Awa and Kotha as the centre and stepping the rate down by Rs. 20,000 an acre for each village further away, with a further allowance for proximity to the international border. It reduced tree compensation to 280 trees, fixed severance at 40 per cent of the value of the acquired land, and held that solatium and additional interest were payable on the land value alone (para 3.3).

### 2.2 The five issues and the holdings

The Court framed five issues and decided them as follows (paras 4 and 11):

| Issue | Supreme Court's answer |
|---|---|
| Market value | High Court's radial model set aside; uniform Rs. 5,00,000 an acre restored |
| Fruit trees | Compensation confined to the 280 trees pleaded: Rs. 5,38,300 |
| Severance | 40 per cent of acquired land value set aside; 50 per cent of the value of the unacquired remaining land restored |
| Solatium and additional interest | Payable only on the Section 23(1) first-factor market value; not on trees, severance, tubewells or structures |
| Tubewells | Rs. 50,000 flat for each tubewell, with no statutory additions |

The Court directed that additional compensation be paid within six months of communication of the order and that any excess recoverable from a landowner be recovered only after written notice and an opportunity to be heard, within nine months (paras 11.6 and 11.7).

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Market value: project-wide parity over a distance formula

Section 23(1) of the 1894 Act directs the court first to the market value of the land on the date of the Section 4 notification. The Court read that as requiring it to weigh positive potential, such as nearness to urban centres, highways and institutions, and negative factors, while giving contiguous land acquired for one purpose consistent compensation (para 5.1).

Applying that standard, the Court found the High Court's reasons unsustainable on four grounds.

First, the High Court had discarded the 1996 Sultanpur benchmark, an award of Rs. 5,00,000 an acre for land taken for a sludge carrier on the same road, because Sultanpur lay within Fazilka's municipal limits. The Supreme Court held that this ignored the principle of parity. The land was about 1.5 kilometres away on the same road and acquired for an identical drainage purpose, and the State's own Sub-Divisional Officer (RW-1) had admitted that the town's built-up area was expanding towards it (paras 5.2.2 and 5.2.3). It relied on *Ali Mohammad Beigh v. State of Jammu and Kashmir*, (2017) 4 SCC 717, and *Union of India v. Harinder Pal Singh*, (2005) 12 SCC 564, which approved a uniform rate across five villages that could be treated as one unit.

Second, it held that the 20 per cent "smallness" cut applied to the Karni Khera sale exemplar overlooked the highway frontage and nearby institutions, citing *Udho Dass v. State of Haryana*, (2010) 12 SCC 51, and *Bhagwathula Samanna v. Special Tahsildar*, AIR 1992 SC 666, on when deductions for development are inappropriate (para 5.3).

Third, the radial model displaced local transaction evidence. The Registration Clerk (PW-2) had proved that the villages were classified as "Special Villages" within five kilometres of the municipal limits, attracting a 9 per cent urban stamp duty rate rather than the 5 to 6 per cent rural rate (paras 3.2A and 5.4.2). The Court treated this as evidence of suburban potential that an abstract formula could not displace.

Fourth, the model produced anomalies. Village Sabuana, 10 kilometres from the border, was priced at Rs. 2,79,000 an acre while Alamshah, 1.5 kilometres from it, was priced at Rs. 3,19,000 (para 5.4.5). The Court also invoked *Krishan Kumar v. State of Haryana*, 2025 INSC 638, for the principle that land of similar locational and developmental potential must be compensated equitably unless clear, objective distinctions justify otherwise (para 5.4.3).

The reasoning is persuasive on the evidence. Its weaker point is the Court's treatment of *Manoj Kumar v. State of Haryana*, (2018) 2 RCR (Civil) 815. The Court quotes a passage in which that decision cautions against following other awards blindly and observes that land value varies with distance and that highway land may fetch more than interior land. It then calls the High Court's reliance on *Manoj Kumar* and on *General Manager, ONGC Ltd. v. Rameshbhai Jivanbhai Patel*, (2008) 14 SCC 745, "misplaced" without explaining why (para 5.4.4). The quoted passage, taken alone, supports the High Court's instinct that distance matters. The judgment's real answer is evidential: on this record, the distance difference was not borne out. The judgment would have been clearer had it said so. Nor does it decide whether proximity to an international border can ever be a legitimate negative factor. Para 5.1 accepts that negative factors count, and para 5.4.5 criticises only the way the High Court's formula worked.

A further point needs care. The judgment restores Rs. 5,00,000 an acre for all villages in the 47 appeals (paras 5.4.6 and 11.1). Its own table records that the Reference Court had awarded less in Awa, Kotha and Salem Shah. Whether the claimants there had themselves sought, and were entitled to, the higher rate is not explained in the judgment, and a practitioner relying on the case for uniformity across a whole project should read the operative direction against the record of each appeal.

### 3.2 Pleadings: the reference petition is the plaint

On the trees, the Court agreed with the High Court. A reference under Section 18 stands as the plaint, and the landowners' petition of 20 September 2002 claimed orchard damage only for 280 trees on the acquired strip, 246 Malta orange and 34 guava. They then led evidence that a further 482 Malta trees on unacquired land died in 2005 after the drip irrigation system was disrupted, but never amended the petition. Relying on *Bachhaj Nahar v. Nilima Mandal*, (2008) 17 SCC 491, the Court held that a court cannot decide an issue that does not arise from the pleadings. Compensation was therefore restricted to 280 trees at Rs. 1,922.50 each, a total of Rs. 5,38,300 (paras 6.2 and 6.3).

This holding is orthodox. As our own observation, and not a point the judgment addresses, the Court treated the 2005 loss as a claim for trees. It did not consider whether damage to the drip system could have been pleaded as damage from severance or as a consequence of the acquisition. The lesson is that however the claim is framed, it must be framed in the reference petition.

### 3.3 Severance: the baseline is the land left behind

The most significant doctrinal correction concerns severance. Section 23(1) "Thirdly" compensates damage sustained by the person interested "by reason of severing such land from his other land". The High Court had computed 40 per cent of the value of the land acquired, applying clause (2) of the formula in *Tehal Singh v. State of Punjab*, 1987 RRR 495.

The Supreme Court held that this shifted the baseline to the wrong land. For the lead claimant, Surinder Ahuja, 79 kanals 2 marlas were taken as a strip, leaving 249 kanals 17 marlas on the other side. A percentage of the strip's value, it held, left the landowner uncompensated for the loss of utility across the much larger remainder, contrary to *Tribeni Devi v. Collector of Ranchi*, (1972) 1 SCC 480 (para 7.3).

It also held that the High Court had applied *Tehal Singh* as a geometric rule of thumb keyed to the village abadi, without regard to the physical facts. The State's witness had admitted that the drain was about 100 feet wide and 10 to 15 feet deep, and the High Court itself recorded that the nearest bridges were two kilometres apart and one at Odian was seven feet wide, with a capacity of nine tonnes, so that combine harvesters could not cross (paras 7.4 and 7.5). It cited *Walchandnagar Industries Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra*, (2022) 5 SCC 71, for the proposition that compensation follows the actual loss of utility of what remains (para 7.6).

The reasoning on the baseline is sound as a matter of statutory language. The third factor speaks of damage by severance of land from other land, and the loss is naturally measured on the land that suffers it. Two features of the disposal deserve scrutiny. The Court restored the Reference Court's 50 per cent "across all villages under challenge" (para 7.9), though the Reference Court had awarded 10 per cent in Awa, Kotha and Salem Shah on the strength of the same SDO evidence, and the judgment does not explain why a uniform 50 per cent is right for each. And the judgment reaches the result partly by invoking the constitutional parity principle of *Krishan Kumar* and project-wide parity. Parity is a sound principle for market value, where the question is what comparable land would fetch. Severance damage depends on how each holding was cut, and a parity rationale fits it less comfortably.

### 3.4 Solatium and additional interest: tied to the first factor

The Reference Court had awarded solatium and additional interest on trees and structures, reasoning from the Punjab and Haryana decision in *Improvement Trust, Jind v. Narinder Kumar*, 1990 (1) RRR 437, that trees fall within "land" under Section 3(a). The Supreme Court held that this contradicts *State of Punjab v. Amarjit Singh*, (2011) 4 SCC 734, which it quotes as holding that the additional amount under Section 23(1A) and solatium under Section 23(2) are payable only on the Section 23(1) market value and not on damages or expenses under the second to sixth factors (para 8.2). Trees fall under the second factor, severance under the third, and tubewells and kothas under the fourth or fifth (para 8.3). A High Court decision cannot prevail over *Amarjit Singh* (para 8.5).

This is a straightforward application of binding authority and the structure of Section 23. The landowners' argument had intuitive force, since a standing orchard is a major part of the value of the land, but the statute lists separate heads and attaches solatium to one of them. The decision is confined to the 1894 Act. The judgment does not discuss the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, and nothing in it should be read as deciding how compensation components are computed under that statute.

### 3.5 Tubewells and proof of documents

The Court upheld the High Court's flat Rs. 50,000 for each tubewell and rejected the Reference Court's Rs. 1,00,000. The private valuation reports on which the higher figure rested had been exhibited, but their author, an engineer, was never examined. Citing *S. Sangeetha v. TMT P. Ponni*, 2026 INSC 813, and *Life Insurance Corporation of India v. Ram Pal Singh Bisen*, (2010) 4 SCC 491, the Court held that marking a document does not prove its contents (paras 9.2 and 9.3). It added that motors, engines and pumps are movable and can be reused on the remaining holding, and that a flat figure "appropriately balances private inflation against government underestimation" (para 9.4).

The evidentiary point is clear. The flat figure is a less rigorous exercise. The Court was strict with the claimants' evidence and then accepted a round number as a realistic figure.

### 3.6 Article 136 and questions left open

The judgment closes by describing interference under Article 136 of the Constitution as "extremely limited and restrictive", while stating that the Court has "attempted to remove injustice or institutional high-handedness wherever it was found" (paras 10.1 and 10.2). It interfered on the two issues with the greatest financial effect, market value and severance.

Several questions remain open. Whether border proximity can lawfully depress market value, and on what evidence, is not decided. The tension between project-wide parity and the statement in *Manoj Kumar* that other awards are not binding precedents is not reconciled. The basis for a uniform severance percentage across differently affected holdings is not explained. And the position of the three villages where the Reference Court awarded less than the restored rates is not addressed.

## 4. Practical significance

**For claimants.** The judgment rewards the claimant who builds the record. In a reference proceeding the petition defines the claim, so every head of loss, including losses on retained land, must be pleaded at the outset or by timely amendment. A private valuer's report must be proved through the valuer. For market value, the evidence that carried the day was concrete and official: a prior award on the same road, a Collector's classification of villages as "Special Villages", the higher stamp duty rate that followed, and an admission by the State's own officer that the town was growing towards the land.

**For severance claims.** Claimants should plead and prove the physical effect of the work on the retained land: width and depth of the channel, bank height, the location and load capacity of crossings, and the machinery used on the land. The measure is the diminution in value of the remaining land, and the percentage claimed should be tied to that evidence. Formulae from earlier cases, such as that in *Tehal Singh*, were treated here as guides subordinate to the facts.

**For the State and acquiring bodies.** Linear projects present the risk that a single benchmark award, once established, will be extended along the whole alignment. Departments defending references should lead evidence on genuine differences between stretches, since a distance formula will not substitute for it. Severance exposure is now measured against the remaining holding, which is often far larger than the strip taken.

**For advisers on solatium and interest.** The judgment confirms that the statutory additions are limited to land value under the 1894 Act. Claimants who include trees, structures and severance in the base are likely to fail. The Court's direction on recovery, which requires notice and an opportunity to be heard within nine months, shows that any excess is to be recovered by process and not by deduction (para 11.7).

## 5. Conclusion

*Surinder Ahuja* clarifies principle in two places and applies settled law in the rest. On market value, the Court will not allow a project-wide acquisition to be valued by a formula that creates differences between neighbouring parcels that the evidence does not support. On severance, the measure is the harm to the land left behind, not a percentage of the strip taken. On solatium, trees, tubewells and pleadings, it reaffirms existing authority.

The reasoning is at its best where it follows the record and weaker where it rests on a broad appeal to constitutional parity. Claimants should plead and prove every head, and acquiring bodies should evidence real differences between stretches of land.

**Quick answer:** In *John Mathew v. Santha Paul & Ors.*, decided on 21 September 2026, the Supreme Court set aside more than a decade of rulings holding a title-and-possession suit time-barred, because the trial court had severed the limitation issue for "preliminary" determination only after the plaintiff had already led his evidence and the defendants had entered upon theirs. Order XIV Rule 2(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 permits a qualifying issue of law to be tried "first" only prospectively, before a composite trial of the framed issues has begun; that power lapses once evidence has started, whatever stage the framing had reached. The suit, arising from a sale deed executed by a power-of-attorney holder, returns to trial with the underlying limitation question — three years to cancel a void instrument, or twelve to recover possession — left open.

## 1. Introduction

A suit filed in 2013 over the sale of agricultural land in Kottarakara, Kerala, took thirteen years and four judicial forums to reach the point where a court would finally decide, on the merits, what the parties' rights actually were. What delayed it was not the underlying dispute — whether a power-of-attorney holder had authority to sell — but a procedural shortcut: an application, filed midway through trial, asking the court to decide limitation "as a preliminary issue" under Order XIV Rule 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 ("the Code"). The trial court granted it and dismissed the suit; the first appellate court affirmed, recharacterising the dismissal as a rejection of the plaint; the Kerala High Court then reversed both and held the suit within limitation — without ever confronting whether the "preliminary issue" route had been open to the trial court at all, given that evidence-in-chief was already underway when the application was moved.

*John Mathew v. Santha Paul & Ors.* answers that anterior procedural question, filling a genuine gap: at what point does a court lose the power to sever an issue of law for prior trial under Order XIV Rule 2(2)? The Bench of K.V. Viswanathan and Arun Palli, JJ. held that the power is exhausted not when all issues have been framed, and not even when the suit has been listed for evidence, but only once the court has actually embarked on a composite trial of the issues generally. Framing every issue at the outset does not foreclose a later application to try one of them — typically limitation or jurisdiction — first; what forecloses it is evidence having already begun on the issues as a whole.

This article sets out the facts, the doctrinal architecture the Court built around Order XIV Rule 2, and what the ruling means for practitioners handling title suits that turn on the validity of a sale executed by a power-of-attorney holder.

## 2. Case summary and background

The plaintiff, T. Oommen Panicker, executed a General Power of Attorney ("GPA") on 21 May 2005 in favour of one Biju John ("Defendant No. 1"), authorising him to manage agricultural land and, per its recitals, to execute sale agreements, sale deeds and gift deeds "for and on behalf of" the plaintiff. Four days later, Defendant No. 1 executed a registered sale deed conveying the property to John Mathew ("Defendant No. 2") for a stated consideration of Rs. 4,00,000. The plaintiff's case — set out in a suit filed on 10 June 2013 — was that the GPA authorised Defendant No. 1 only to execute a sale the plaintiff himself was already obliged to complete, not to negotiate and settle sale terms on his own initiative; that no consideration was in fact paid; and that the sale deed was accordingly fraudulent, in excess of the GPA's authority, and void ab initio. The plaint sought a declaration that the sale deed was void, a declaration of title, and recovery of possession with mesne profits. The plaintiff pleaded knowledge of the sale deed from 22 May 2009, having revoked the GPA by registered notice on 20 July 2009 once Defendant No. 1 failed to account for his management of the property. The defendants denied fraud, asserted a sale made with full knowledge and consent for genuine consideration, and pleaded limitation.

On 8 July 2015, the trial court framed five issues, including, as Issue No. 5, "whether the suit is barred by limitation," and the suit proceeded to trial. The plaintiff led and concluded his entire evidence; the defendants commenced theirs and completed their evidence-in-chief. Only then, on 17 September 2018, did Defendant No. 2 file I.A. No. 2647 of 2018, invoking Order XIV Rule 2(2)(b) read with Section 151 of the Code, asking that Issue No. 5 be tried and decided as a preliminary issue.

The trial court allowed the application. Treating the suit as one to cancel a void instrument, it applied Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963 — three years from the date the plaintiff learned of the facts entitling cancellation. Since the plaintiff pleaded knowledge from 22 May 2009 but filed suit only on 10 June 2013, the trial court held the suit time-barred and dismissed it on 3 November 2018, without recording a decree. The first appellate court (A.S. No. 23 of 2020) agreed the limitation finding was "perfectly legal," but, noting the absence of a decree, recast the disposal as a rejection of the plaint under Order VII Rule 11(d) instead — an unusual course on an appeal filed by the plaintiff himself, which the Supreme Court later flagged without deciding.

On second appeal, the Kerala High Court held that limitation here was a mixed question of fact and law that could not be decided without first determining whether the sale deed was void for want of authority under the GPA — and that, once the sale deed was treated as void, recovery of possession attracted the twelve-year period under Article 65 rather than the three-year period under Article 59. It held the suit within limitation and remitted the matter, but did not examine whether the "preliminary issue" procedure had been available at all given the stage the trial had reached. Defendant No. 2 appealed to the Supreme Court, which framed the case as raising "questions of recurring procedural importance concerning the scheme of Order XIV Rule 2."

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory text and its legislative history

Order XIV Rule 2, in its current form, is a product of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976. Before that amendment, a court that considered a suit capable of disposal on issues of law alone was required to try those issues first, postponing the issues of fact if it thought fit. The Law Commission's 54th Report identified the resulting difficulty: where a suit was disposed of on a preliminary point later reversed in appeal, the litigation had to be remanded in its entirety, causing delay. Parliament's response was to invert the default: sub-rule (1) now requires a court to pronounce judgment on all issues, "notwithstanding that a case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue," subject to sub-rule (2), which preserves a narrower power — where issues of law and fact both arise and the court considers the case capable of disposal on an issue of law only, it "may" try that issue first, but only if it relates to (a) the court's jurisdiction, or (b) a statutory bar to the suit, and may, "if it thinks fit," postpone settlement of the remaining issues. The Court read this as three cumulative conditions — law only, jurisdiction or statutory bar, and capability of disposal — with the power discretionary even where all three are met.

### 3.2 What counts as an "issue of law only"

Drawing on *Major S.S. Khanna v. Brig. F.J. Dillon* (AIR 1964 SC 497) and *Ramesh B. Desai v. Bipin Vadilal Mehta* ((2006) 5 SCC 638), the Court reaffirmed that the Code confers no jurisdiction to try a suit on mixed questions of law and fact as a preliminary issue: where a legal question's answer itself depends on disputed facts, it cannot be severed for prior trial. The three-judge bench in *Nusli Neville Wadia v. Ivory Properties* ((2020) 6 SCC 557) had already held that "two full-fledged trials by leading evidence are not contemplated," and that limitation can be decided as a preliminary issue only where it turns on admitted facts. The Court in *John Mathew* treated this as settled: limitation "may, in one case, be a pure question of law, and, in another, a mixed question" — the label is not decisive, only whether disputed facts must first be adjudicated. *Sukhbiri Devi v. Union of India* (2022 SCC OnLine SC 1322) illustrated the converse: limitation was properly tried as a preliminary issue there because the foundational facts fixing the limitation period were "vividly and specifically" pleaded, leaving nothing factual to try.

### 3.3 Framing, trial, and the point of no return

None of this settled the question actually before the Court: assuming an issue qualifies, at what procedural stage does the sub-rule (2) power cease to be available? The Court held that the Code distinguishes framing an issue, trying it, and determining it — citing Order XIV Rules 1 and 5 and Order XLI Rule 25, which separately addresses an omission "to frame or try any issue, or to determine any question of fact." Read with Order XX Rule 5, which requires findings on every issue "unless the finding upon any one or more of the issues is sufficient for the decision of the suit," the Court identified four postures a suit might occupy: (i) only the qualifying issue framed, the rest postponed; (ii) all issues framed together, but the qualifying one contemporaneously earmarked for prior trial; (iii) all issues framed and the suit listed for evidence, but no sub-rule (2) application yet made and no evidence yet led generally; and (iv) the court already embarked on a composite trial of the issues generally.

The power to "try that issue first" survives the first three postures but is extinguished once the fourth is reached. The reasoning is textual: "first" is relational, describing one issue's trial relative to the others', and once evidence has begun on a composite basis that ordering can no longer be brought about, prospectively or by retrospective severance. A court may still consider one issue before another while composing its judgment, but that is not the power sub-rule (2) confers. The dividing line, therefore, is not whether all issues have been framed, but whether the court has actually commenced evidence generally. The Court found support in its own recent decision in *Manjula v. D.A. Srinivas* (2026 INSC 465), which described "the remaining issues framed under Order XIV Rule 1" as capable of being left for later decision even after all issues stood framed.

### 3.4 Reconciling *Prithvi Raj Jhingta* and *Sathyanath*

The Full Bench of the Himachal Pradesh High Court in *Prithvi Raj Jhingta v. Gopal Singh* (2006 SCC OnLine HP 25: AIR 2007 HP 11) had gone further, holding that sub-rule (2) applies only where the court initially frames the qualifying legal issue alone and postpones the rest. The Supreme Court approved the narrow holding — once issues have entered a common trial, none can retrospectively be treated as awaiting trial "first" — but declined to endorse the broader proposition, which converts an expressly optional consequence into a condition precedent for the power's existence, contrary to the statutory text.

*Sathyanath v. Sarojamani* ((2022) 7 SCC 644) required separate treatment. It disapproved a direction to try res judicata as a preliminary issue, because res judicata is inherently a mixed question of fact and law and never qualified for sub-rule (2) treatment. Its observations favouring findings on all issues apply where the exception is unavailable on the facts; they do not override the exception where validly invoked, and *Sathyanath* never decided whether prior framing of the remaining issues, by itself, disables the power.

### 3.5 Application to the facts, and what was left open

Applying this framework, the Court found the case squarely within the fourth posture: by the time I.A. No. 2647 of 2018 was filed, the plaintiff had concluded his entire evidence and the defendants had completed their evidence-in-chief. Issue No. 5 was already part of a composite trial in progress, and the statutory occasion to try it "first" had passed. Section 151, the Code's inherent-powers provision, could not resuscitate that lapsed power, since inherent power cannot circumvent the sequence and conditions the Code expressly attaches to a power it has itself conferred. On this ground alone, the trial court's order, the first appellate court's judgment, and the High Court's decision were all held unsustainable, and the suit was restored to the trial court to resume from the point immediately preceding I.A. No. 2647 of 2018.

Significantly, the Court left open the substantive question the High Court had purported to decide: whether a sale deed found void for want of GPA authority converts the suit into one for recovery of possession attracting the twelve-year period under Article 65, or remains a suit for cancellation of an instrument governed by the three-year period under Article 59. The appellant had relied on *Rajpal Singh v. Saroj* ((2022) 15 SCC 260) and *Rajeev Gupta v. Prashant Garg* (2025 SCC OnLine SC 889) for the shorter period; the respondents and the High Court had relied on *Sopanrao v. Syed Mehmood* ((2019) 7 SCC 76) and *Shanti Devi v. Jagan Devi* (2025 SCC OnLine SC 1961) for the longer one. The judgment records these rival authorities without adjudicating between them, holding that the characterisation question "must, therefore, remain entirely open" and that the High Court's observations on it "cannot govern the further proceedings."

## 4. Practical significance

For practitioners handling title and possession suits, the lesson is procedural and time-sensitive: an application under Order XIV Rule 2(2) to sever limitation, jurisdiction, or another qualifying issue must be made — and decided — before evidence has begun on the issues generally. A defendant confident a suit is time-barred on largely admitted facts should raise that plea at, or shortly after, the framing of issues, not once the plaintiff's evidence, let alone the defendant's own, is already underway; once a composite trial has commenced, *John Mathew* forecloses that route, and Section 151 will not revive it. A plaintiff facing a belated application at an advanced stage of trial now has clear authority to resist it on this threshold ground alone.

The judgment also bears on how suits arising from power-of-attorney conveyances should be pleaded — a fact pattern common across India wherever an owner is absent or elderly. The dispute turned on a fine distinction in the GPA's terms: authority to execute a sale the owner was already obliged to complete is not authority to negotiate and settle new terms unilaterally. Drafters of powers of attorney intended to authorise a sale should state that scope unambiguously, since it can determine whether a resulting conveyance is void, voidable, or valid, and consequently which limitation period governs any challenge.

That feeds into conveyancing due diligence. Purchasers acquiring title through a chain that includes a GPA-executed sale deed should treat these facts as a cautionary template: a sale executed within days of the GPA, at a consideration the principal later disputes having received, is exactly the profile of transaction vulnerable to being characterised as void — and if so characterised, the twelve-year period under Article 65, not the three-year period under Article 59, may govern any suit to recover possession, materially lengthening exposure to challenge. Due diligence that stops at confirming the GPA's existence, without scrutinising whether its terms actually authorised the transaction executed, leaves that exposure unaddressed. For registrars, registration of a sale deed executed by an attorney-holder does not itself resolve whether the underlying authority was validly exercised — that question may remain triable decades later, as this history shows.

Finally, the case cautions against procedural applications filed without regard to timing: one aimed at an early, efficient disposal on limitation instead produced thirteen years of litigation across four forums, precisely because it came after the point the Code permitted it. Counsel advising on any Order XIV Rule 2(2) application should treat timing as a threshold question, independent of the plea's substantive strength.

## 5. Conclusion

*John Mathew v. Santha Paul* resolves a genuine gap in the settled law on Order XIV Rule 2: it confirms that the power to try a qualifying legal issue "first" is available across a wider range of procedural postures than the Himachal Pradesh Full Bench in *Prithvi Raj Jhingta* had suggested — framing all issues together does not foreclose later severance — but is extinguished the moment a composite trial on the issues generally has begun, whatever stage the framing had reached. The ruling gives trial courts and litigants a workable, textually grounded test in place of the prior uncertainty, without disturbing the narrower, correctly decided holdings in *Sathyanath* and *Prithvi Raj Jhingta* themselves.

For the underlying dispute, the practical effect is a return to square one: a suit filed in 2013, with issues framed in 2015, now goes back to the Munsiff Court at Kottarakara to be tried afresh on all its issues — including, unresolved, whether the 2005 power-of-attorney sale deed was void, voidable, or valid, and which limitation period governs recovery of possession. The Supreme Court directed the trial court to decide the suit within three months, an unusually pointed acknowledgment of how much time the procedural detour had cost. The judgment's forward-looking value lies less in that outcome than in the clear timing rule it now supplies for every future application to sever limitation, or any other qualifying issue, from a suit's trial.

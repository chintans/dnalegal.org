**Quick answer:** In *M/S Vertilink Media Solutions Private Limited v. Anti Evasion Branch and Ors.*, decided on 25 September 2026, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Anil Kshetarpal and Rajneesh Kumar Gupta, JJ.) dismissed a writ petition challenging an order dated 29 August 2024 that had raised a demand of ₹10,57,75,250 by denying input tax credit under Section 16(2)(c) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, pursuant to a show cause notice issued under Section 73 for the period April 2019 to March 2020. The petitioner argued that this proceeding was barred by Section 6(2)(b) of the CGST Act because the Directorate General of GST Intelligence had earlier issued a common show cause notice under Section 74 to forty-five noticees, including the petitioner, over overlapping input tax credit transactions — and that those DGGI proceedings had since stood concluded, without recovery from the petitioner, after the principal noticee paid the entire demand. The Court held that the petition failed on four independent grounds: an unavailed statutory appeal, unexplained delay of nearly two years, the absence of "same subject matter" between a Section 73 self-assessment proceeding and a Section 74 fraud investigation, and the inapplicability of the double-jeopardy-style bar in Section 75(13) in the absence of any prior penalty actually imposed on the petitioner.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Section 6(2)(b) turns on identity of subject matter, not identity of taxpayer, period, or credit head.** The Court held that two proceedings touching the same assessee's affairs, the same broad period, and overlapping input tax credit do not automatically become the "same subject matter" — the statutory provision invoked, the nature of the allegation, and the liability sought to be determined must also coincide.
- **A Section 73 notice and a Section 74 notice are not interchangeable for this purpose.** The Court treated the difference between a self-assessment shortfall proceeding under Section 73 and a fraud-based extended-period proceeding under Section 74 as itself evidence that the two notices were not directed at the same subject matter, even though both concerned input tax credit claimed by the same entity.
- **A deemed conclusion under Explanation 1 to Section 74 protects only what it actually resolves.** The DGGI proceedings against the petitioner stood concluded only in the sense that no further tax, interest or penalty was to be recovered from it in that specific case; that conclusion did not retrospectively invalidate an independently initiated Section 73 proceeding raising a different liability.
- **Section 75(13) requires a prior penalty in fact, not a prior notice.** The bar against penalising the same act or omission twice presupposes that a penalty was actually imposed in the earlier proceeding; being named as a co-noticee whose case was administratively closed without a demand does not satisfy that precondition.
- **Delay is fatal on its own terms.** The Court treated a nearly two-year gap between the impugned order and the writ petition, unexplained except by the pendency of a rectification application, as an independent ground for dismissal — separate from, and prior to, its findings on the Section 6(2)(b) merits.

## 1. Introduction

Parallel proceedings by different arms of the GST administration — a jurisdictional tax officer on one side, the Directorate General of GST Intelligence ("DGGI") on the other — remain one of the most litigated procedural questions in Indian indirect tax practice. Section 6(2)(b) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 ("CGST Act") is the statutory answer: it bars one tax authority from proceeding on a "subject matter" that another authority has already taken up. But the provision does not define "subject matter," and taxpayers facing multiple notices arising from a single set of business dealings have repeatedly argued that any overlap in assessee, period or transaction is enough to trigger the bar.

*Vertilink Media Solutions* tests that proposition against an unusually layered fact pattern: an Anti-Evasion Branch search that was transferred to the DGGI, a common notice to forty-five noticees including the petitioner, a *separate* notice from a jurisdictional Sales Tax Officer covering the same broad period, an unappealed adjudication order, and — after all that — the DGGI proceedings themselves reaching a conclusion favourable to the petitioner. The Delhi High Court's answer touches four distinct doctrines: the alternate remedy rule, laches, the "same subject matter" test under Section 6(2)(b), and the narrower double-jeopardy-style protection in Section 75(13). This article works through each, the sequence of dates that made the delay point decisive, and what the judgment means for assessees who are simultaneously entangled with more than one wing of the GST administration.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

The petitioner, M/S Vertilink Media Solutions Private Limited, is a company providing script services to Zee Entertainment Enterprises Ltd. and is registered with the Delhi GST authorities. On 3 March 2021, a search was conducted at the petitioner's office and at the residence of its director, Mr. Naresh Dhoundiyal, who was subsequently arrested. The investigation was thereafter transferred to the DGGI, Mumbai Zonal Unit, and the petitioner states that it joined that investigation and responded to summons.

On 31 March 2023, the DGGI issued a common show cause notice to forty-five noticees — including the petitioner and its former director — under the CGST Act, the Maharashtra Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, and the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. On the petitioner's own account, that notice concerned allegations of fraudulent availment of input tax credit, invoking the provisions applicable to suppression, fraud or wilful misstatement — that is, the extended-period machinery under Section 74.

While that notice remained pending, a separate authority — Respondent No. 2, a jurisdictional Sales Tax Officer in Delhi — issued its own show cause notice on 29 May 2024, this time under Section 73 of the CGST Act, covering the period April 2019 to March 2020, alleging that the petitioner had not declared its correct tax liability. The petitioner replied on 28 June 2024, objecting that this notice was barred by Section 6(2)(b) because the DGGI had already taken up the same transactions. Respondent No. 2 proceeded regardless and, on 29 August 2024, passed an order holding that the petitioner had not declared its correct tax liability and had availed input tax credit from suppliers who had not discharged the corresponding output tax — denying that credit under Section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act and raising a total demand of ₹10,57,75,250.

The petitioner did not appeal this order within the statutory period under Section 107. Instead, on 7 April 2025, it filed a rectification application reiterating the Section 6(2)(b) objection. In the meantime, the DGGI's proceedings against the forty-five noticees culminated in an Order-in-Original recording that the principal noticee, Zee Entertainment Enterprises Ltd., had paid the entire input tax credit demanded, together with interest and a 25 per cent penalty. As a result, the proceedings against the remaining noticees, including the petitioner, were treated as concluded under clause (ii) of Explanation 1 to Section 74 of the CGST Act, read with the corresponding provisions of the state and integrated enactments. The petitioner brought this development to the notice of Respondent No. 2 and asked that the pending rectification application be disposed of in light of it. When no action followed, the petitioner filed the present writ petition in August 2026 — roughly two years after the impugned order.

The petitioner sought certiorari quashing both the 29 May 2024 show cause notice and the 29 August 2024 order as without jurisdiction, and a direction that Respondent No. 2 close proceedings on a subject matter already adjudicated (in the petitioner's characterisation) by the DGGI. The Division Bench dismissed the petition in its entirety.

## 3. Legal Analysis

**The alternate remedy bar.** The Court began, as Delhi High Court panels increasingly do in GST writs, with the threshold question of maintainability. The CGST Act provides a statutory appeal under Section 107 against an order such as the one dated 29 August 2024, and the petitioner admittedly did not avail it within the prescribed period. The Court reiterated that the existence of an efficacious alternate remedy is a material consideration in the exercise of discretionary jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution, and found no circumstance justifying the bypass of the appellate mechanism — the challenge, in the Court's assessment, concerned jurisdictional and factual questions of the kind an appellate authority is equipped to examine.

**Laches.** Independently, the Court treated the delay in filing the petition as fatal. The impugned order was passed on 29 August 2024; the writ petition was instituted only in August 2026. The petitioner's sole explanation — that a rectification application had been filed on 7 April 2025 and remained pending — was rejected as insufficient. The Court held that the pendency of a rectification application cannot by itself obliterate the delay in challenging the original order, particularly where the petitioner had consciously allowed the statutory appeal period to lapse rather than avail it. Writ jurisdiction being discretionary and equitable, a party who lets the appeal window close must satisfactorily explain any subsequent delay in seeking equivalent relief by writ; no such explanation was furnished here.

**The Section 6(2)(b) "same subject matter" test.** Having disposed of the petition on maintainability and delay, the Court went on to address the merits, and it is this part of the judgment that carries the greatest doctrinal weight. Section 6 of the CGST Act cross-empowers officers of the Central and State administrations to act under either enactment, while Section 6(2)(b) simultaneously bars a second authority from initiating a fresh proceeding "on the same subject matter" once one authority has already done so. The Court held that the operative word is "subject matter," and that this cannot be established merely by showing that two proceedings arise from the affairs of the same assessee, touch an overlapping period, or share a common factual background. The nature and scope of each proceeding has to be independently examined.

Applying that test, the Court identified several points of divergence between the two notices. The Section 73 notice concerned the petitioner's correct tax liability for April 2019 to March 2020 specifically, and the consequent order turned on denial of input tax credit under Section 16(2)(c) — a provision addressed to credit claimed where the supplier has not paid the corresponding output tax, which does not itself require proof of fraud. The DGGI's Section 74 notice, by contrast, was founded on allegations of fraudulent availment of credit without actual supply of goods or services, directed against forty-five noticees collectively. The Court treated the difference between Section 73 and Section 74 as more than a labelling distinction: the two provisions operate in materially different circumstances — one addressed to ordinary short-payment, the other to suppression and fraud attracting an extended limitation period and enhanced penalty. The mere presence of common vocabulary — "input tax credit," "tax liability" — across both notices, the Court held, does not render their subject matter identical.

The petitioner's reliance on the deemed conclusion of the DGGI proceedings fared no better. The Court accepted that those proceedings had indeed concluded, under Explanation 1(ii) to Section 74, as a consequence of the principal noticee's payment of tax, interest and 25 per cent penalty. But it held that this conclusion meant only that no further tax, interest or penalty stood recoverable from the petitioner *in those proceedings* — it could not retrospectively invalidate a proceeding independently initiated on a different statutory basis and a different subject matter. A closure that operates by virtue of one noticee's payment does not, on this reasoning, immunise every other noticee from a separately founded demand.

The Court also rejected the argument that the DGGI's prior seisin — arising from the transfer of the original Anti-Evasion Branch investigation — operated as a blanket ouster of Respondent No. 2's jurisdiction. The transfer of an investigation concerning one set of allegations, the Court held, cannot oust the jurisdiction otherwise available to another proper officer absent an identity of subject matter with what was transferred.

**Distinguishing the petitioner's own authority.** The petitioner had relied on *Amit Gupta v. Union of India* (2023 SCC OnLine Del 6664) for the proposition that Section 6(2)(b) reflects a legislative intent against multiplicity of GST proceedings. The Court did not disagree with that general proposition — it accepted that *Amit Gupta* recognises the statutory scheme against multiple proceedings on the same subject matter — but held that the decision does not dispense with the foundational requirement that the two proceedings in question must, in fact, concern the same subject matter. Since that requirement was not satisfied on the facts before it, *Amit Gupta* did not assist the petitioner.

**Section 75(13).** Finally, the Court addressed the petitioner's reliance on Section 75(13) of the CGST Act, which bars the imposition of a penalty on a person for the same act or omission for which a penalty has already been imposed under another provision. The Court held this provision presupposes that a penalty has, in fact, already been imposed on the petitioner in the earlier proceeding. The record showed no such prior penalty against the petitioner specifically — the DGGI proceedings against it had been treated as concluded by virtue of the principal noticee's payment, not by any adjudication imposing a penalty on the petitioner. Being named as a co-noticee in a notice that concluded without a demand against oneself does not, the Court held, amount to a prior imposition of penalty for the same act or omission, and Section 75(13) accordingly had no application.

Taken together, the four grounds are not merely cumulative make-weights: the Court expressly clarified that its consideration of the jurisdictional challenge was confined to whether the petitioner had made out a case for invoking Article 226, and that nothing in the order should be read as an opinion on issues that may arise before the competent statutory authority — leaving open, in substance, whatever the pending rectification application or a future statutory remedy might yield on the underlying tax question itself.

It is worth noting what the judgment does not decide. The Court did not have occasion to consider whether the outcome would differ if the two notices had both been issued under Section 74, or if the DGGI notice itself had denied credit under Section 16(2)(c) rather than alleging fictitious supply — situations that would present a closer question on identity of subject matter than the one actually before it. Nor did the Court need to decide whether a taxpayer who *had* timely appealed the Section 73 order, and raised the Section 6(2)(b) objection before the appellate authority, would have fared differently on the merits; the appellate remedy point was dispositive on its own terms, and the merits discussion proceeds on an assumption, not a finding, that the writ route was otherwise open to scrutinise the jurisdictional question. Future litigants relying on this order for its Section 6(2)(b) reasoning should keep in mind that the reasoning was, on the Court's own framing, not strictly necessary to the result.

## 4. Practical Significance

For assessees who find themselves the subject of both a DGGI investigation and separate jurisdictional proceedings, this judgment is a caution against assuming that any overlap in subject matter is enough to invoke Section 6(2)(b). The safer course, where a taxpayer genuinely believes two notices target the same liability, is to plead — and be prepared to demonstrate — that the statutory provision invoked, the nature of the alleged contravention, and the liability sought to be fixed are substantially identical across both notices, not merely that both notices arise from the same business relationship or touch overlapping years. A notice invoking Section 73 alongside a notice invoking Section 74, even against transactions with a shared commercial counterparty, will not readily be treated as the same subject matter on the reasoning applied here.

The judgment also has consequences for how taxpayers should treat a favourable resolution of one proceeding while a related proceeding remains pending elsewhere. A co-noticee whose exposure in a multi-party DGGI notice is administratively closed because the principal party paid the demand should not assume that closure extends to a separately raised demand against it on a different statutory footing. Where a taxpayer wishes such a closure to have a bearing on a parallel proceeding, the correct route — as this judgment implicitly signals by leaving the rectification application open — is to press that argument through the statutory rectification or appellate process, rather than to treat the closure as self-executing relief available by writ.

On litigation strategy more broadly, the judgment reinforces two now-familiar features of Delhi High Court GST practice. First, an assessee who allows the statutory appeal period under Section 107 to lapse takes a real risk that a subsequent writ, however meritorious the underlying jurisdictional objection, will be defeated on delay and laches before the merits are even reached. Filing a rectification application is not a substitute for filing a timely appeal, and does not toll the clock for a later writ. Second, where a jurisdictional objection is itself contested on facts — as the Section 6(2)(b) plea was here, requiring the Court to compare the statutory basis and factual content of two separate notices — the availability of an alternate remedy carries more weight than it would for a purely legal, uncontested jurisdictional defect. Practitioners advising clients with overlapping DGGI and jurisdictional-officer exposure should treat the appeal deadline, not the resolution of any parallel proceeding, as the operative limitation period to protect.

Finally, the decision is a reminder that Section 75(13) is a narrow, fact-specific bar. It cannot be invoked merely because a taxpayer was named in an earlier notice that has since been closed; a taxpayer seeking to rely on it must be able to point to an actual, prior order imposing a penalty on that same taxpayer for the same act or omission.

## 5. Conclusion

*Vertilink Media Solutions* does not announce a new test under Section 6(2)(b) so much as it applies, with some precision, the settled principle that the provision protects against duplication of subject matter, not duplication of assessee or period. The Court's willingness to treat the Section 73/Section 74 distinction as substantively meaningful — rather than a matter of administrative labelling — gives practitioners a concrete marker for future disputes: where the provisions invoked address different kinds of default (ordinary short-payment versus fraud) and different classes of noticees, a shared commercial transaction will rarely be enough to establish identity of subject matter. Equally instructive is the Court's treatment of the DGGI's deemed conclusion: a closure achieved through one noticee's payment resolves only what it resolves, and cannot be read expansively to shield every other party from independently founded demands. Underlying both holdings, however, was a more mundane but decisive procedural failure — an unavailed appeal and a two-year delay — which meant the jurisdictional question, however carefully argued, was never strictly necessary to the result. Taxpayers facing multi-front GST proceedings would do well to treat the appellate timeline, not the fate of a parallel investigation, as the deadline that matters most.

## FAQ

**Does Section 6(2)(b) of the CGST Act bar any second proceeding against a taxpayer already investigated by another GST authority?**
No. The bar applies only where the second proceeding concerns the same subject matter as the first — the same statutory provision, the same nature of allegation, and the same liability. Two proceedings touching the same taxpayer and an overlapping period, but invoking different provisions (here, Section 73 against Section 74) and different factual allegations, are not barred merely because of that overlap.

**If a DGGI show cause notice against multiple noticees is treated as concluded because the principal noticee paid the demand, does that protect every co-noticee from all other GST demands?**
Not necessarily. The Delhi High Court held that such a conclusion protects a co-noticee only from further recovery in that specific proceeding. It does not retrospectively invalidate a separate proceeding raised by a different authority on a different statutory basis.

**Can a pending rectification application justify a delayed writ petition against the original order?**
The Court held no. A rectification application does not toll the limitation period for a statutory appeal, nor does its pendency by itself explain a multi-year delay in approaching the writ court.

**Quick answer:** In *M/s. Global Coal and Mining Private Limited v. Deputy Commissioner of State Tax, CT & GST Circle, Angul* (W.P.(C) No. 16619 of 2026), decided on 7 October 2026, a Division Bench of the Orissa High Court (Dr. Sanjeeb K. Panigrahi and Murahari Sri Raman, JJ.) considered a demand of Rs. 6,50,61,882 raised under Section 74 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 for 2019-20. The assessee had already paid the tax on a turnover of Rs. 1,61,10,378.91 after an audit finding, and the Department had told it in writing that the audit proceedings stood closed. The Court issued notice and held, on a prima facie view, that the conditions for initiating Section 74 proceedings were not satisfied and that the initiation was "misconceived and contrary to statutory provisions". It directed that no coercive action be taken on the order until the next date, 4 November 2026. This is an interim order, not a final adjudication.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Payment on an audit finding is not evidence of fraud or suppression.** The Court was prima facie unwilling to treat tax paid "on being pointed out in the audit report" as the product of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax (para 8.1).
- **A departmental closure letter carries weight.** The Court read the Department's own intimation, that no further proceeding was required because tax, interest and penalty had been paid "as per Sub Section (6) of Section 73 / 74", alongside the later Section 74 notice (paras 3 and 8).
- **The Court looked at the notice's own stated basis.** The notice relied on an unexplained reduction of turnover in the reconciliation statement and called it a "willful misstatement"; the Court found that characterisation unsupported at the prima facie stage (para 8).
- **Relief is interim and narrow.** The Court restrained coercive action pursuant to the 30 March 2026 order; it did not quash the order, decide limitation, or rule on the alternative statutory remedy (para 9).
- **The reasoning is tied to Section 74 for 2019-20.** The Court did not consider Section 74A, which governs later periods, and made no finding on how the closure letter would affect any notice for a different amount.

## 1. Introduction

A taxpayer that accepts an audit finding and pays the tax would ordinarily expect the matter to end there. The Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (the CGST Act) is built to allow exactly that: Section 74(5) lets a person pay tax, interest and a fifteen per cent penalty before a show cause notice is served, and Section 74(6) then bars the proper officer from serving a notice "in respect of the tax so paid". What happens when the Department nevertheless issues a notice and passes an order for a much larger sum is the question that this order of the Orissa High Court touches.

The order is short. It issues notice, records a prima facie view and restrains coercive recovery, and it cites no case law of its own. It deserves attention because the fact pattern is common: audit findings are routinely settled by payment, and the formation that audited the assessee is not always the one that later issues a notice. The assessee is then left with a closure letter on one side and a demand under Section 74, with a penalty equal to the tax, on the other.

This article sets out the facts and the procedural position, analyses the three strands of the Court's reasoning (the effect of the closure intimation, the requirement of fraud or suppression, and the nature of the interim direction), considers what the order does not decide, and then turns to the practical consequences for assessees and advisers. The petition is listed again on 4 November 2026, after the State files its counter affidavit.

## 2. Case summary and background

**The parties and the order challenged.** The petitioner, M/s. Global Coal and Mining Private Limited, was represented by Mr. Rudra Prasad Kar, Senior Advocate, assisted by Mr. Jiban Jyoti Pradhan. The Deputy Commissioner of State Tax, CT & GST Circle, Angul, and another were the opposite parties, represented by Mr. Sunil Mishra, Standing Counsel. The petition challenged an order dated 30 March 2026 passed by the Deputy Commissioner under Section 74 of the CGST Act and the Odisha Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 for the tax period 1 April 2019 to 31 March 2020, together with the notice dated 20 September 2025 on which it rested (para 2).

**The audit and the payment.** The petitioner's counsel took the Court to an audit report enclosed with Form GST ADT-02, the form in which the findings of an audit are communicated to the registered person. The audit identified a disputed amount of Rs. 1,61,10,379. The petitioner deposited the tax and interest shown to be deficient. The State Tax Officer, Angul Circle, then wrote to the petitioner. The Court reproduced the intimation in full:

> "This is in reference to the Audit conducted for the period Apr,2019-Mar,2020 As you have paid the amount of tax and other dues mentioned in the GST ADT-02 along with the applicable interest and penalty, in full, as per Sub Section (6) of Section 73 / 74 of CGST Act/..... Section ...... of (State Act), therefore, no further proceeding in this matter is required to be taken, hence Audit proceedings in this case stand closed." (para 3)

The text is plainly a template, with unfilled blanks for the State Act provision, and the Court quoted it as it stood.

**The later notice and demand.** Despite that intimation, the Deputy Commissioner of CT & GST, Angul Circle, issued a notice dated 20 September 2025. The notice, as quoted by the Court in para 8, stated that the petitioner had "reduced your sale turnover of Rs.1,61,10,378.91 by putting in table 50, which is specified for 'Adjustments in turnover due to reason not listed above'", that it had "not mentioned the reason for such reduction of turnover, anywhere in your reconciliation statement", and that the reduction was therefore treated as "unreasonable and willful misstatement". The notice required the petitioner to discharge tax at eighteen per cent on that turnover. It put the liability at Rs. 14,49,934 under the CGST Act and Rs. 1,49,934 under the State Act, with interest at eighteen per cent under Section 50(1). Those figures are reproduced as the Court recorded them. The proceeding culminated in a summary of order in Form GST DRC-07 dated 30 March 2026 demanding Rs. 6,50,61,882. The judgment does not break that figure into tax, interest and penalty, and it does not say how a notice quantifying tax at the sums above produced a demand of that size.

**The submissions.** For the petitioner, Mr. Kar argued that the initiation of Section 74 proceedings was bad in law because the officer had issued the notice "without verifying existence of necessary condition to initiate proceeding" and had raised a demand on that basis (para 4.2). He pointed out that the notice's own basis was the same Rs. 1,61,10,378.91 on which the petitioner had already discharged its liability after the audit report (para 4.4). He relied on Instruction No. 05/2023-GST (F. No. CBIC-20004/3/2023-GST) dated 13 December 2023 of the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs, which refers to the Supreme Court's judgment of 19 May 2022 in *CC, CE & ST, Bangalore (Adj.) v. Northern Operating Systems Private Limited*, Civil Appeal Nos. 2289-2293 of 2021. The Court reproduced paragraphs 3.1 to 3.3 of that Instruction. The Standing Counsel accepted notice and sought four weeks to file a counter affidavit (para 6). The judgment records no argument advanced for the State on the merits.

**The holding.** The Court issued notice, listed the matter on 4 November 2026, and in the accompanying order on the interim application (I.A. No. 9840 of 2026) recorded that, on the audit report and the intimation, the petitioner had discharged tax on the turnover in question. It held "prima facie" that initiating Section 74 proceedings was "misconceived and contrary to statutory provisions" (para 8.1). On that footing it directed that no coercive action be taken pursuant to the order of 30 March 2026 until the next date (para 9).

## 3. Legal analysis

**The statutory frame.** Section 74(1) of the CGST Act permits a notice where tax has not been paid or short paid, or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised, "by reason of fraud, or any wilful-misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax". The penalty is equivalent to the tax. Explanation 2 to Section 74 defines "suppression" as non-declaration of facts or information which a taxable person is required to declare in the return, statement, report or any other document furnished under the Act, or failure to furnish information when asked for in writing by the proper officer. Section 74(5) allows payment before service of notice, with interest under Section 50 and a penalty of fifteen per cent, on the basis of the person's own ascertainment or the tax ascertained by the proper officer. Section 74(6) provides that the proper officer, on receipt of that information, "shall not serve any notice under sub-section (1), in respect of the tax so paid or any penalty payable". Section 74(7) preserves a route for a notice for any shortfall: where the officer thinks the amount paid "falls short of the amount actually payable", a notice may issue "in respect of such amount which falls short". These provisions apply to the period before 1 April 2024. Section 74A, inserted by the Finance Act, 2024, governs 2024-25 and later years, and the Court did not discuss it.

**First strand: the closure intimation.** The Court did not itself cite Section 74(6). It reached the point through the Department's own words, which invoked "Sub Section (6) of Section 73 / 74" and treated the audit proceedings as closed. Counsel's submission that there was a "clear inhibition under the statute for initiation of any further proceeding on the subject-matter covered under the audit" (para 4) was recorded, and the Court's prima facie conclusion that the initiation was "contrary to statutory provisions" is most naturally read as accepting it. The reasoning is persuasive on the narrow facts. The subject-matter of the notice and the subject-matter of the audit payment were, on the Court's reading of the record, the same turnover of Rs. 1,61,10,378.91. Once the Department had itself certified that payment of that tax was in full satisfaction, an inference that the same payment was the fruit of suppression is difficult to sustain.

There is a limit to what that reasoning shows, and the Court did not address it. Section 74(6) is confined to "the tax so paid", and Section 74(7) expressly permits a notice for a shortfall. A demand of Rs. 6,50,61,882 against an audit amount of about Rs. 1.61 crore invites the question whether any part of it concerns tax not covered by the audit payment. The judgment does not say, because the notice quoted in para 8 refers only to the Rs. 1,61,10,378.91 reduction. If the final order in fact concerns that turnover alone, the larger figure must be made up of interest and penalty, and Section 74(6) speaks of both the tax so paid and "any penalty payable". If it reaches other turnover, Section 74(7) may support the notice for that part. The State's counter affidavit is where that distinction will be tested.

A further point is that the template intimation refers to Sections 73 and 74 together, so the letter does not identify which provision governed the payment. A Department seeking to resist the closure argument could say that a template letter cannot create an estoppel against the statute. The order gives no sign that this was argued, and the better view is that the letter matters less as an estoppel than as evidence that the proper officer had accepted the payment as complete.

**Second strand: fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression.** The Court observed that the Deputy Commissioner had acted "as if the tax liability discharged on Rs.1,61,10,378.91 on being pointed out in the audit report is the outcome of fraud/wilful/misstatement/suppression of facts to evade tax" (para 8.1). Section 74 carries a penalty equal to the tax and is available only where the specified mental element is present; the Department must show it. The notice here rested on a single feature of the record: the petitioner had made an adjustment in a table meant for "Adjustments in turnover due to reason not listed above" and had not explained it in the reconciliation statement.

The Court's reasoning on this point sits close to the Instruction relied on by the petitioner. The extract reproduced in the judgment states that Section 74(1) "cannot be invoked merely on account of non-payment of GST, without specific element of fraud or wilful mis-statement or suppression of facts to evade tax", and that evidence of such conduct "should also be made a part of the show cause notice" (para 4.3). The Court did not say that it was applying the Instruction or the Supreme Court decision to which it refers, and it did not analyse either. It quoted them as part of the petitioner's case. The better reading is that the Court regarded the absence of any identified fraudulent element as sufficient for a prima facie conclusion, which is consistent with the Instruction without being derived from it. Nothing in the order turns on *Northern Operating Systems* itself; the Instruction cites it in the context of secondment, and its relevance here is only through the CBIC's own use of it.

A critique is in order. The inference the Court draws, that payment on an audit finding sits uneasily with an allegation of suppression, is reasonable but not conclusive. Payment on an audit finding and concealment in a return can co-exist: an assessee may have under-reported, been caught, and paid. What the order really holds is narrower and sounder: where the Department closes the audit on payment and then reopens the same turnover without pointing to any independent evidence of intent, the notice cannot be sustained merely by describing an unexplained adjustment as a "willful misstatement". The Explanation 2 definition of suppression, which speaks of non-declaration of facts the taxpayer is "required to declare", does not obviously cover an adjustment that was declared in a table of the return but not explained in a reconciliation statement. The Court did not enter into that question, and it will matter at the final hearing.

**Third strand: the character of the relief.** The direction in para 9 is carefully bounded. The Court did not stay the order of 30 March 2026, quash it, or restrain the adjudicating authority from any other step. It directed that "no coercive action shall be taken against the petitioner in pursuance of order dated 30th March, 2026" until the next date. That is protection against recovery on the strength of the order. It does not, on its terms, extend to the limitation for a statutory appeal, an issue the Court did not address.

The order also does not discuss the alternative remedy. The CGST Act provides an appeal to the Appellate Authority under Section 107, conditioned on pre-deposit of part of the disputed tax, and a petitioner who invokes Article 226 without it is usually asked why. The Court recorded no argument from the State on the point and gave no reasons for entertaining the petition, other than the prima facie view that the initiation was without statutory foundation. Whether absence of the statutory conditions is a recognised exception is for the final judgment. The order is best read as interim protection in a case that looked strong on the audit record.

**What survives amendment and what remains unresolved.** The statutory text quoted above is that of Section 74 as it applied to 2019-20. Section 74A, which applies from 2024-25, restructures the notice and limitation provisions. Whether a closure letter issued under an audit module for a later year would have the same effect depends on the text of that section, which the Court had no occasion to consider. The order also leaves open whether Section 74(6) is engaged by a payment that follows audit findings, how the demand of Rs. 6,50,61,882 is made up, and whether the adjustment amounts to suppression within Explanation 2.

## 4. Practical significance

**Keep the closure paper.** The intimation was central to the petitioner's case. Assessees who settle an audit by payment should obtain and retain the closure communication, the ADT-02, the payment challans and any acknowledgment from the proper officer, and should record the date and form of each. In a later dispute, those documents decide whether the Department treated the payment as complete.

**Match the payment to the audit finding.** The order protects the assessee where the notice concerns the same turnover that was audited and paid for. It offers little help where a notice concerns different transactions or a different head. Advisers should reconcile the notice against the audit report and say in the reply which entries were audited and paid for; the line between Section 74(6) and Section 74(7) will be drawn on that comparison.

**Answer the allegation of intent directly.** Where a notice invokes Section 74 on the basis of an unexplained adjustment, the reply should address each ingredient: fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression, and the intent to evade tax. The CBIC Instruction quoted in the judgment is addressed to field officers, and it is a legitimate reference point in a reply, because it describes what the Board itself expects of a notice. Reliance on the Instruction should be paired with the statutory text; the Court did not hold that the Instruction binds an adjudicating authority beyond what the statute already requires.

**Choose the remedy deliberately.** A writ petition may secure early protection where the statutory conditions for a notice are absent on the face of the record, as here. It carries a risk that a court will relegate the petitioner to the appeal, as happens frequently in disputes that turn on facts, and by then the period for appeal may have run. An assessee considering a writ should compute the appeal limitation under Section 107 from the date of communication of the order, consider a protective appeal with the required pre-deposit, and seek exclusion of the time spent in the writ proceedings where available. The present order does not say whether any such course was taken.

**Treat the interim direction as exactly that.** The restraint on coercive action lasts until the next date and depends on the State's counter affidavit. Registered persons in comparable positions should not read the order as an assurance that a Section 74 demand after audit closure is invalid. They should read it as a reasoned indication that such a demand, unaccompanied by evidence of intent and relating to turnover already paid for, will receive close scrutiny at the Division Bench level in Odisha.

## 5. Conclusion

The Orissa High Court's order is a small decision on a large and recurring question. It holds, prima facie, that a Section 74 proceeding cannot stand on turnover that the assessee has already taxed after an audit finding and that the Department itself has closed, unless the notice discloses something more than the payment. It restrains coercive recovery until the State responds. It does not quash the demand, rule on limitation or discuss the alternative remedy, and it does not cite a single authority of its own.

Its value lies in the posture it describes. The Court looked first at what the Department had said in its own closure letter, then at what the notice alleged, and found the gap too wide to permit recovery on an interim basis. That approach, testing the notice against the Department's earlier conduct as well as the statute, is likely to interest assessees elsewhere. Whether it hardens into a principle will depend on the final judgment, in particular on how the Court treats Section 74(7) and the unexplained difference between the audit amount and the demand.

## FAQ

**Did the Orissa High Court quash the Section 74 order?**
No. It issued notice, recorded a prima facie view that initiating Section 74 proceedings was misconceived and contrary to statutory provisions, and directed that no coercive action be taken pursuant to the order of 30 March 2026 until the next date.

**Does paying tax on an audit finding bar a later Section 74 notice?**
Section 74(6) bars a notice in respect of tax paid under Section 74(5), and the Court prima facie treated the Department's own closure intimation as significant. The order does not decide how far that bar extends where a notice covers a shortfall or different turnover, which Section 74(7) contemplates.

**Does the order apply to periods after 31 March 2024?**
The order concerns Section 74 for 2019-20. It does not discuss Section 74A, which governs later periods, so its application to those years is untested.

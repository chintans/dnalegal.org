**Quick answer:** In *Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana*, Special Leave Petition (C) No. 13917 of 2009, reported at (2012) 1 SCC 656, a Bench of Justices R. V. Raveendran, A. K. Patnaik and H. L. Gokhale held on 11 October 2011 that a "GPA sale", or an agreement of sale combined with a general power of attorney and a will (an "SA/GPA/Will" transfer), neither conveys title nor creates an interest in immovable property. Only a registered deed of conveyance does that. Such papers survive as an agreement to sell, with the limited protection of Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882.

---

## 1. Introduction

Few documentary practices in Indian real estate have been as durable as the GPA sale. A buyer pays the full price, takes possession and receives not a sale deed but a bundle: an agreement of sale, a power of attorney over the property, and a will as insurance against the seller's death. The bundle is cheaper than a conveyance and leaves no registered record of the buyer's ownership.

The Supreme Court examined the practice in a reference that began with an order of 15 May 2009, reported at (2009) 7 SCC 363, and ended with the judgment of 11 October 2011 discussed here. It remains the first authority cited whenever a title chain runs through a power of attorney rather than a deed. This note sets out what the Court decided, tests the reasoning against the statute, and draws out the consequences for drafting and due diligence.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

### 2.1 The practice and its ill-effects

The Court called the labels misnomers: "there cannot be a sale by execution of a power of attorney nor can there be a transfer by execution of an agreement of sale and a power of attorney and will." The vendor takes the consideration, delivers possession and signs an agreement of sale (or an agreement plus an affidavit of payment and possession), an irrevocable general power of attorney, and a will in the buyer's favour. The Court distinguished genuine arrangements, such as a power given to a relative to manage affairs or execute a conveyance, or a development agreement empowering a developer to sell plots or apartments.

Reproducing its earlier order, the Court named the users of the device: vendors with imperfect title, purchasers investing undisclosed money, and purchasers avoiding stamp duty and registration charges. It recorded revenue evasion, circulation of unaccounted money, resales by vendors who exploit the absence of a public record, and the growth of a real estate "mafia". The Union of India and the States of Delhi, Haryana, Punjab and Uttar Pradesh agreed the practice should be discouraged. Mr. Gopal Subramanium assisted as amicus curiae.

### 2.2 The holding

The Court reasoned in three steps. First, a sale. Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act provides that transfer of tangible immovable property worth Rs. 100 or more "can be made only by a registered instrument" and that a contract for sale "does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property". The Court relied on *Narandas Karsondas v. S.A. Kamtam*, (1977) 3 SCC 247, and *Rambhau Namdeo Gajre v. Narayan Bapuji Dhotra*, (2004) 8 SCC 614, for the point that Section 53A is a shield against the transferor and has nothing to do with ownership.

Second, a power of attorney creates an agency, not a transfer. It is revocable unless made irrevocable in a manner known to law, and "even an irrevocable attorney does not have the effect of transferring title to the grantee", citing *State of Rajasthan v. Basant Nehata*, (2005) 12 SCC 77. An attorney holder may, however, execute a deed of conveyance that passes title. Third, a will operates only on death and is revocable until then; registration does not make it more effective.

The conclusion: an SA/GPA/Will transaction "does not convey any title nor create any interest in an immovable property". Observations of the Delhi High Court in *Asha M. Jain v. Canara Bank*, 94 (2001) DLT 841, treating power of attorney sales as a recognised mode of transaction were "unwarranted and not justified", and decisions accepting such transactions as concluded transfers "are not good law". Such papers cannot be the basis for mutation in municipal or revenue records, and the same applies to leasehold property, which can be transferred only by registered assignment.

### 2.3 What the Court preserved

The Court said it had "merely drawn attention to and reiterated the well-settled legal position", and did not make the ruling prospective. It softened the effect nonetheless. Existing papers "can continue to be treated as existing agreement of sale", and may be used to obtain specific performance or to defend possession under Section 53A. If entered into before the date of judgment, they may support applications to development authorities for regularisation. Mutations already effected on such documents "need not be disturbed, merely on account of this decision".

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 A statutory argument

The decision's strength is how little it adds to the statute. Section 54 makes a registered instrument the exclusive route for transferring such property, and Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 makes non-testamentary instruments that create or extinguish rights in immovable property compulsorily registrable. A bundle in which no document is a conveyance cannot satisfy those provisions by being read together. The Court's characterisation of each component (contract, agency, testament) is orthodox, and its refusal to let the label "GPA sale" substitute for substance is sound.

The Court also noted the 2001 amendment inserting Section 17(1A) in the Registration Act, which requires registration of contracts to transfer for the purpose of Section 53A and denies unregistered ones effect for that purpose, and the State stamp-law amendments charging conveyance-level duty on agreements with possession. It treated these as partial measures. Its own contribution was to close the doctrinal door the Delhi High Court's observations had left ajar.

### 3.2 Section 53A as the residual protection

Because Section 53A survives, the buyer is not remediless, but the protection has two limits. It is defensive: per *Rambhau Namdeo Gajre*, as quoted in the judgment, it "cannot be pressed in service against a third party". And under Section 17(1A), an agreement executed after the 2001 amendment took effect and left unregistered has "no effect" for Section 53A purposes. A buyer holding such an agreement cannot invoke the doctrine.

### 3.3 Questions left open

The line between genuine and impermissible powers of attorney is drawn by example, not by test. The Court contrasts a relative's power with a purchaser's irrevocable power, and places development agreements outside the ruling; cases between those poles must be argued on their facts. The preservation of existing mutations protects what had already happened, and the judgment's statement that such papers cannot support mutation governs fresh applications. And the regularisation window turns on the date of the transaction, which shifts the dispute to proving that date.

A critic might add that the Court's emphasis on black money, though accurate about why the device is used, could suggest that every such buyer was dishonest. Preserving agreement-to-sell consequences implicitly recognises that many were not.

## 4. Practical Significance

**Due diligence.** A chain that includes a GPA sale or an agreement, power and will shows no transfer at that link. The executant remains owner, and the purchaser should insist on a registered conveyance from the person holding title.

**Drafting.** Where a power of attorney is genuinely needed, it should authorise the attorney to execute a registered conveyance for the owner, and that conveyance should then be executed and registered. The power is not the transfer. Development agreements should comply with the State's stamp and registration requirements.

**Existing buyers.** A buyer holding such papers should obtain a registered conveyance while the vendor is alive and cooperative. Failing that, the agreement supports a suit for specific performance, and possession can be defended under Section 53A if the registration condition is met.

**Litigation.** Pleading ownership solely on SA/GPA/Will papers invites an early challenge. Plead the agreement, with Section 53A in the alternative.

**Lenders and officials.** The judgment gives revenue officers a ground to refuse mutation and lenders a ground to refuse such papers as title. The protection of mutations already made should be read narrowly.

## 5. Conclusion

*Suraj Lamp* did not change the law; it declined to let a commercial habit pass for a legal rule. A sale is a transfer of ownership by registered instrument, a power of attorney is an agency, and a will speaks only at death. Combining them does not create a conveyance, however complete the price and possession.

The judgment has stayed workable because it was firm on principle yet left existing buyers a path through specific performance, Section 53A and regularisation. The lesson for practitioners is practical: where the economics are complete, the registered deed should follow, and any lesser document should be described, pleaded and diligenced as what it is, an agreement to sell.

**Quick answer:** In *Amit Agency v. Commissioner, CGST and CX & Others* (CWJC No. 14955 of 2024), decided on 13 October 2026, a Division Bench of the Patna High Court (Justice Rajeev Ranjan Prasad and Justice Sunil Dutta Mishra) declined to quash a show cause notice issued under Section 74(1) of the Bihar Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (read with the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017) after a departmental audit covering 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20. The Bench found that the notice stated its reasons, that the assessee was not shown to have contested the final audit report, and that the notice showed neither non-application of mind nor jurisdictional error. It held that the High Court, exercising writ jurisdiction, does not weigh the sufficiency of the officer's satisfaction that tax was short paid by reason of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. The order-in-original was appealable under Section 107, so the Court left the assessee to that remedy and allowed one month from the judgment to file the appeal. The audit-limitation, single-notice, corrigendum and interest objections were not decided.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The notice is judged on its own text.** The Bench accepted the rule it drew from the Supreme Court's decision in *G.R. Infra Projects* that a notice must carry its own reasons, and then found those reasons present in this notice (paragraphs 40 and 45).
- **Audit-detected default after an unanswered audit report can support Section 74(1).** The Court treated non-compliance with the final audit report within the period allowed as relevant to the officer's satisfaction of wilful suppression (paragraph 46).
- **Bona fide non-payment remains outside Section 74.** The Bench itself said that a simple case of non-payment for bona fide reasons cannot be brought within the extended period (paragraph 46).
- **Writ review of "it appears to the proper officer" is narrow.** The Court borrowed the reopening-of-assessment standard from income tax and declined to examine the sufficiency of reasons (paragraph 47).
- **A good writ point does not displace the appeal.** The Court examined the jurisdictional plea first, found nothing in it, and relegated the assessee to Section 107 (paragraphs 27 and 50).
- **Several objections are left open.** The Court recorded that all questions are left open and that its observations were confined to the challenge to the notice (paragraphs 53 and 54).

## 1. Introduction

A departmental audit under Section 65 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (the "CGST Act") is now the commonest route by which an old return period is reopened. Sub-section (7) of Section 65 lets the proper officer initiate action under Section 73 or Section 74 where audit detects tax not paid or short paid, erroneously refunded, or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised. Section 74 is the harsher of the two provisions: it applies where the default is attributed to fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, carries a penalty equal to the tax, and allows an order to be passed within five years of the due date for the annual return.

Whether an audit finding of ordinary non-payment can be framed under Section 74 at all has occupied the Supreme Court twice in recent months. In *G.R. Infra Projects Ltd., Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, the Court insisted that the allegations leading to an inference of fraud or suppression must emerge from the notice itself. In *Tata Steel Ltd. v. Union of India* (2026 INSC 920), it set aside a Section 74 notice that rested on audit observations without foundational facts. Both decisions are now routinely invoked against audit-based Section 74 notices.

*Amit Agency* shows how a High Court applies those two decisions to an audit-originated notice. The Bench read them narrowly, as decisions about notices bereft of reasons, and then declined to treat the notice before it as such. It also declined to decide a cluster of other objections, because it considered the order appealable.

This article sets out the facts and the demand, then analyses the three questions the Court did decide: whether the notice disclosed reasons for invoking Section 74(1); how far a writ court may examine the officer's satisfaction; and whether the alternative statutory remedy barred the petition. It then identifies what remains open and the practical consequences for taxpayers facing audit-based demands.

## 2. Case summary and background

### The assessee and the audit

The petitioner is a partnership firm engaged in the business of cigarettes, registered under the GST laws (paragraph 2). A notice dated 13 December 2021 called for its records for an audit under Section 65 of the CGST Act for the financial years 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20 (paragraphs 2 and 28). The notice warned that if the firm did not comply, it would be presumed not to hold the records. The Court recorded that the petitioner did not deny receiving that notice but maintained silence in the writ petition about why it did not respond (paragraph 28). The department's case was that reminders followed and that the audit commenced on 26 April 2023 on production of documents for the first two years (paragraphs 15 and 16).

An audit memo dated 26 June 2023 recorded ten objections (paragraphs 3 and 29). They included interest of Rs. 68,64,631 on delayed payment (paragraph 1 of the memo); short payment of tax of Rs. 33,75,668 for 2018-19 and 2019-20; non-payment of GST of Rs. 1,77,120 on vehicle hiring; late fee of Rs. 1,10,950 for delayed filing of Form GSTR-1; unpaid tax under the reverse charge mechanism (where the recipient, not the supplier, pays tax); and an unreconciled turnover of Rs. 9,688 (paragraph 29). The Court noted that the petitioner disagreed with objections 1 to 9 and agreed to, and paid, objection 10 (paragraph 31).

### The monitoring committee, the final report and the notice

The discrepancies went before a Monitoring Committee Meeting held on 7 July 2023, which approved them with a direction to recover the amounts within 15 days, failing which a show cause notice would issue (paragraphs 17 and 32). The minutes were communicated as the final audit report under Section 65(6) on 18 July 2023 (paragraph 17). A demand-cum-show cause notice under Section 74(1) followed on 30 August 2023 (paragraph 18). The summary in Form GST DRC-01 uploaded on the portal on 31 January 2024 covered only July 2017 to March 2018; the department explained that this was due to paucity of space, and the portal remarks showed a total of Rs. 1,06,35,806 (CGST Rs. 6,30,529, SGST Rs. 6,47,938 and Cess Rs. 93,57,339) as unpaid (paragraph 18).

The adjudicating authority gave personal hearings on 12 December 2023, 10 January 2024, 18 January 2024 and 25 January 2024. The petitioner filed written submissions on 16 January 2024 (paragraph 18). By an order-in-original dated 22 April 2024 covering all three years, the authority confirmed the demand with penalty equal to the tax (paragraph 5). A corrigendum dated 27 August 2024 and a separate order dated 18 October 2024 for 2017-18 followed, and the petition was amended to challenge them (paragraph 1).

### The challenge

The amended petition sought to quash the audit report, the notice, the order-in-original, the corrigendum and the separate order. The petitioner argued, in substance, that: the audit exceeded the three-month limit in Section 65(4); Section 74 was invoked mechanically on the strength of an audit report without any element of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression; a single notice and order for three years was impermissible; the notice and order were not uploaded properly and lacked a digital signature as Rule 26(3) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 and Rule 142(5) and (6) require; the corrigendum could not introduce a penalty under Section 161; interest could run only until payment; and substantial credit sat in the electronic credit ledger and in Form GSTR-2A (paragraphs 1, 6 to 11 and 23 to 24). It relied on CBIC Instruction No. 05/2023-GST dated 13 December 2023, *G.R. Infra Projects* and *Tata Steel* (paragraph 6).

The department answered that the order was appealable under Section 107, the petition was filed beyond the four-month period of appeal and condonation, the audit was completed within the Section 65(4) timeline, and the notice recorded its reasons (paragraphs 13 to 21).

### The holding

The Court held that the notice did not suffer from non-application of mind or jurisdictional error (paragraph 49), that it would not examine the sufficiency of the officer's satisfaction (paragraph 47), and that the order-in-original, being appealable, would not be reviewed under Article 226 of the Constitution of India (paragraph 50). It noted that a copy of the petition had been served on the department's office on 21 August 2024, within four months of the order. It therefore allowed the petitioner to appeal within one month of the judgment, with the appellate authority to consider the appeal on merits (paragraph 51).

## 3. Legal analysis

### The scheme: Sections 65, 73 and 74

Section 65(7) leaves the choice of provision to the proper officer: the Bench read it as the officer's responsibility to take a view on which provision applies (paragraph 34). Section 74(1) is engaged "where it appears to the proper officer" that tax has not been paid or has been short paid, or credit wrongly availed, by reason of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Sub-section (5) allows the taxpayer, before service of notice, to pay the tax with interest under Section 50 and a reduced penalty of fifteen per cent of the tax. The Bench read the 15-day direction in the audit report as an opportunity to take that route, and treated the notice as the consequence of the petitioner's failure to use it (paragraph 36).

The judgment states that Section 74 governs periods up to financial year 2023-24, and notes that Section 74A was inserted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, effective 1 November 2024 (paragraph 35 and footnote). The ratio therefore concerns 2017-18 to 2019-20 under the Bihar and Central Acts; the Court treated the Bihar and Central provisions together.

### Issue one: were reasons recorded?

The Bench began by accepting the central proposition of *G.R. Infra Projects*, which it quoted at length: the allegations that lead to an inference of fraud or concealment "should emanate from the notice itself" and must not be a "mechanical use" of the statutory words (paragraph 39). It also accepted that validity is judged on the notice and "cannot be supplanted by a counter affidavit in Court" (paragraph 40). It then reproduced paragraphs 9 to 15 of *Tata Steel*, in which the Supreme Court held that the Assessing Officer must enter his satisfaction even when objections arise from audit, and that the words "are not to be mechanically recited in the notice" (paragraph 43).

The Bench then distinguished *Tata Steel* on its facts (paragraph 44). There, the department had transferred the notice to the call book and contested the audit objection before the Public Accounts Committee, which indicated that the Assessing Officer himself had no satisfaction. In *Amit Agency* the final audit report had been served, was not shown to have been contested, and was followed by a notice that stated the defaults head by head. The notice said, in the Court's account, that "had the audit not been conducted of the records/registers of the noticee" the defaults would not have been noticed (paragraph 36), and the Court read that as alleging "blatant violation and defiance of the law" (paragraph 45).

The Court added that, where an assessee knowingly files late but does not pay the late fee, and cannot satisfy the officer of its bona fides, the officer is competent to issue a notice under Section 74(1) (paragraph 46). It also invoked Section 155 of the CGST Act, which places the burden of proving eligibility for input tax credit on the claimant, and described the principle behind Section 74(1) as "breach of the principle of 'utmost good faith'" (paragraphs 37 and 38).

### Issue two: the writ court and the officer's satisfaction

The Bench held that in writ jurisdiction it is "not supposed to go into the sufficiency of the reasons" for the officer's opinion, pointing to the opening words of Section 74(1) (paragraph 47). It drew support from the Supreme Court's reasoning in *Sanand Properties Private Limited v. Joint Commissioner of Income Tax*, ((2026) 488 ITR 337), concerning notices to reopen income tax assessments. On that reasoning, the court may ask only whether the belief truly existed, was bona fide, and rested on material with a rational connection to it. The Bench applied the same limited inquiry to the officer's satisfaction.

### Issue three: alternative remedy

Citing *Commissioner of Income Tax v. Chhabil Dass Agarwal* ((2014) 1 SCC 603) and *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tandon* ((2010) 8 SCC 110), the Bench stated that a statutory appeal which is equally efficacious ordinarily displaces a writ unless the order suffers from a jurisdictional error (paragraphs 25 and 26). It said it would have relegated the petitioner at once, but examined the jurisdictional plea because it was raised (paragraph 27). Having found no jurisdictional error in the notice, it declined to review the order (paragraph 50).

### Critique

The reasoning is orthodox on remedy and more contestable on substance.

First, the remedy analysis is the strongest part. The order was appealable, the department's own record showed the petition was served within four months of the order, and the Court protected the petitioner's limitation. That is a pragmatic disposition.

Second, the substantive treatment of Section 74(1) sits uneasily with *Tata Steel*. The Supreme Court there said the officer must be satisfied that fraud, wilful misrepresentation or suppression "had led to such mismatch or short payment of tax", not merely that a mismatch or short payment exists (paragraph 43, quoting paragraph 11 of the Supreme Court's judgment). The Patna Bench's reasoning in paragraphs 36 and 46 comes close to treating failure to pay after an audit finding as itself the suppression. The audit memo and final report, however, are the department's own detection; defaults found by auditors from the assessee's own books are not obviously suppressed in the ordinary sense, and the notice's statement that the defaults would have "gone unnoticed" without the audit is an inference rather than a foundational fact. The Court's concession that bona fide non-payment cannot attract Section 74 (paragraph 46) preserves the principle, but the line it draws, defiance after a final audit report, makes the assessee's silence the pivot. A reader may reasonably ask whether silence after the audit report can show what the assessee did when filing the returns, which is the time at which suppression must exist.

Third, importing the income tax reopening standard is an analogy, not a statutory reading. The income tax provisions turn on the officer's belief, while Section 74(1) uses "where it appears to the proper officer". The Court did not examine whether the two formulas call for the same degree of review, and *Tata Steel* itself examined the satisfaction in the Section 74 setting. The better view is that *Amit Agency* applies the limited-review standard to a notice that, on the Court's reading, did disclose reasons; it does not hold that the satisfaction is beyond scrutiny.

Fourth, the Court recorded, but did not decide, the points on Section 65(4), the single notice for three years, Rule 26(3) and the digital signature, the corrigendum, and interest. On the penalty, it observed that the notice did propose penalty at paragraph 9(ix) (paragraph 38), which answers the claim that no penalty had been proposed but not the separate objection that a corrigendum under Section 161 cannot supply what the order omitted. The Bench's final directions left all questions open (paragraph 53), so none of these should be treated as decided against assessees.

### Survival after amendment

Because the Court confined Section 74 to periods up to 2023-24, the judgment's reasoning on fraud-based invocation will matter only to legacy periods. For periods from 2024-25, Section 74A governs, and the question of how the Supreme Court's insistence on foundational facts translates to that provision was not before the Court. The judgment also records that Explanation 2 to Section 74 was omitted with effect from 1 November 2024, a point it took from *Tata Steel* (paragraph 43).

### Precedential weight

The judgment was delivered by a Division Bench of a High Court, so it binds single judges of the Patna High Court and is persuasive elsewhere. Its reading of *Tata Steel* is a High Court's reading of a Supreme Court decision, and assessees in other jurisdictions are free to argue that *Tata Steel* demands more.

## 4. Practical significance

**Respond to the audit memo and the final audit report on the record.** The Court gave weight to the petitioner's silence: it had not placed its audit objections on the record, had not said it challenged the final audit report, and had not responded to the original audit notice (paragraphs 28 and 33). A written, reasoned reply to every audit objection, and a request for its consideration by the monitoring committee, gives the assessee both a merits record and a platform for arguing that any later Section 74 notice lacks foundational facts.

**Use Section 74(5) deliberately.** The Bench presented the 15-day window as an opportunity to pay tax and interest with a fifteen per cent penalty before notice. Where the audit objection is sound, paying under Section 74(5) avoids a penalty equal to the tax. Where the objection is contested, the assessee should expect a notice and prepare for it.

**Scrutinise the notice for its own facts.** Under *G.R. Infra Projects*, as the Bench accepted it, a notice cannot be repaired by an affidavit. Assessees should compare the notice with the audit report and ask what facts it actually pleads on suppression, as opposed to mere non-payment. A notice that recites the statutory words only is vulnerable. One that, like this one, explains how the defaults came to light may not be.

**Choose the remedy early and watch the clock.** The Court was willing to examine the notice in writ jurisdiction, but then relegated the petitioner to appeal. The practical lesson is that a writ should be reserved for a clear jurisdictional defect and filed promptly, while the appeal period under Section 107 is protected. The Section 107 appeal period is three months, extendable by one month on sufficient cause, and the Court observed that it was this window that the department invoked (paragraphs 13 and 51). A petitioner who loses a writ should not count on the grace the Court gave here, which turned on service of the petition within four months of the order.

**Keep the other objections alive.** Because the Court left all questions open, an assessee in the same position should plead the audit-limitation, single-notice, signature and corrigendum points in its appeal grounds. For the audit-limitation point in particular, the department's position was that audit commenced on 26 April 2023 and the report followed on 18 July 2023 (paragraphs 15 and 17); the commencement date is a fact an assessee should test against Explanation to Section 65(4).

## 5. Conclusion

*Amit Agency* is best read as a decision on how far a High Court will go before sending an assessee to appeal, not as a broad statement that audit detection justifies fraud-based demands. The Court accepted the Supreme Court's requirement that a Section 74 notice carry its own reasons, found those reasons in this notice, and declined to examine the sufficiency of the officer's satisfaction. That outcome follows partly from the record: an unanswered audit notice, an audit memo disputed but not answered on file, an unchallenged final report and a notice that explained its basis.

The weaker part is the equation, in paragraph 46, between failure to comply with the final audit report and wilful suppression. If read expansively, it would convert every audit-detected default that the assessee does not pay within fifteen days into a Section 74 case. The Court itself rejected that reading when it said that a bona fide default cannot attract the extended period, and later benches may be expected to hold the department to that limit.

For now, the safest positions are practical ones: answer audit findings in writing, consider Section 74(5) where the objection is sound, insist that notices plead foundational facts, and preserve every procedural objection for the appeal. Whether the appellate authority will accept the Section 65(4) and single-notice objections is the next question, and the Court has deliberately left it open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a Section 74 notice be issued merely because an audit found short payment of tax?**
Not on that ground alone. The Bench itself said that a simple case of non-payment or short payment for bona fide reasons cannot be brought within the extended period under Section 74(1). Its decision rests on the notice explaining how the defaults came to light, an unchallenged final audit report and the assessee's failure to comply with it.

**Will a High Court quash a Section 74 notice for lack of reasons?**
It may, where the notice is a mechanical recital of fraud or suppression without foundational facts, as the Supreme Court held in *G.R. Infra Projects* and *Tata Steel*. In this case the Patna High Court found the notice sufficiently reasoned and would not examine the sufficiency of the officer's satisfaction.

**Does a writ petition stop the clock for a statutory appeal under Section 107?**
No. Here the Court noted that the petition had been served within four months of the order, and, in the circumstances, gave one month from the judgment to appeal. An assessee cannot assume the same indulgence.

**Were the audit time-limit and single-notice objections decided?**
No. The Court left all questions open and said its observations related only to the challenge to the notice.

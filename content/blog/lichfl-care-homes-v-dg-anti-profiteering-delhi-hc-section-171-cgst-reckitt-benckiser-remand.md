**Quick answer:** In *LICHFL Care Homes Limited v. Director General of Anti-Profiteering*, decided on 28 September 2026 (judgment reserved on 17 September 2026), a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Anil Kshetarpal and Shail Jain, JJ.) dismissed a writ petition against an order dated 23 March 2026 of the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, Principal Bench ("GSTAT"). The order had upheld a finding under Section 171 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 that the developer of the "Jeewan Anand" project at Bhubaneswar profiteered by ₹2,07,08,131 over the period July 2017 to November 2019, and had directed payment of ₹2,31,93,107 (after adding 12% GST) with interest at 18%. The Court held that a methodology which quantifies additional input tax credit against purchase value, then divides the project-level saving by total area, does not resurrect the turnover-based method rejected in *Reckitt Benckiser India Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India*. It also held that the pre-GST comparison rests on credit actually availed, not credit the developer could have claimed.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Post-remand methodology survives a "relabelling" challenge.** The Court held that replacing "turnover" with "purchase value" as the denominator, and then converting the project-level saving into a per-square-foot figure, is materially different from the ITC-to-turnover comparison held flawed in *Reckitt Benckiser* (¶¶31–35).
- **Actual availment, not eligibility, is the test.** Because the developer's ST-3 returns showed nil CENVAT credit actually availed before GST, unclaimed but legally available credit could not be treated as though it had reduced pre-GST tax incidence (¶¶37–41, 60).
- **Goods and services credit are both counted.** The Court rejected an attempt to confine the benefit to the ₹14,52,570 of credit on inward goods, leaving out ₹1,93,28,564 on inward services (¶42).
- **A "without prejudice" computation is not a conclusive admission, but it may still matter.** The Court accepted that such a submission cannot mechanically be treated as an unconditional admission, yet held the order stood independently on the computation (¶¶45–47).
- **Writ jurisdiction is not a second appeal on quantum.** A contention that was considered and rejected by the Tribunal does not attract interference absent manifest error of law or non-compliance with a binding direction (¶¶28, 53–56, 61–62).
- **Incidental costs such as a sub-station were left open.** The Court expressed no view on whether such expenditure is recoverable from homebuyers, while observing that the *Reckitt Benckiser* method does not contemplate balancing every commercial cost against the credit benefit (¶¶49–50).

## 1. Introduction

Section 171 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 ("CGST Act") is the anti-profiteering provision of Goods and Services Tax ("GST") law. In summary, it requires a supplier to pass on to the recipient, by way of commensurate reduction in prices, any reduction in the rate of tax or any benefit of input tax credit ("ITC") that GST brought about. ITC is the mechanism by which a registered person deducts the GST paid on its inputs from the GST it collects on its outputs. Because the provision speaks of a "benefit" without prescribing how to measure it, the method of computation has generated most of the litigation in this area.

Real estate has been the hardest sector. Before GST, developers paid service tax; after 1 July 2017 they claimed ITC on inputs and input services. The question was whether that shift put money in the developer's pocket that should have gone to homebuyers as lower prices. The authorities answered the question by comparing pre-GST and post-GST ratios of ITC to turnover. In its judgment of 29 January 2024, reported as 2024 (82) G.S.T.L. 344 (Del.), the Delhi High Court in *Reckitt Benckiser India Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India* found that method flawed for real estate and directed that the total savings from GST be computed for each project and divided by the total area.

*LICHFL Care Homes* is a decision on what compliance with that direction looks like. The developer's case was that the Directorate General of Anti-Profiteering ("DGAP") complied in form but not in substance. The Division Bench disagreed, and in doing so it settled three points that will recur in the many remanded real estate matters: whether a change of denominator is a change of method, how pre-GST credit that was never claimed is to be treated, and how far a High Court will re-examine a computation in writ jurisdiction.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

**The project and the original proceedings.** The petitioner, LICHFL Care Homes Limited, developed "Jeewan Anand", a residential project at Bhubaneswar, Odisha, comprising flats and covered parking. It was commenced in 2011 and completed in November 2019, with a total area taken by the DGAP at approximately 2,70,048 square feet (¶7). A homebuyer complained that the benefit of ITC had not been passed on. The DGAP's first report dated 28 January 2021 found profiteering of ₹1,85,70,263 for July 2017 to September 2020, and the erstwhile National Anti-Profiteering Authority ("NAPA") accepted it by an order of 20 June 2022 (¶¶2, 8). The developer challenged that order in W.P.(C) 12533/2022.

**The remand.** While that petition was pending, the Court delivered *Reckitt Benckiser* in a batch that included the petitioner's case. By an order of 25 April 2024 the petitioner's matter was remanded for fresh determination, and the Competition Commission of India ("CCI") directed the DGAP on 7 May 2024 to reinvestigate (¶¶9–10). The DGAP called for information on 28 May 2024, and the petitioner responded on several dates between 13 June 2024 and 12 February 2025 (¶11). The developer's position was that there was no profiteering or, alternatively, that any benefit on account of ITC on goods could not exceed about ₹13,49,003, which had already been passed on.

**The recalculation.** The DGAP's report of 4 April 2025 covered July 2017 to November 2019. It found that the ratio of ITC to purchase value was nil before GST and 17.99% afterwards. Applying that percentage to post-GST purchase value of goods and services (excluding taxes and duties) of ₹11,54,27,648, it computed total savings of ₹2,07,65,434. Dividing by the project area gave ₹76.895 per square foot. Applied to the sold area of 2,69,304 square feet, that produced a profiteered amount of ₹2,07,08,131. GST at an effective 12%, being ₹24,84,976, was added, giving ₹2,31,93,107 (¶13).

**The developer's case before GSTAT.** The developer argued that construction was outsourced and that it could not take credit on construction materials before GST. It said it paid service tax of ₹1,79,44,457 in the pre-GST period and was legally entitled to CENVAT credit of about ₹2,38,25,609 on input services, which it had not availed through inadvertent error (¶¶14–15). It also argued that GST increased its output tax incidence, so higher ITC could not be treated wholly as a benefit (¶16). The DGAP's clarification of 21 November 2025 answered that the ST-3 returns showed nil CENVAT credit availed, so the service tax paid on input services had been a real cost before GST (¶17).

**GSTAT's order.** After hearings beginning on 26 September 2025 and continuing on several dates through 2 March 2026 (¶54), GSTAT held that the DGAP's approach was consistent with *Reckitt Benckiser*. It recorded total purchase value of ₹46,03,72,534, nil pre-GST ITC and post-GST ITC of ₹2,07,76,653 (¶20). It upheld ₹2,07,08,131, added 12% GST following paragraph 157 of *Reckitt Benckiser*, and directed payment with interest at 18% (¶21).

**Issues before the High Court.** The Court framed the principal issue as whether the DGAP, on remand, adopted a methodology contrary to *Reckitt Benckiser*, and whether GSTAT erred in law in upholding it (¶5). In substance it decided six sub-questions: (i) whether substituting purchase value for turnover resurrected the rejected method; (ii) whether unclaimed pre-GST CENVAT credit had to be counted; (iii) whether only credit on goods could constitute the benefit; (iv) the effect of higher tax incidence and of the developer's "without prejudice" alternative computation of ₹1,39,93,358; (v) the sub-station expenditure; and (vi) the addition of 12% GST and 18% interest.

**Holding.** The writ petition was dismissed with pending applications. The Court found no "patent jurisdictional error, manifest illegality or failure to comply with the binding directions" in *Reckitt Benckiser* (¶62).

## 3. Legal Analysis

**Scope of review.** The Court began by fixing the standard (¶28). The petition was not an appeal on quantum. Articles 226 and 227 of the Constitution of India could be exercised where a tribunal acted contrary to governing law, exceeded jurisdiction or failed to give effect to a binding direction of the Court, but not to substitute the Court's assessment of factual material. It reinforced the point at ¶56 by distinguishing a tribunal that fails to consider a material contention from one that considers and rejects it; only the first ordinarily warrants judicial review. This framing did much of the work. The developer's core complaint was that the computation was wrong, and the Court treated most of that complaint as a challenge to findings of fact within the specialised tribunal's competence.

**Issue 1: did "purchase value" simply replace "turnover"?** The petitioner's lead argument was that the DGAP had again compared pre-GST and post-GST ratios, merely changing the denominator. The Court reproduced paragraphs 124, 129 and 157 of *Reckitt Benckiser* (¶29) and read paragraph 129 narrowly. What was found flawed there was a method that inferred benefit by comparing ITC to turnover, on the premise that credit accrual tracks receipts from buyers. That premise fails in real estate because construction expenditure and eligible credit are not uniform over a project's life, and accrual of credit is not related to the amounts collected from buyers (¶31). The Court did not hold that every pre-GST versus post-GST comparison was impermissible.

The Court then distinguished the DGAP's exercise. Purchase value was not a proxy for turnover used to derive a benefit relatable to amounts collected from individual buyers. It was the denominator used to quantify the proportion of ITC available against project expenditure, after which the resulting saving was expressed as a project-wide per-square-foot figure so that equal areas receive equal benefit (¶¶32–34). The Court also stated the governing question with care: "The question is not whether the methodology is the only possible methodology, but whether it is a fair and reasonable methodology consistent with the specific direction issued by this Court" (¶35). It reinforced this by referring to paragraph 124 of *Reckitt Benckiser*, which holds that no fixed mathematical formula can be laid down, while adding that a methodology is not impermissible merely because it involves computation (¶52).

**Issue 2: eligibility versus availment of pre-GST credit.** The Court called this contention "more substantial" (¶36). The developer asserted that CENVAT credit of about ₹2,38,25,609 on service tax paid was legally available before GST, so post-GST ITC on services was not incremental. The Court answered that "the distinction between eligibility and actual availment is material" (¶37). The DGAP had relied on the developer's own ST-3 returns, which showed nil CENVAT credit actually availed. The developer's affidavit before GSTAT confirmed that it had not claimed the credit because of an alleged human error and had paid service tax in cash (¶39).

The Court's reasoning is textual and economic. Section 171 concerns the benefit of ITC "actually accruing to the supplier" and its passing on to the recipient (¶38). To credit the developer with a notional pre-GST credit would compare "actual post-GST benefit with a hypothetical pre-GST benefit" (¶40). The Court accepted that the developer "may have been legally entitled" to the credit, but held the authorities were justified in examining actual credit in each period (¶60).

The Court did not equate CENVAT credit under the service tax regime with ITC under the CGST Act; it compared only what was actually availed in each period.

**Issue 3: goods versus services.** The developer argued that at most ₹14,52,570 of credit on inward goods could count, because credit on services was available before GST. The Court held that the distinction between goods and services "is not determinative where the question is the total additional ITC actually availed by the supplier after introduction of GST" (¶42). Post-GST ITC comprised ₹14,52,570 on goods and ₹1,93,28,564 on services. This follows from the answer to Issue 2: the services argument depended entirely on notional credit.

**Issue 4: higher tax incidence and the "without prejudice" computation.** The developer contended that GST raised the tax on services, so extra ITC did not represent an economic gain. The Court held the point showed no error in the specific computation, since the DGAP had not treated all post-GST ITC as an arbitrary windfall but had quantified it against purchase value (¶43). It noted that the developer had itself, without prejudice, computed profiteering at ₹1,39,93,358 on the assumption that ITC attributable to the higher tax incidence was excluded (¶¶18, 44).

The Court handled the "admission" point evenly. A submission made "without prejudice" cannot mechanically be treated as an unconditional admission, and the alternative computation is not determinative of liability under Section 171 (¶¶45–46). But GSTAT's order did not rest on the alleged admission alone. It independently examined the DGAP computation, purchase value, ITC availed, project area and sold area (¶46), so even if the admission label was wrong, the substantive basis remained (¶47).

**Issue 5: the sub-station expenditure.** The developer said it had absorbed costs for a sub-station and allied electrical infrastructure that could have been recovered from homebuyers. The Court declined to decide the point, observing that it depends on the agreements between the parties and that the developer may pursue any remedy permitted in law (¶49). It added that the *Reckitt Benckiser* method "does not contemplate a broad balancing of every commercial expense incurred by a developer against the ITC benefit" and cannot be converted into "an unrestricted enquiry into every commercial cost or expenditure of the project" (¶50).

**Issue 6: GST and interest.** The addition of ₹24,84,976 at 12% followed paragraph 157 of *Reckitt Benckiser*, which treats GST collected on the additional realisation as part of the profiteered amount (¶57). Interest at 18% "follows from the statutory scheme", and no independent jurisdictional infirmity was shown (¶58).

**Precedents.** The only authority in the judgment is *Reckitt Benckiser*, which the Court treated as binding. Its contribution is interpretive: paragraph 129 rejects a specific inference, not every temporal comparison.

**Provision, period and amendment.** The judgment concerns Section 171 of the CGST Act for July 2017 to November 2019, a project that was completed in November 2019. It does not discuss any amendment to Section 171 or to the institutional machinery, and this article expresses no view on whether the reasoning would survive one.

**Critique.** The Court's approach is defensible on the standard of review, but the reasoning invites three observations.

First, on the figures recorded, the "comparison" adds little where pre-GST ITC is nil. The ratio of 17.99% equals post-GST ITC of ₹2,07,76,653 divided by post-GST purchase value of ₹11,54,27,648 (¶¶13, 20, 37). Multiplying it back by the same purchase value returns approximately the credit actually availed (₹2,07,65,434 against ₹2,07,76,653). In substance, the profiteered saving is the whole of the post-GST credit, spread over area. That may be a fair way to measure a "total saving", but it is different from the incremental comparison the developer's argument assumed, and the Court's insistence that the method is "materially different" (¶33) rests on how the number is distributed, not on how it is derived.

Second, the treatment of tax incidence is the thinnest part of the analysis. If GST raised the tax burden on services from the pre-GST rate, the credit is partly the offset to that increase. The Court does not decide whether Section 171 requires netting that effect. It holds only that the developer did not show error in the computation and had itself supplied an alternative figure (¶¶43–45). An assessee in a different position, with a fuller record on incidence, would not be foreclosed by this ruling.

Third, the eligibility-versus-availment distinction is sound on the statutory language and consistent with the anti-profiteering purpose, yet harsh in application: a developer that could have claimed credit but did not, through error, bears both the pre-GST cost and the post-GST profiteering liability.

## 4. Practical Significance

**For developers with remanded or pending Section 171 matters.** The decision signals that the DGAP's post-remand method (post-GST ITC measured against purchase value, saving divided by project area, applied to sold area) is unlikely to be set aside in writ jurisdiction merely because it echoes pre-GST versus post-GST comparison. Developers should concentrate on the factual record. That means demonstrating actual pre-GST credit availed, actual incidence of tax on each input head, and any specific price reductions already passed on. Arguments that require the Court to recompute are precisely those the Court declined to entertain (¶53).

**For credit positions.** The lesson on availment is practical. A developer that was entitled to CENVAT credit in the pre-GST period but did not claim it is treated as having borne the cost. The Court treated the filed ST-3 returns as decisive of actual availment, absent material showing they were factually incorrect (¶41). Where credit was in fact utilised or carried forward, the records should show it.

**For choice of forum and remedy.** The developer took its full case to GSTAT and then to the High Court under Articles 226 and 227. The Court's insistence that it is "not an appeal against the quantum" (¶28) is a reminder that the evidentiary battle must be won before the Tribunal. Anything not placed on record there, including a fuller analysis of tax incidence, will be hard to introduce later. The Court also left the sub-station recovery question open for separate remedies between developer and buyers (¶49), which may be an independent avenue depending on the sale agreements.

**For "without prejudice" submissions.** The Court's treatment (¶¶45–47) is helpful but not a safe harbour. Alternative computations should be framed carefully, with their assumptions recorded alongside.

**For homebuyers.** The per-square-foot method gives equal benefit for equal area, and the addition of GST and 18% interest (¶¶57–58) means the sum payable exceeds the bare profiteered figure.

**For audit and departmental proceedings.** The DGAP established nil availment from the ST-3 returns. Developers should reconcile pre-GST returns, books and post-GST credit registers before answering any notice.

**Limits of the ruling.** The decision repeats that no fixed formula exists (¶52) and rests on this record. It should not be cited for the proposition that any DGAP methodology will be upheld.

## 5. Conclusion

The Delhi High Court in *LICHFL Care Homes* did not weaken *Reckitt Benckiser*; it read it precisely. The earlier judgment rejected an inference (that ITC accrual tracks turnover) and prescribed an outcome (project-level savings divided by area). The DGAP's revised exercise abandoned the inference and produced the outcome, and the Court held that was enough. It also confirmed that anti-profiteering is concerned with credit that actually reached the supplier's books and not with credit that could have.

The decision leaves real questions open. It does not decide whether Section 171 requires the higher post-GST tax incidence on services to be netted from the credit benefit, and it does not decide whether developers may recover incidental costs such as electrical infrastructure from buyers. Those questions will turn on the record in later cases.

For practitioners, the message is to build the factual record before the authorities and GSTAT, because a writ court will not rebuild it. For developers, unclaimed credit is a cost that stays with the taxpayer.

## FAQ

**Does this judgment approve a single methodology for anti-profiteering in real estate?**
No. The Court repeated that no fixed formula exists and asked only whether the method adopted is fair, reasonable and consistent with the direction in *Reckitt Benckiser*. It found that the DGAP's post-remand method met that test on this record.

**Can a developer reduce its profiteering liability by pointing to pre-GST CENVAT credit it was entitled to but did not claim?**
Not on this ruling. The Court held that the comparison turns on credit actually availed, and returns showing nil availment prevented the developer from treating unclaimed credit as if it had reduced its pre-GST tax incidence.

**Is a "without prejudice" alternative computation a binding admission?**
The Court said it cannot mechanically be treated as an unconditional admission, and that it does not determine liability under Section 171. But it may be noted as showing that an issue was raised, and the order can stand on the independent computation.

**Why does the profiteered amount include GST and interest?**
The Court upheld the addition of 12% GST following paragraph 157 of *Reckitt Benckiser*, which treats GST collected on the additional realisation as part of the profiteered amount, and upheld interest at 18% as following from the statutory scheme.

**Quick answer:** In *Ganapati (Dead) By His LRs v. Prabhakar & Others*, decided on 22 September 2026, the Supreme Court dismissed a title suit brought by the legal representatives of a son against his half-sister and a third-party purchaser, holding that his stepmother's statutory half-share in her deceased husband's property under Section 3(2) of the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937 had matured into her absolute ownership under Section 14(1) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 — even though she never obtained a formal partition and the courts below found no evidence she had possessed the property as its owner. She was therefore competent to bequeath that share by will, and her legatee competent to sell it on.

## 1. Introduction

A dispute over two houses in Terdal village, Bagalkot district, Karnataka, travelled through a Munsiff Court, a Fast Track Court, the Karnataka High Court and finally the Supreme Court before settling — nearly eighty years after the death that started it — what should have been a routine question: when a Hindu man dies intestate before 1956, leaving a son and a widow, and the widow later wills her share to her daughter, must the widow have first partitioned the property, or actually possessed it as owner, before her share counts as hers to give away? The trial court and the first appellate court answered yes, on the footing that a widow who never asserted partition holds nothing beyond a right to claim one, and that the estate passes to the surviving son by survivorship if she dies without exercising it. The Karnataka High Court, in second appeal, answered no. The Supreme Court has now affirmed the High Court, and in doing so has extended a line of authority developed for a widow's maintenance rights to the distinct statutory devolution right conferred by Section 3(2) of the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937 ("the 1937 Act").

The Bench of S.V.N. Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria, JJ. (opinion by Bhatti, J.) held that a widow's interest under Section 3(2) of the 1937 Act, once it devolves on her husband's death, becomes her absolute property under Section 14(1) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 ("the 1956 Act") regardless of whether she ever partitioned it from her husband's coparcenary or actually possessed it in her own right. The judgment is marked non-reportable and breaks no entirely new ground — it applies, rather than creates, the doctrine associated with *V. Tulasamma v. Sesha Reddy* — but it resolves a genuine gap those precedents left open: whether the "pre-existing right" reasoning developed for a widow's maintenance charge extends equally to her statutory share on intestate devolution. This article sets out the facts, the doctrinal reasoning, and what the ruling means for anyone advising on succession to property where a Hindu man died intestate in the period between 1937 and 1956.

## 2. Case summary and background

The propositus, Gangaram, died intestate in 1949, survived by his son Ganapati (by his first wife, deceased) and his second wife, Kashibai, who had two daughters, Shakuntala (Defendant No. 1) and Kamalavva (Defendant No. 2). The suit properties were two house properties, TMC Nos. 1074 and 1075, situated in Terdal village, Jamkhandi taluka, Bagalkot district. Ganapati's case, filed originally as O.S. No. 9 of 1988 before the Munsiff Court at Banahatti (later transferred and re-registered as O.S. No. 267 of 1999 before the Principal Civil Judge, Jamkhandi), was that he alone succeeded to his father's estate on Gangaram's death and had remained its absolute owner ever since. The suit was triggered by events after Kashibai's own death in 1986: Shakuntala secured mutation of the properties in her name in the 1987–88 municipal records, relying on a registered will dated 13 October 1984 by which Kashibai had bequeathed the properties to her. While the suit was pending, Shakuntala sold TMC No. 1074 to a third party, Tatappa Burashi (Defendant No. 3), by a registered sale deed dated 3 August 1995; his legal representatives were impleaded and eventually carried the appeal to the Supreme Court.

The defendants' primary case was that an oral partition had occurred between Ganapati and Kashibai under which the suit properties fell to Kashibai's share, evidenced circumstantially by Ganapati having sold a different house he retained and left the village. Their alternative, and ultimately decisive, case was that Kashibai held an independent statutory right as Gangaram's widow regardless of any partition.

The trial court, by judgment dated 17 July 2002, found that Ganapati had succeeded as sole surviving coparcener under Mitakshara law on Gangaram's 1949 death (which preceded the 1956 Act), that Kashibai acquired only a limited interest under Sections 3(1) and 3(3) of the 1937 Act with a right to seek partition, that she never exercised it, that the defendants had failed to prove the alleged oral partition (rejecting a daughter's hearsay account of what her mother had told her), and that the estate accordingly devolved on Ganapati alone by survivorship on Kashibai's death. It held Kashibai's will void for want of title to give, and moulded relief to order possession to Ganapati notwithstanding that his plaint had sought only declaration and injunction. The Fast Track Court at Jamkhandi, hearing the first appeal (R.A. No. 19 of 2002), confirmed this reasoning in a judgment dated 3 January 2007, adding that the defendants had not even pleaded the particulars — date, form, or witnesses — of the oral partition they alleged, and that Section 14(1) of the 1956 Act enlarges a widow's interest only where she is shown to have held or possessed the property, in her own right, as its owner.

The Karnataka High Court, Circuit Bench at Dharwad, reversed on second appeal (R.S.A. No. 551 of 2007) by judgment dated 19 July 2012, framing the substantial question of law as whether the courts below were justified in ignoring the vesting of a right in Kashibai under Section 3(2) of the 1937 Act read with Section 14(1) of the 1956 Act. It held that Gangaram's intestacy, occurring as it did before 1956, triggered Section 3(2), under which Kashibai took a half-share equal to Ganapati's; that this half-share, on the coming into force of the 1956 Act, became her absolute property under Section 14(1); that no further act — partition, or proof of possession as owner — was needed to complete that transformation; and that she was accordingly free to bequeath it, making Shakuntala the absolute owner and her sale to Tatappa Burashi valid. Ganapati's legal representatives appealed to the Supreme Court.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory scheme: Section 3 of the 1937 Act and Section 14 of the 1956 Act

The Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937, enacted to improve a Hindu widow's position under classical law without abolishing the joint family system, provided in Section 3(2) that where a Hindu governed by the Mitakshara school dies having an interest in Hindu joint family property, his widow "shall... have in the property the same interest as he himself had," subject to Section 3(3), which characterises that interest as a limited "Hindu woman's estate" carrying a right to claim partition as if she were a son. The widow thus stepped, on her husband's death, into an interest equal in quantum to a son's — but qualitatively limited, defeasible on her death or remarriage, and without a full owner's power of alienation.

Section 14 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, engineered the transition away from that limited estate. Sub-section (1) provides that "any property possessed by a female Hindu, whether acquired before or after the commencement of this Act," is held by her "as full owner thereof and not as a limited owner," with an Explanation defining "property" broadly to include property acquired by inheritance, partition, in lieu of maintenance, by gift, by her own skill, or by any other means whatsoever, and property held by her as "stridhana" immediately before the Act's commencement. Sub-section (2) carves out an exception: where property is acquired by way of gift, will, or other instrument, or under a decree or award, prescribing a restricted estate, that instrument governs and Section 14(1) does not enlarge it.

### 3.2 The pre-existing right doctrine and its extension

The interpretive difficulty is that Section 14(1) speaks of property "possessed by" a Hindu female, which courts below in this case read as requiring proof that she actually held or possessed the property in her own right as owner — not merely that a statutory interest had devolved on her that she never perfected by partition or possession. The Supreme Court's own precedents had developed a different, more liberal reading in the context of a widow's right to maintenance. In *V. Tulasamma & Others v. Sesha Reddy* (1977) 3 SCC 99, the Court held that a Hindu widow's right to maintenance is not a mere claim granted out of grace but a tangible, pre-existing legal right against the family property (a jus ad rem); an instrument allotting property to her in lieu of that right, even one purporting to restrict her estate, merely recognises a right she already had, so that Section 14(1) — read liberally to advance the 1956 Act's object of converting limited interests into absolute ownership — operates to enlarge it in full, and Section 14(2) has no application because that sub-section is confined to instruments that create an independent title for the first time. The Court held further that "possessed by" carries the widest amplitude, extending to constructive or legal possession, so long as it is backed by some vestige of title and not the possession of a mere trespasser. *Raghubar Singh & Others v. Gulab Singh & Others* (1998) 6 SCC 314 reaffirmed this: a widow's maintenance right arises from Shastric Hindu law itself, not merely from the 1937 Act, so that any instrument recognising it — whatever restrictions it purports to impose — attracts Section 14(1) rather than the narrower Section 14(2) exception, and Section 14(2) applies only to instruments creating a genuinely new and independent title.

Both authorities dealt with a widow's maintenance charge, allotted or crystallised by an instrument. *Ganapati* presented a different fact pattern: Kashibai's interest arose not from an instrument allotting maintenance, but directly from the statutory devolution mechanism in Section 3(2) on her husband's intestate death, and no partition or instrument had ever been executed in her favour. The appellants argued this distinguished the case entirely — the "pre-existing right" in *Tulasamma* and *Raghubar Singh* was a maintenance right recognised by an instrument, whereas Kashibai had neither an instrument nor proof that she ever partitioned or possessed the property as owner. The respondents also relied on *Potti Lakshmi Perumallu v. Potti Krishnavenamma*, 1964 SCC OnLine SC 35, for the proposition that a widow's interest is quantified as at the date she seeks to enforce partition rather than the date of her husband's death.

The Supreme Court sided with the respondents, holding that "even assuming the Defendants have failed to prove the factum of the oral partition pleaded by them, the necessary corollary in law under the Act, 1937, read with Section 14(1) of the Act, 1956, remains intact and unaffected." It treated Section 3(2) itself as conferring a right functionally equivalent to the pre-existing right recognised in *Tulasamma* and *Raghubar Singh*: the sub-section is, in the Court's words, "a confirmation by the Legislature of a right for a person to obtain the property of another on the latter's death in certain circumstances" — devolution effected by legislative act, not merely by survivorship or testamentary or contractual instrument. On that footing, partition between the son and the widow "is not a sine qua non for continuing to enjoy the right as an independent or absolute owner of the property left behind by the prepositus" [as spelt in the judgment]. Even on the assumption that no partition had in fact occurred in an undivided estate, the Court reasoned, the shares within the notionally undivided coparcenary "are subject to fluctuation but not to denial of a right" — meaning Kashibai's entitlement existed and matured under Section 14(1) whether or not it had been formally carved out.

### 3.3 What the ruling leaves undisturbed

The judgment does not hold that every statutory or customary interest a Hindu woman might hold automatically ripens into absolute ownership irrespective of its source; it expressly preserves Section 14(2) for instruments — gifts, wills, decrees, awards — that create an independent title in a woman's favour for the first time, restricting only interests that recognise or confirm something she already held. Nor does it revisit the settled proposition, undisturbed since *Tulasamma*, that a genuinely new grant with a restricted estate remains restricted. What the Bench added is narrower: that a statutory devolutionary interest under Section 3(2) of the 1937 Act — itself a legislative act of transfer, not an instrument inter partes — falls on the *Tulasamma* side of that line rather than requiring separate proof of partition or possession-as-owner before Section 14(1) can operate on it. The Court also confined itself, consistently with the limited scope of a second appeal under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, to holding that the High Court had correctly identified and answered a substantial question of law on admitted facts; it did not purport to reopen the trial court's factual findings on the alleged oral partition, which it treated as immaterial to the outcome.

## 4. Practical significance

The ruling matters most for succession disputes arising from Hindu intestacies between 1937 (when the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act came into force) and 1956 (when the Hindu Succession Act supplanted it) — a category that remains very much alive in Indian litigation, since a death in that window can still generate title disputes among an intestate's children and their own successors-in-interest decades later, as this case shows. Practitioners advising heirs or purchasers tracing title through such an intestacy should treat a widow's Section 3(2) share as having ripened into her absolute property by the time the 1956 Act came into force, without needing to locate a partition deed, a decree, or evidence of her exclusive possession — and should advise clients accordingly rather than assuming, as the trial and first appellate courts here did, that an unexercised right to partition simply lapses on the widow's death in favour of a surviving son.

For conveyancing due diligence, the practical consequence is to widen, not narrow, the class of instruments capable of passing good title: a will executed by a widow whose husband died intestate in this period, disposing of what looks on paper like an unpartitioned share of joint family property, may nonetheless convey a valid absolute title, and a purchaser from her legatee — as Defendant No. 3 was here — can rely on it even where no partition was ever formalised. Conversely, sons and other coparceners who assume that a stepmother's or mother's failure to formally partition preserves their own reversionary expectation should be advised that this assumption is legally unsound after *Ganapati*: the statutory share vested and matured regardless.

The case also illustrates a recurring evidentiary trap for defendants pleading oral partition: both the trial court and the first appellate court rejected the plea for want of particulars — no date, no form, no independent witness beyond hearsay from a daughter recounting what her mother had told her. Counsel pleading an oral partition in any property suit should plead and prove it with the specificity these courts expected, since a bare assertion invites exactly this rejection; here, the point ultimately did not matter because the widow's independent statutory right rendered proof of partition unnecessary, but litigants should not assume that fallback will always be available on materially different facts.

Finally, being marked non-reportable, the judgment carries less formal precedential weight than a reported decision and does not purport to lay down new law; it applies existing doctrine to a fresh fact pattern. Practitioners should cite it, if at all, as an application of *Tulasamma* and *Raghubar Singh* to Section 3(2) devolution rather than as freestanding authority, and continue to anchor arguments on this point in those reported decisions.

## 5. Conclusion

*Ganapati v. Prabhakar* answers, for the first time in terms this clear, a question the maintenance-right cases had left latent: a Hindu widow's statutory half-share on her husband's pre-1956 intestate death under Section 3(2) of the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937 counts as a pre-existing right within the meaning of *Tulasamma* and *Raghubar Singh*, so that it matures into her absolute ownership under Section 14(1) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 without any further step of partition or proof that she possessed the property as its owner. For the parties, the effect was to uphold, after thirty-eight years of litigation across four fora since the suit was filed in 1988, both a widow's will made in 1984 and a sale made in 1995 in reliance on the title that will created. For practitioners, the judgment supplies a workable answer for any client tracing or disputing title through an intestacy that fell in the gap between the 1937 and 1956 Acts — a gap that, as this case demonstrates, can still surface live disputes eight decades later.

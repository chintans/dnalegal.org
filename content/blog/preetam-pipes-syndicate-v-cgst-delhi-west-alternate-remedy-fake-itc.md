**Quick answer:** On 23 September 2026, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Anil Kshetarpal and Bharat Parashar, JJ.) declined to entertain writ petitions filed by noticees named at serial numbers 614–615 and 299–301 of a common Order-in-Original dated 26 December 2025, passed against 629 firms and individuals over an alleged fake-invoicing racket run through 107 dummy firms. The petitioners argued that the adjudicating authority had not considered their individual replies to the show cause notice, and relied on the Supreme Court's decision in *M/s. ASP Traders v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors.* (2025 INSC 890) for the principle that an unreasoned quasi-judicial order defeats the statutory right of appeal. The Court held *ASP Traders* distinguishable — it concerned an order against a single assessee, not a common order against over six hundred noticees — and relegated the petitioners to their statutory appeal under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, for both the sufficiency of the order's engagement with their replies and the question of their actual role in the underlying transactions.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **A mass, multi-noticee adjudication order is not treated the same as an individual unreasoned order for natural-justice purposes.** The Delhi High Court held that *ASP Traders*' principle — that a taxpayer's unaddressed reply to a show cause notice defeats the right of appeal — does not automatically transfer to an order addressing 629 noticees across 1,881 pages.
- **The alternate-remedy bar in GST writs survives a general natural-justice argument that is not tied to specifics.** A bare assertion that individual submissions were not considered will not, without more, bring a case within the recognised exceptions to the bar on writ jurisdiction where a statutory appeal exists.
- **Disputed questions of fact — including a noticee's actual role in a fraud scheme — belong before the Appellate Authority, not a writ court.** The Delhi High Court expressly left both the sufficiency of the order's engagement with the petitioners' replies, and their factual role in the transactions, for appellate determination.
- **The scale and structure of the impugned order did the analytical work.** The distinction the Court drew was not that *ASP Traders* was wrongly decided or narrow in principle, but that a lengthy, reasoned order addressing a common fraudulent scheme across hundreds of noticees is not, on its face, the silent or template order that natural justice forbids.
- **The order carries no neutral citation and sits in Delhi High Court's "Orders" series rather than its reported "Judgments" series** — a reminder that not every disposition addressing a significant point of GST procedure is formally reported, and that its precedential weight should be assessed accordingly.

## 1. Introduction

When a single show cause notice names 629 firms and individuals, and the resulting adjudication order runs to 1,881 pages, the practical question for any one noticee is rarely whether the underlying allegation — fraudulent availment of input tax credit on the strength of invoices unsupported by any actual supply of goods — is correct. It is whether a High Court, sitting in its extraordinary writ jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution, will step in to examine that question at all, or will instead send the noticee to the statutory appellate track built into the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017.

On 23 September 2026, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court answered that question for two such noticees — M/s. Preetam Pipes Syndicate LLP and M/s. Sahuwala Exports Private Limited, each named at a specific serial number in a common Order-in-Original directed against 629 firms and individuals arising out of an alleged fake-invoicing racket run through 107 dummy firms. The petitioners argued that the adjudicating authority had never engaged with their individual replies to the show cause notice, and invoked the Supreme Court's decision in *M/s. ASP Traders v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors.* for the proposition that a quasi-judicial order which fails to record reasons on a taxpayer's submissions renders the statutory right of appeal illusory. The Delhi High Court declined to accept the analogy, distinguished *ASP Traders* on its facts, and relegated the petitioners to the appellate remedy under the CGST Act.

The order is unremarkable in one sense — courts routinely decline to entertain GST writs where a statutory appeal is available — and instructive in another: it shows how the increasingly common enforcement pattern of a single, mass, multi-noticee adjudication order interacts with the natural-justice jurisprudence that GST writ petitioners have built around individually reasoned orders. This article sets out the facts, situates the Court's reasoning against *ASP Traders* and the statutory scheme it invoked, and considers what the order means for practitioners advising clients caught up in a shared show cause notice.

## 2. Case summary and background

### The underlying investigation

The dispute traces back to a search operation conducted on 9 October 2024 at the office and residential premises of three individuals — Vikrant Singhal, Sachin Singhal and Pradeep Kumar — whom the Department alleged were engaged in arranging invoices without any corresponding supply of goods to a large number of recipient firms. The search yielded invoices, e-way bills and transporters' bills, together with cash of ₹22,96,550 seized from the premises of two of the three individuals. On the Department's case, the three masterminded a racket involving the creation of 107 fake firms through which fictitious purchase and sale invoices were generated and circulated, enabling downstream recipients to avail input tax credit that did not correspond to any real movement of goods.

A show cause notice dated 30 June 2025 followed, addressed collectively to the firms and individuals said to be implicated in the chain. The Additional Commissioner, CGST, Delhi West, as adjudicating authority, passed a common Order-in-Original on 26 December 2025 — later rectified on 25 June 2026 — against as many as 629 firms and individuals, running to 1,881 pages in the paper book placed before the Court.

### The petitioners' position

M/s. Preetam Pipes Syndicate LLP and its co-petitioner appear at serial numbers 614 and 615 of the Order-in-Original; M/s. Sahuwala Exports Private Limited and its co-petitioners appear at serial numbers 299 to 301. Each filed a writ petition before the Delhi High Court — W.P.(C) 4991/2026 and W.P.(C) 5448/2026 respectively, together with connected miscellaneous applications — seeking to quash the Order-in-Original as it applied to them, along with other similarly situated petitioners who had filed connected writ petitions arising out of the same investigation and the same consolidated order.

Before the Division Bench, counsel for the petitioners did not dispute that a statutory appeal against the Order-in-Original was available. Instead, three submissions were advanced to justify bypassing that remedy: first, that no role had been attributed to the petitioners in the show cause notice itself; second, that the petitioners had filed detailed replies to the notice which the adjudicating authority had not considered while passing the Order-in-Original; and third, that the petitioners were not in fact involved in the alleged racket and that their cases ought to have been examined separately rather than swept into a common order.

### The issue framed and the holding

The short question before the Court was whether these submissions — particularly the claim that individual replies had gone unconsidered — justified entertaining the writ petitions notwithstanding the availability of a statutory appeal. Counsel anchored the argument in paragraph 18 of the Supreme Court's decision in *ASP Traders*, delivered on 24 July 2025, for the proposition that where a taxpayer files a reply to a show cause notice, the adjudicating authority must consider it and render a reasoned, speaking order, failing which the statutory right of appeal is rendered illusory. The Court held that *ASP Traders* was "clearly distinguishable on facts," since it concerned an order passed against a single, individual assessee and did not address a common order passed against more than 600 firms and individuals. Whether the petitioners' replies had in fact been considered, and what precise role each played in the underlying transactions, were, in the Court's view, matters properly examined by the appellate authority rather than by a writ court. Concluding that the case involved detailed and disputed questions of fact unsuited to writ adjudication, the Bench declined to exercise its jurisdiction under Article 226 and relegated the petitioners to their statutory remedy of appeal, disposing of both writ petitions — and the connected matters arising out of the same investigation — in those terms.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory scheme the order sits within

The Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 builds a complete appellate mechanism around an adjudication order: an aggrieved person may appeal to the Appellate Authority under Section 107 of the Act, and from there to the GST Appellate Tribunal and the courts beyond. That structure ordinarily forecloses recourse to a High Court's writ jurisdiction under Article 226, which Indian courts have long treated as a discretionary remedy to be withheld where an equally efficacious statutory alternative exists — subject to well-recognised exceptions for breach of natural justice, want of jurisdiction, or a challenge to the vires of the provision itself. The order under discussion does not purport to disturb that framework; it applies it. What makes the case worth close attention is not the existence of the alternate-remedy bar — which is settled and uncontroversial — but the specific natural-justice argument the petitioners used in an attempt to bring themselves within its exception, and why the Court found that argument did not survive contact with the facts of a mass adjudication order.

### 3.2 What *ASP Traders* actually decided, and why the Court found it inapposite

*ASP Traders* arose out of a wholly different fact pattern: a consignment of arecanut detained in transit, a show cause notice issued under Section 129(3) of the CGST Act for release of the goods, and a taxpayer who paid the demanded amount under protest while continuing to dispute the levy. The Allahabad High Court had held that payment under Section 129(5) brought the proceeding to a "deemed conclusion," so that no further order, and no reasons, needed to be recorded. The Supreme Court reversed that view. Writing for the Bench, R. Mahadevan, J. held, at paragraph 18, that "the principles of natural justice mandate that when a taxpayer submits a response to a show cause notice, the adjudicating authority is required to consider such response and render a reasoned, speaking order," because the statutory right of appeal under Section 107 "is predicated upon the existence of a formal adjudication," and an appeal can lie only against an order — a reasoned one. In support, the Court drew on its earlier decision in *Kranti Associates (P) Ltd. v. Masood Ahmed Khan* (2010) 9 SCC 496, which catalogued why recording reasons is now treated as inseparable from natural justice in judicial, quasi-judicial and administrative decision-making. On that footing, the Supreme Court set aside the High Court's refusal to direct a reasoned order and directed the proper officer to pass one, in Form GST MOV-09, within one month.

The principle *ASP Traders* establishes — that a bare, unreasoned disposal of a taxpayer's submissions defeats the statutory right of appeal — is a general one, capable in principle of extending well beyond Section 129 detention cases to any GST adjudication. That is precisely why the Preetam Pipes petitioners invoked it: if failing to engage with a taxpayer's reply is itself a natural-justice defect exposing the order to writ interference, the argument runs, then a taxpayer need not wait for the appellate authority to notice the defect — a writ court can strike it down at the threshold. The Delhi High Court's answer was not to reject the principle but to hold that the petitioners had not shown it was engaged on these facts. *ASP Traders* concerned a single order against a single assessee, where the question of whether that assessee's specific submissions were addressed was capable of a clean, individualised answer from the face of the order. Here, by contrast, the Order-in-Original addresses 629 firms and individuals across 1,881 pages, following what the Court described as an examination of statements of various persons, together with the accounts, invoices and transactions of various firms, and contains an express finding identifying Vikrant Singhal, Sachin Singhal and others as the masterminds behind the creation of 107 fake firms. Whether that lengthy, consolidated document in fact considered — even if compendiously rather than noticee-by-noticee — the specific replies filed by parties at serial numbers 614–615 and 299–301, and what each of those parties' actual role was said to be, are not questions a writ court found itself well placed to resolve on affidavit evidence. They are, on the Court's reasoning, questions of degree and of fact that the appellate authority, empowered to reappraise both the record and the reasoning below, is institutionally better positioned to answer.

### 3.3 The scale of the order as the operative distinction

The Court's distinction is worth making explicit, because it is doing real analytical work rather than simply gesturing at the alternate-remedy bar in the abstract. A show cause notice and adjudication order naming 629 firms and individuals is not merely a large version of an individual order; it reflects a deliberate enforcement choice by the Department to treat a fraud investigation — premised on a common set of facts about fictitious firms and circulated invoices — as a single proceeding rather than 629 separate ones. That choice has an inherent trade-off. It allows the Department to establish, once, the common factual foundation without relitigating it for every recipient; but it necessarily means that the order's treatment of any single noticee's individual defence — that a particular firm neither knew of nor participated in the fraud — will be less granular than an order confined to that firm alone would be. The petitioners' argument, in effect, asked the Court to treat that inherent feature of a consolidated proceeding as itself a natural-justice defect sufficient to open the door to writ jurisdiction for every noticee dissatisfied with the depth of the order's engagement with their case. The Court's refusal to do so preserves the distinction between a case where reasons are simply absent — the vice identified in *ASP Traders* — and a case where reasons exist, address the common transactional scheme at length, and are said by an individual noticee to be insufficiently particularised to their own position. The latter, the Court held, is a matter of appellate re-examination, not writ-stage nullification.

### 3.4 What the Court did not decide

The order does not engage with, and expressly leaves open for the appellate authority, the merits of the underlying allegation: whether the input tax credit availed by the petitioners was in fact ineligible, whether the petitioners had any actual role in the invoice-trading scheme, and what, if anything, in their specific replies to the show cause notice the Order-in-Original addressed. Nor does the order pass on the correctness of treating 629 noticees within a single adjudication as a matter of administrative law more generally; it disposes of the writ petitions strictly on the ground that the facts here do not bring the case within the natural-justice exception recognised in *ASP Traders*, leaving that precedent's scope for future cases involving smaller, more individualised common orders to be worked out as they arise. The order also does not carry a neutral citation and is listed among the Delhi High Court's "Orders" — a category distinct from its reported "Judgments" — so its authority for later cases will rest on the persuasiveness of its reasoning rather than on any binding, reported status.

## 4. Practical significance

For GST practitioners, the order is a caution against over-reading *ASP Traders* as a general licence to bypass the appellate remedy whenever a taxpayer's reply to a show cause notice does not appear to have been engaged with line by line. The Delhi High Court's distinction turns on the structure of the adjudication order under challenge: a bare or template order disposing of an individual noticee's submissions without any reasoning at all remains vulnerable to the *ASP Traders* principle, but a lengthy, consolidated order addressing a common scheme across hundreds of noticees is unlikely to be treated, at the writ stage, as suffering from the same defect merely because it does not separately catalogue each noticee's individual defence. Counsel acting for a client caught up in a multi-party fake-invoicing show cause notice should therefore assume, as a starting position, that the statutory appeal is the forum in which the sufficiency of the order's engagement with that client's specific reply will be tested — and should build the appeal record accordingly, rather than treating a writ petition as a first resort.

That has consequences for how such an appeal is prepared. Because the Court has left open, for the Appellate Authority, both whether the petitioners' replies were considered and what their actual role in the transactions was said to be, an appeal memorandum should address both questions squarely: it should identify, where possible, exactly where in a lengthy Order-in-Original the appellant's specific case is or is not addressed, rather than relying on a general assertion that individual submissions were overlooked, since a writ court has now indicated that such a general assertion will not, on its own, unlock Article 226 jurisdiction against a mass order of this kind. Where an appellant genuinely transacted with one or more of the fake firms but disputes knowledge or complicity, the appeal is also the stage to press documentary evidence — invoices, e-way bills, payment trails — going to bona fide receipt of goods, since the writ court has expressly left that factual enquiry for the appellate forum.

The order is also a reminder, for compliance teams rather than litigators, of the exposure that flows from transacting with counterparties later found to be part of an invoice-trading network, however unwittingly: a recipient firm's own tax position can become entangled in a proceeding of this scale well after the transactions in question have closed, and the notice may arrive years later — the search here was conducted in October 2024 and the show cause notice followed only in June 2025. Maintaining verifiable proof of actual receipt of goods, not merely invoice and payment documentation, remains the most durable defence against being drawn into such a proceeding at all.

Finally, for anyone drafting or reviewing a writ petition challenging a GST adjudication order on natural-justice grounds, the order sharpens the pleading burden: a bare invocation of *ASP Traders* will not suffice where the impugned order is long, reasoned in relation to the common scheme, and directed against a large class of noticees. The petition must demonstrate, with specificity, that the order's treatment of the petitioner's own submissions is genuinely and demonstrably absent — not merely compendious — to distinguish itself from the class of case this order has now placed outside the *ASP Traders* exception.

## 5. Conclusion

The Delhi High Court's order in *Preetam Pipes Syndicate* does not narrow the natural-justice principle the Supreme Court articulated in *ASP Traders*; it draws a line around its application. A quasi-judicial order that says nothing at all about a taxpayer's reply remains open to challenge for want of reasons, consistently with the Supreme Court's insistence that the statutory right of appeal cannot be rendered illusory by an authority's silence. But an order that runs to nearly two thousand pages, addresses a common fraudulent scheme involving over six hundred noticees at length, and identifies the individuals said to be behind it, is not, without more, in the same position — and a writ court is entitled to say so and send the noticee to the appellate forum Parliament has provided. For firms and individuals swept into large, consolidated GST fraud proceedings, the practical lesson is to treat the statutory appeal, not the writ petition, as the primary battleground on which the sufficiency of the adjudicating authority's engagement with their individual case will have to be won. Until the GST Appellate Tribunal and higher courts have occasion to test how far a consolidated order's general reasoning can substitute for individualised engagement with each noticee's defence, that is the safer and, on this order, the only available course.

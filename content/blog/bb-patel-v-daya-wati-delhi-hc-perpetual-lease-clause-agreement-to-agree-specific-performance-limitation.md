**Quick answer:** On 28 September 2026 the Delhi High Court (Neena Bansal Krishna J.) held that a covenant to execute "another lease deed" on unsettled terms is an unenforceable agreement to agree, that such an option cannot lie dormant for 25 years, and that a declaration of the rate of rent falls outside Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963.

---

## 1. Introduction

Commercial tenancies in central Delhi often outlive the people who signed them. Rents stay frozen, and an old clause promising a "perpetual" lease sits in a drawer until a landlord's notice makes it valuable. This judgment asks what such a clause is worth when finally invoked.

The tenant in this case had occupied the second floor of 48 Janpath, New Delhi, since 1 March 1976 under an agreement for 11 months at Rs 221 a month. The agreement contained a clause under which the lessors would execute "another lease deed", which could be a perpetual one, at the lessee's option. The tenant first demanded that lease in April 2002 and sued in November 2004. The trial court rejected his plaint under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC), the first appellate court agreed, and the High Court has now confirmed both orders in a regular second appeal under Section 100 CPC.

The judgment matters beyond its facts for three reasons. It separates a lease from a promise to grant one, using the Supreme Court's renewal-covenant cases. It treats the passage of time as fatal to an option with no stated outer limit. And it holds that a declaration of the quantum of rent concerns a pecuniary obligation that Section 34 does not reach. This article sets out the facts and holdings, analyses each strand and its weak points, and then draws out the consequences for drafting and litigation.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 The agreement and the suit

The appellant, Shri B.B. Patel, a partner in M/s Nicholian Inc. (earlier his proprietorship, Nicholian Bros.), was inducted into the second floor of 48 Janpath under an agreement dated 1 March 1976 with the late Shri Murari Lal and the defendants. The defendants later became co-owners of the property under Murari Lal's Will dated 22 November 1986.

The term was 11 months from 1 March 1976 at Rs 221 a month. Clause 12 provided that the lessee would hold and enjoy the premises "in perpetuity" on observing the covenants. Clause 16 is the operative provision, reproduced in full by the Court (para 72):

> "That it is agreed by the lessor that he would execute another lease deed, may be a perpetual lease deed or any other lease deed for the period at the option of the lessee after or before the expiry of the period of lease of this lease deed. For writing the lease deed of the type mentioned in this para, permission is required to be taken from competent authority under the Vacant Land Ceiling Act, 1976. The lessors hereby agree to apply for obtaining such permission and all expenses in that respect such as Advocate fee, stamps, clerkage shall be paid by the lessee."

The tenant stayed in possession after February 1977. On his pleaded case the rent stayed at Rs 221, while he paid lump sums for electricity and water that rose over the years to Rs 2,000 a month. On 23 February 2002 the landlords claimed rent of Rs 2,200 a month under Section 6A of the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958. The tenant replied on 8 April 2002, calling for a perpetual lease deed within a month, and sent a further notice on 1 August 2002. In his suit (No. 342 of 2004) he sought specific performance of Clause 16, a declaration that the rent was Rs 221 a month plus statutory enhancement, and an injunction against any higher demand.

### 2.2 The proceedings below

On the landlords' application under Order VII Rule 11 CPC, the Civil Judge rejected the plaint on 9 July 2012. The Judge held that Clause 16 should have been enforced when the term expired in 1977, that a 2002 notice could not revive it, and that a declaration of the rate of rent was not maintainable in a civil court given the Delhi Rent Control Act. The Additional District Judge dismissed the tenant's appeal on 5 May 2014 (RCA No. 29/2012).

### 2.3 Questions in the second appeal

On 6 January 2016 the High Court framed three substantial questions of law: limitation; whether the plaint could be rejected on that ground under Order VII Rule 11; and whether rejection of prayers (a) and (b) under Section 50 of the Delhi Rent Control Act was proper.

### 2.4 The holdings

The Court answered against the tenant on every point:

1. Clause 16 was no more than an agreement to agree. It fixed no term, rent or other essential condition and supplied no yardstick, so it could not be specifically enforced (paras 73–99).
2. Even if enforceable, the option had to be exercised within a reasonable time after the 1977 expiry. A gap of over 25 years was not reasonable, and the 2002 notice could not start a fresh period of limitation under Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963 (paras 100–126).
3. A declaration of the rate of rent is not a declaration of "legal character" or of a "right as to any property" under Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act. The Court therefore upheld rejection of prayer (b) on that footing, and expressly said it might not be correct to treat the suit as barred by Section 50 of the Delhi Rent Control Act (paras 128–154). The injunction fell with the declaration (para 164).

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 A promise to grant a lease is not a lease

The Court's central move was to separate Clause 12 from Clause 16. The tenant read the two together as showing an intention to create a lease in perpetuity. The Court read them harmoniously, but to the opposite effect. Clause 16 contemplated "another lease deed", which would be pointless if the 1976 agreement had already created a perpetual lease (para 89). It followed that the perpetual lease could come into existence only through an executed and registered instrument under Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and none was ever made (para 90).

That moved the claim from the law of leases into the law of contract. The tenant was seeking to enforce "a covenant to enter into a future arrangement" rather than a completed contract (para 73). The Court relied on a line of authority that is well settled and that practitioners should have to hand:

- *Hitkarini Sabha, Jabalpur v. Corporation of the City of Jabalpur*, (1972) 3 SCC 325: a renewal clause "on such terms and conditions as may be agreed" is too uncertain to enforce (para 75).
- *Shanti Prasad Devi v. Shankar Mahto*, (2005) 5 SCC 543, and *Naveen Chand v. Nagarjuna Travels & Hotels Ltd.*, (2002) 6 SCC 331: renewal rights whose terms were left to later agreement were "uncertain and incapable of specific performance" and "shrouded in uncertainty and vagueness" (paras 80–83).
- *Khivraj Chordia v. Esso Standard Eastern Inc.*, AIR 1975 Mad 374: the distinction between a renewal covenant that supplies an objective yardstick for future terms and one that leaves them at large (paras 86–87).

The last case supplies the working test. A covenant survives if its terms are "capable of being made certain" under Section 29 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and fails if the essential terms are left entirely for future agreement. Clause 16 left the type of lease (perpetual or fixed), its duration and the rent to be determined later, with no formula and no mechanism (para 97). On that footing, the Court's conclusion follows from authority and is hard to resist.

The Court added the distinction in *State of U.P. v. Lalji Tandon*, (2004) 1 SCC 1, between an extension, which operates under the existing instrument, and a renewal, which needs a fresh deed. Continued possession does not supply the missing instrument; occupation without it continues as a month-to-month tenancy (paras 91–96). The tenant's long possession proved a tenancy but not a perpetual one.

One point of precision: the landlords also argued that the 1976 agreement was inadmissible for want of stamp duty and registration (paras 26, 58), but the Court's reasoning does not turn on admissibility. It refers to Section 107 only to explain when a perpetual lease could come into existence.

### 3.2 An option without an outer limit is not an option without a time limit

Having held that there was no enforceable contract, the Court said that the question of computing limitation for enforcing it "would not arise" (para 99). It then examined limitation on the alternative assumption that Clause 16 was enforceable. This part of the judgment is the most useful for litigators, because it addresses the usual answer to a limitation objection.

The tenant relied on the second limb of Article 54 of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963: where no date is fixed for performance, time runs from when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. He cited *Panchanan Dhara v. Monmatha Nath Maity*, (2006) 5 SCC 340, and *Urvashiben v. Krishnakant Manuprasad Trivedi*, (2019) 13 SCC 372, for the proposition that the date of refusal is a mixed question that cannot be decided on an Order VII Rule 11 application (paras 52–53, 114).

The Court answered in two steps. First, it treated Clause 16 as an option exercisable within a reasonable time, relying on *Caltex (India) Ltd. v. Bhagwan Devi Marodia*, AIR 1969 SC 405, *Manjunath Anandappa v. Tammanasa*, (2003) 10 SCC 390, *Shikha Misra v. S. Krishnamurthy*, 2014 SCC OnLine Del 1565, and *DDA v. Ram Prakash*, (2011) 4 SCC 180, which it treated as a statutory-power case that "reinforces the principle" instead of governing directly (paras 102–107). Its conclusion was direct: "An option attached to a lease of merely 11 months, cannot be kept dormant for twenty-five years and thereafter, treated as enforceable" (para 110).

Second, it refused to let the formal demand set the clock. If the cause of action arose only on the 2002 notice, the tenant could postpone limitation indefinitely by postponing the notice (para 118). It also noted the absurdity of a perpetual lease in 2002 on terms "commensurate with those existing in the year 1977" (para 111).

The Court rejected two further arguments. The tenant said both sides were awaiting clearance under the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976, repealed in 1999; but the plaint did not aver that any application was ever made, Clause 16 itself required the tenant to call on the lessors to apply, and even from repeal the suit came more than five years later (paras 119–121). And limitation was not a mixed question needing evidence, since every relevant fact came from the plaint and the plaintiff's own documents (para 122).

**Critique.** The reasoning is persuasive on the facts, but two points deserve attention. First, the Court alternates between two yardsticks: a "reasonable time" (paras 109–110) and "at best, within three years" of February 1977 (paras 112, 116). These are not identical. A reasonable time for an option is a different inquiry from the three-year period in Article 54, and the judgment does not say which one it treats as controlling. The result is the same on either view, but later courts may be asked to choose. Second, the judgment meets *Panchanan Dhara* and *Urvashiben* through the nature of the covenant, which works only because the Court had already found no concluded contract. Where a covenant is certain and no date is fixed for performance, the second limb of Article 54 keeps its ordinary force.

### 3.3 Declarations, rent and Section 34

The third strand concerns prayer (b). Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 allows a declaration in favour of a person "entitled to any legal character, or to any right as to any property". The Court held that the rate of rent is neither.

On "legal character", it relied on *Sri Mahalinga Thambiran Swamigal v. Sri La Sri Kasivasi Arulnandi Thambiran Swamigal*, (1974) 1 SCC 150, which separates status from its incidents. The tenant's status was admitted; only an incident, the amount payable, was disputed (paras 131–134). On "right as to any property", rent is a financial obligation arising from the tenancy, and attaching a monetary liability to immovable property does not make it a right as to property (paras 135–136). The Court drew on decisions refusing declarations that touch only the pecuniary relationship of the parties, including *Mahabir Jute Mills v. Firm Kedar Nath Ram Bharose*, AIR 1960 All 254, *M/s Kular Construction Ltd. v. Gurnam Singh*, 2019 SCC OnLine Del 6976, and *State of M.P. v. Khan Bahadur H.H.D.H. Bhiwandiwala & Co.*, AIR 1971 MP 65 (paras 137–151).

Two features stand out. The Court did not rest on Section 50 of the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958, on which the Civil Judge had relied. It observed that "it may not be correct to say" that the suit is barred by Section 50, and held the question otiose once the declaration failed under Section 34 (paras 152–153). It also did not decide who may fix the rent, so the judgment leaves open the forum question under the Rent Control Act. It is authority on Section 34, not on the exclusivity of the Rent Controller.

Alternatively on the merits, relying on *Karnani Properties Ltd. v. Augustine*, AIR 1957 SC 309, it held that "rent" is comprehensive enough to cover payments for amenities and services. Since the plaint itself admitted payment of Rs 2,000 a month, and the tenant's own reply of 8 April 2002 enclosed a cheque computed at the rates he disputed, the declaration was untenable even on the plaint's own averments (paras 155–163).

The Section 34 holding carries a practical risk: a tenant who says the landlord has inflated the rent may find a bare declaration unavailable, leaving him to defend a recovery or eviction claim or to use the rent control statute's own machinery.

## 4. Practical significance

**Drafting renewal and perpetual lease covenants.** A landlord or tenant who wants a long term should create it in the instrument itself and register it. If an option is the commercial deal, it must state the term, the rent or a formula for fixing it (for example, an escalation percentage or a reference to a named valuation method), and the manner of exercise. Words such as "on terms to be agreed" will defeat enforcement under *Hitkarini Sabha* and *Shanti Prasad Devi*. The option should also carry an express exercise window. Where it does not, the Court's reasoning will supply a reasonable-time limit, and that limit may be short.

**Tenants.** Possession is not exercise. A lessee holding an option must serve a written exercise notice within the window or a reasonable time, and sue promptly after refusal. Waiting for a dispute before invoking the clause is dangerous, and the tenant's own pleadings and correspondence, such as rent admitted or cheques tendered, are scrutinised at the threshold.

**Landlords.** Order VII Rule 11 works where the plaintiff's own pleaded documents show the bar. The method is to build the chronology from the plaint and its documents and show that no evidence is needed. It will not work where the pleaded facts leave genuine doubt about when performance was refused.

**Property buyers and lenders.** A purchaser told that a tenant holds a "perpetual lease" should ask for the registered instrument. An unregistered covenant to grant one is a weak encumbrance on this reasoning, though it evidences a tenancy.

**Litigation strategy on rent.** A claim framed as a Section 34 declaration of the amount of rent now meets a direct Delhi High Court holding against it. Counsel should plead a relief the rent control statute recognises instead.

**Limits of the decision.** This is a single-judge decision on one covenant and the plaint's own averments. It binds trial courts in Delhi and persuades elsewhere.

## 5. Conclusion

*B.B. Patel v. Daya Wati* decides that a promise to execute a lease later is not itself a lease and cannot be enforced as one unless it fixes, or supplies a way to fix, the terms of the lease. It adds that an option tied to a short tenancy cannot be revived by a notice served a quarter of a century later, and that a bare declaration of the rent payable is not available under Section 34.

The strongest part of the reasoning is the first. It follows a consistent line of Supreme Court authority on uncertain renewal covenants and applies it to a clause that was uncertain on every term. The limitation reasoning reaches a sound result but would benefit from a clearer statement of whether the governing test is reasonableness or three years. The Section 34 holding is orthodox, but it leaves open the forum question under the Delhi Rent Control Act and may disadvantage tenants who have no other route to test an inflated rent.

For practitioners the lesson is practical. Contract for the term you intend, register it, and give an option a clock and a mechanism. A tenancy held for decades is evidence of long occupation, and it does not create rights that were never documented.

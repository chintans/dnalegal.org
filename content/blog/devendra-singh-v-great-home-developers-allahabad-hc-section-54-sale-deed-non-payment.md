**Quick answer:** In *Devendra Singh v. Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd.*, decided on 25 September 2026, the Allahabad High Court dismissed a second appeal by a landowner seeking cancellation of a registered sale deed on the ground that the purchaser's cheques towards the balance price had been dishonoured. Applying Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the Supreme Court's decisions in *Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali* and *Raziya Begum v. Nafisa Begum*, the Court held that actual payment of the full price is not a precondition to a completed, registered sale; a seller left unpaid must sue for the money, not for cancellation of the deed that already transferred title and possession.

## 1. Introduction

Few disputes recur in Indian property litigation as often as the one at the heart of *Devendra Singh v. Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd.*: a seller who has executed and registered a sale deed, allowed the buyer's name to be mutated in the revenue records, and then — years later, once the buyer's payments falter or a better price becomes available elsewhere — turns to the civil court asking that the deed itself be declared void. The Allahabad High Court's judgment of 25 September 2026, dismissing a second appeal from a Hapur landowner on precisely this fact pattern, is a compact and useful restatement of why that strategy almost always fails once a sale deed has been validly registered.

The appellant, Devendra Singh, had sold agricultural land in Hapur district to Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd. under a registered sale deed executed in December 2008. He later claimed that the price actually agreed was far higher than the price recorded in the deed, that it was to be paid in monthly instalments against delivery of possession only on full payment, and that the cheques tendered towards that higher price had bounced, leaving a substantial balance unpaid. On that basis he sued for cancellation of the sale deed. Both the trial court and the first appellate court rejected his case on the facts, and the High Court, hearing a second appeal confined to a single substantial question of law — whether a sale deed can be declared void under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 if the consideration is found not to have been paid — answered that question against him.

This article sets out the facts as recorded in the judgment, the Court's reasoning and the authorities it relied on, and what the decision means for parties who structure land sales around deferred or instalment payment, and for anyone assessing title where a seller's payment grievance survives long after the conveyance itself.

## 2. Case summary and background

Devendra Singh claimed ownership of a one-third share, measuring 3.3400 hectares, in several khasra plots of agricultural land at village Hasanpur Lodha, Tehsil Dhaulana, District Hapur, Uttar Pradesh. On 10 December 2008 he executed a registered sale deed in favour of Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd., registered before the Sub-Registrar, Hapur. The deed itself recited a sale consideration of Rs. 2,13,76,000, computed at Rs. 800 per square metre for the 33,400 square metres conveyed, on which stamp duty of Rs. 10,70,000 had been paid. Critically, the deed also recited that the entire consideration — three cheques of Rs. 70 lakh each drawn on the Bank of India, Noida, plus Rs. 3,76,000 in cash — had already been received, that possession of the land had been delivered to the purchaser on the spot, that all rights of ownership had passed in full, and that nothing remained payable or would be considered payable in future.

In 2011, Devendra Singh filed Original Suit No. 360 of 2011 before the Civil Judge (Junior Division)-II, Hapur, seeking cancellation of that very sale deed. His case, as pleaded, was that the true bargain had been struck orally at Rs. 44,25,000 per bigha, yielding a total price of Rs. 5,84,24,750, to be paid over twenty monthly instalments, with possession to follow only after the full price was paid. He alleged that the cheques tendered by the purchaser — some drawn on a related company, Opus Buildtech Pvt. Ltd., and signed by one Vaibhav Agrawal — were repeatedly dishonoured for insufficient funds, and that of the agreed price only about Rs. 2,13,20,000 had actually reached him, leaving a balance of roughly Rs. 3.71 crore unpaid. On this footing he asked the court to declare the registered sale deed null and void, characterising it as an instrument obtained without real payment and, in substance, by fraud.

Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd. resisted the suit on a fundamentally different account of the transaction. It denied that any higher price had ever been agreed, denied that possession was to follow payment rather than precede or coincide with it, and relied squarely on the terms of the registered deed: a price of Rs. 800 per square metre, full receipt of consideration, and delivery of possession on the date of registration itself. It also pointed out that the plaintiff had allowed mutation of the purchaser's name in the revenue records in January 2009 without raising any objection, and that if the plaintiff had any genuine grievance about non-payment, his remedies lay in a civil suit for recovery of money or a criminal complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 for dishonour of cheques — a complaint the plaintiff had, in fact, already filed separately.

At trial, the plaintiff's own testimony proved central to the outcome. In cross-examination, he admitted that he could not recall the khasra numbers of his own land without referring to documents, that he had received Rs. 2.10 to 2.20 crore under the sale deed, that he had not objected to the mutation of the purchaser's name because "there was no dispute" at the time, and — most damagingly — that "the dispute is of money only and there is no dispute of sale deed." A supporting witness, who claimed the true oral price was the higher figure and that possession had never in fact passed to the purchaser, could not recall the khasra numbers either and gave an account that the trial court evidently found unconvincing against the documentary record. The Civil Judge dismissed the suit in May 2018; the first appellate court, the Additional District and Sessions Judge/FTC-I, Hapur, dismissed the first appeal in November 2025, upholding the trial court's findings in full. Devendra Singh then filed Second Appeal No. 71 of 2026 before the Allahabad High Court, which was admitted on the single substantial question set out above and decided by a learned Single Judge on 25 September 2026.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Section 54: payment is not a precondition to a completed sale

The Court's analysis begins, appropriately, with the text of Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, which defines "sale" as "a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised or part-paid and part-promised," and which requires that a sale of tangible immovable property valued at Rs. 100 or more be made "only by a registered instrument." The words "paid or promised or part-paid and part-promised" are doing real work here: the statute contemplates, on its face, a sale in which the price is wholly deferred, wholly paid, or paid in part with the balance merely promised. Nothing in the definition makes actual, complete payment at the time of execution a condition for the sale's validity.

The Court adopted, in substance, the reasoning of the Supreme Court's 2020 decision in *Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali (Gajra)*, (2020) 7 SCC 366, which in turn drew on the earlier authority of *Vidyadhar v. Manikrao*, (1999) 3 SCC 573, for the proposition that actual payment of the whole price at the time of execution of a sale deed "is not a sine qua non for completion of the sale." Once the document is executed and registered, title passes to the transferee, and non-payment of part of the price does not affect the validity of the transfer. This Court also invoked the Supreme Court's more recent, non-reportable decision in *Raziya Begum v. Nafisa Begum Abdul Hamid*, 2026 INSC 814, where the Supreme Court, on closely analogous facts — a sale deed executed on receipt of part consideration with the balance left outstanding — held that "the sale deed executed with full knowledge of the part consideration alone having been passed, cannot be rendered void or inoperative merely because the balance consideration promised to be paid... had not been complied with," and that "the right of the plaintiffs was to file for recovery of balance sale consideration and not to seek declaration that the sale deeds were null and void." This article has independently verified both of these quotations against the text of *Dahiben* and *Raziya Begum* as retrieved from the Supreme Court's own reported record; the remaining authorities discussed below are reported here only as they appear relied upon within the Allahabad High Court's own judgment, which is the primary text this article is otherwise based on.

Applying that framework, the Allahabad High Court held that even taking the plaintiff's version of the facts at its highest — that a substantial part of the price remained unpaid — the remedy available to him was a civil suit for recovery of the balance consideration (or, as he had already pursued, a criminal complaint for dishonour of the cheques), not cancellation of a sale deed that had already achieved a completed conveyance. The Court accordingly answered the substantial question of law against the appellant: non-payment, even non-payment of a substantial part of the price, is not by itself a ground on which a completed, registered sale deed can be declared void under Section 54.

### 3.2 A registered instrument's recitals, and the limits of oral evidence against it

A second strand of the Court's reasoning turned on the evidentiary weight of the registered deed itself. The judgment invoked the presumption of correctness attaching to a document registered before a Sub-Registrar, tracing this, by reference to the Supreme Court's decision in *Ishwar Das Jain v. Sohan Lal*, to the endorsement made under Section 58 of the Registration Act, 1908 — a presumption rebuttable, the Court noted, only by strong evidence. Section 91 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 reinforces the same point from a different angle: where the terms of a disposition of property have been reduced to a document, no evidence of those terms may be given except the document itself (or admissible secondary evidence of it). Here, the plaintiff's own pleaded case — an oral price nearly three times the figure recorded in the deed, and an oral condition postponing possession until full payment — sat squarely outside what the registered deed itself recorded, and the plaintiff's supporting witness offered nothing beyond oral assertion to displace it.

Against that backdrop, the plaintiff's own admissions in cross-examination did much of the work for the defendant. Having conceded that "there was no dispute" over the deed's terms, that he raised no objection when the purchaser's name was mutated in the revenue records within weeks of registration, and that "the dispute is of money only," the plaintiff had effectively conceded the very point on which the case turned: that the conveyance itself was not in question, only the sufficiency of what he had been paid for it. The Court treated this as consistent with, and reinforcing, the burden-of-proof analysis it drew from the Supreme Court's older decision in *A. Raghavamma v. A. Chenchamma*, AIR 1964 SC 136, distinguishing the legal burden of proof — which, under Section 101 of the Evidence Act, remains fixed on the party who must prove a fact to succeed — from the evidential onus, which shifts as evidence comes in. A plaintiff seeking cancellation of a registered instrument carries the burden of showing why that instrument should not be given effect; on the facts found by two courts below, and left undisturbed by the High Court, Devendra Singh did not meet it.

### 3.3 The narrow compass of a second appeal under Section 100 CPC

The final element of the Court's reasoning is procedural rather than substantive, but it is consequential for how the case reached the outcome it did. Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 confines a second appeal to a "substantial question of law"; it is well established, and the judgment cites Allahabad's own precedent in *Laxmi Narain v. Smt. Hubraja alias Barki* for the point, that concurrent findings of fact recorded by the trial court and the first appellate court cannot be reopened in a second appeal merely because another view of the evidence might have been possible. Here, the trial court's finding — that the sale deed correctly recorded the parties' bargain and that the entire consideration had been received — was affirmed on first appeal, and the second appeal was admitted on a legal question rather than an invitation to re-weigh the oral evidence. The High Court's disposal accordingly proceeded on the facts as concurrently found, testing only whether those facts, taken at face value, could in law support cancellation under Section 54 — and, for the reasons above, held that they could not.

The judgment also records, in general terms and by reference to the Supreme Court's observations in *Maria Margarida Sequeira Fernandes v. Erasmo Jack De Sequeira*, a broader concern about property litigation being used strategically — false claims and false defences pressed in the hope that an opposing party will tire of the process and settle. That observation is offered as context for why courts scrutinise cancellation suits carefully rather than as an independent ground of decision, and this article treats it accordingly: as the judgment's own framing device, not as a finding specific to the conduct of either party in this case.

## 4. Practical significance

For conveyancing practice, the most direct lesson is about documentation discipline where a sale price is to be paid over time. A seller who genuinely intends possession to follow, rather than precede, full payment must say so in the registered instrument itself — as an explicit condition, ideally coupled with a mechanism such as an escrow, a mortgage back to the seller for the unpaid balance, or a conditional sale structure recognised under the Transfer of Property Act — rather than relying on an oral understanding that the deed's own recitals contradict. Once a deed recites full receipt of consideration and delivery of possession, a seller who later disputes those recitals bears a heavy evidentiary burden, reinforced by the statutory presumption attaching to registered instruments and by the exclusion of oral evidence to vary a document's terms.

Second, the judgment is a reminder that a seller who is genuinely unpaid retains a real remedy, just not the one the appellant sought here. A suit for recovery of the balance sale consideration, potentially secured by an unpaid vendor's lien or charge over the property where the facts support it, or a complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act for dishonoured cheques (as the appellant here separately pursued), each remain available; what is foreclosed is treating non-payment as a basis to unwind a completed conveyance and revest title in the seller by unilateral litigation. Buyers structuring instalment-based land purchases should recognise the corresponding advantage this doctrine gives them once a sale deed is registered: absent fraud vitiating the transaction itself, or a decree specifically obtained for rescission on recognised grounds, non-payment disputes will ordinarily be channelled into money claims rather than reopening title.

Third, for anyone conducting title due diligence, the case underscores the evidentiary importance of a clean, unbroken mutation record. The plaintiff's failure to object to mutation for roughly two and a half years featured prominently in the Court's assessment of his credibility, illustrating how promptly a genuine grievance over non-payment should be asserted — through revenue authorities, through a suit for recovery, or through a cheque-dishonour complaint — if it is to be taken seriously later. A long, unexplained silence after mutation, followed by a suit years later seeking cancellation, is a pattern courts are likely to view with the same scepticism shown here.

Finally, the case is a caution against pleading and litigation strategy that departs too far from the words of the parties' own registered instrument. Litigants and their counsel who frame a payment dispute as a challenge to the underlying conveyance, rather than as what it actually is — a claim for money — risk not only losing on the merits but inviting rejection at the threshold, since the Supreme Court's decision in *Dahiben*, on which this judgment draws, treated an analogous suit as liable to rejection under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 for disclosing no cause of action for the relief actually sought.

## 5. Conclusion

*Devendra Singh v. Great Home Developers Pvt. Ltd.* does not break new doctrinal ground; the proposition that a completed, registered sale is not undone by a subsequent failure to pay the full price has been settled law since at least *Vidyadhar v. Manikrao* and was restated authoritatively in *Dahiben*. Its value lies in showing that settled proposition doing routine, necessary work in the everyday reality of Indian land transactions, where sellers regularly discover, sometimes years after registration, that the price they were promised did not fully materialise. The Allahabad High Court's answer is consistent and, on this record, unsurprising: a registered sale deed reciting full payment and delivered possession carries a strong presumption of correctness, oral evidence and after-the-fact assertions will rarely displace it, and a seller's remedy for an unpaid balance is a claim for that money, not a decree unwinding the sale itself. For drafters, the lesson is to build payment contingencies into the instrument at the outset; for sellers facing a defaulting buyer, the lesson is to sue for what is actually owed, promptly, rather than for what the deed no longer permits a court to give.

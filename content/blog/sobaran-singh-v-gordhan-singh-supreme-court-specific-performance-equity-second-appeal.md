**Quick answer:** In *Sobaran Singh (Dead) Through Lrs. v. Gordhan Singh (Dead) Thr. Lrs.*, decided on 15 September 2026, the Supreme Court restored a trial court's 1979 decree of specific performance of a 1975 registered agreement to sell agricultural land near Agra. The Allahabad High Court, in second appeal, had itself found the trial court's findings correct and condemned the seller's defence as fabricated, yet it substituted the decreed sale with a cash payment of Rs. 15,00,000 "to maintain equity." The Supreme Court held that once the buyer's readiness and willingness stood affirmed and the sale had already been executed through the court in 1979 — with the buyer in unbroken possession for over four decades — there was no discretionary room left to unwind that completed transaction into compensation, and restored the original decree in full.

## 1. Introduction

Litigation over a single agreement to sell rarely outlives the generation that signed it, but *Sobaran Singh v. Gordhan Singh* did exactly that: an agreement executed in June 1975 produced a Supreme Court order more than fifty years later, by which time both named parties had died and their legal representatives carried on the fight. The case reached the Supreme Court on a narrow but recurring question in specific performance litigation — what happens when an appellate court, having agreed that the buyer proved everything the Specific Relief Act, 1963 required of him, nonetheless declines to give him the property and instead directs the seller to pay him money "to maintain equity"?

The Allahabad High Court, hearing a second appeal confined by law to substantial questions of law, upheld every finding the trial court had made in the buyer's favour, including its assessment that the seller's resistance to the suit was a "cooked-up defense." Yet, rather than affirm the decree those findings supported, it enhanced a modest advance-refund figure of Rs. 5,000 to Rs. 15,00,000, treating the case as a candidate for judicially engineered compensation rather than performance. The Supreme Court's order restoring the trial court's decree is short — it runs to six pages and was delivered as an "Order," not a lengthy judgment — but the reasoning it contains bears directly on how far an appellate court may go in re-shaping relief once it has stopped disputing the underlying facts, and on what "equity" can and cannot do to a sale that has already been carried into effect.

This article sets out the facts as the Supreme Court recorded them, examines the doctrinal basis for treating an affirmed decree of specific performance as something other than a bargaining chip for a later cash settlement, and considers what the ruling means for practitioners handling old specific performance suits still working their way through the appellate hierarchy under the pre-2018 discretionary regime.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

The dispute traced back to a registered agreement to sell dated 16 June 1975, under which the seller agreed to sell roughly five bighas of agricultural land near Agra — the remaining portion of a total seven-bigha holding, two bighas of which he had already sold to a third party who later testified as an attesting witness for the buyer. According to the trial court's findings, the buyer already owned adjoining land and wished to purchase the suit plot to consolidate his holding, while the seller, who was not personally cultivating the land and had been letting it out, wanted to sell because he needed money.

The buyer sued for specific performance. The trial court found the agreement duly proved through the buyer's own testimony and that of two attesting witnesses, rejected the seller's objection that government sanction was required because the land fell within an urban agglomeration (holding that the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 does not apply to agricultural land), and held that discretionary relief under Section 20 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 was "perfectly in order." It decreed specific performance on 28 February 1979, directing execution of the sale deed on payment of the balance consideration. What followed is procedurally unusual: acting on that decree, the buyer obtained the sale of the property through the court on 7 June 1979, and has remained in possession ever since — a fact the Supreme Court's order treats as settled and undisturbed throughout the litigation that came after.

The first appellate court reversed the trial court, holding that nothing had been produced to establish the buyer's readiness and willingness to perform his own obligations under the agreement — a statutory precondition to specific performance — and directed a refund of the advance with interest instead. The seller's legal representatives then carried the matter to the Allahabad High Court by way of Second Appeal No. 817 of 1998, which was decided on 27 May 2016. The High Court reinstated the trial court's view of the facts in full, finding that the trial court had correctly held in the buyer's favour and that the seller had mounted a fabricated defence to defeat the sale. Despite that finding, the High Court did not restore the decree of specific performance; instead, it directed the seller to pay the buyer Rs. 15,00,000 as "interest" on the original Rs. 5,000 advance — an outcome the Supreme Court's order describes as the High Court's attempt to "settle equities."

The seller's representatives brought the matter to the Supreme Court by special leave in 2016. The petition was, at one stage, referred to the Supreme Court Mediation Centre with the parties' consent, with instructions to report back within two months; the docket record does not indicate that mediation resolved the dispute, and the appeal proceeded to a final hearing before a bench of J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, JJ., which allowed it on 15 September 2026, restoring the trial court's decree and directing that the sum the seller had deposited pursuant to the High Court's order be refunded to the seller with accrued interest.

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 Readiness and willingness is a finding of fact, and the High Court had already affirmed it

Specific performance under the pre-2018 Specific Relief Act, 1963 required a plaintiff to plead and prove that he was, and remained, ready and willing to perform the essential terms of the contract that lay on his side — a requirement carried in Section 16(c) of the Act, both before and after the 2018 amendment. The first appellate court's reversal rested entirely on its view that this element was not made out. That is a finding of fact, and it is precisely the kind of finding a second appeal under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 is not meant to reopen except on a properly framed substantial question of law addressing an error in how the lower courts approached the evidence.

The Allahabad High Court, on second appeal, went the other way: it held that the trial court's original assessment — that readiness and willingness was established, and that the seller's opposition was contrived — was correct. Once that determination was made, the statutory precondition to specific performance stood satisfied precisely as the trial court had found it, and the discretionary threshold in Section 20 of the unamended Act, under which a court was "not bound to grant" specific performance merely because it was lawful to do so, had already been crossed in the buyer's favour by the trial court's own reasoning, which the High Court expressly approved. There was, at that point, no live factual dispute left for equity to operate on.

### 3.2 "Equity" is not a licence to unwind a sale that has already happened

The difficulty with the High Court's approach, as the Supreme Court's order makes plain, is not that appellate courts may never temper specific performance with compensation — the pre-amendment Section 20 regime was built around exactly that kind of discretion. It is that the discretion to decline specific performance in favour of compensation operates at the point of deciding what relief to grant, not as a tool for retrospectively dismantling a sale that has already been completed and given full legal effect. Here, the trial court's decree had not remained a paper entitlement: the buyer had obtained execution of the sale through the court itself in June 1979, and had held possession, on the Supreme Court's own account of the facts, for more than four decades by the time the appeal was finally heard. A finding that the buyer had correctly proved his case, coupled with an order requiring him to accept a lump sum in place of a sale that had already been legally accomplished forty-five years earlier, is not a compromise between competing equities; it is, in substance, an order compelling the buyer to surrender a completed conveyance and settled possession without either party having sought that outcome.

### 3.3 The reliance on *Pratap Lakshman Muchandi* and its limits

The order records — apparently referring to the High Court's reasoning, though the text as it appears attributes the reliance to "the Trial Court," an internal inconsistency in the judgment's own drafting that this article notes rather than resolves — reliance on *Pratap Lakshman Muchandi and Others v. Shamlal Uddavadas Wadhwa and Others*, (2008) 12 SCC 67. As the Supreme Court's order describes that decision, it involved an agreement executed in 1982 where, given the long lapse of time, the sale was permitted to proceed on an enhanced balance consideration — the original Rs. 1,10,000 increased by a further Rs. 5,00,000 — rather than at the historic contract price. That is a materially different exercise from what happened here: in the cited case, as described, the buyer was still required to complete the purchase, merely at an inflation-adjusted price, so that delay was priced into the transaction the buyer still received. In the present case, by contrast, there was no transaction left to price — the sale had already occurred in 1979 — and the High Court's order did not ask the buyer to pay more for anything; it took the property-based relief away from him altogether and substituted money he had not sought. Treating the two situations as interchangeable applications of "equity" conflated a case about adjusting the terms of a sale still to be completed with a case about reversing a sale already finished.

### 3.4 Decades of delay and possession weigh against reopening a completed sale, not for it

The Supreme Court's own framing — that "equity ... has to be applied in favour of the plaintiff who parted with Rs. 20,000/- more than four decades and a half back, and obtained the ownership and possession of the property" — inverts the assumption that seemed to animate the High Court's order, namely that the passage of decades since 1975 justified converting the buyer's entitlement into cash. On the Supreme Court's reasoning, the same passage of time, coupled with possession held without interruption since 1979 under a sale executed by the court's own process, is what makes restitution of the property — not compensation in its place — the equitable outcome. The order's direction that the sum the seller had deposited be refunded to the seller, rather than paid over to the buyer, reflects that logic: once the sale itself is restored, the substitute cash award has no further function.

## 4. Practical Significance

For litigators handling long-pending specific performance suits still governed by the pre-2018 discretionary regime, the decision is a caution against assuming that a court's sympathy for the sheer duration of the litigation can be converted into a claim for enhanced compensation once a plaintiff has actually proved his case. Delay by itself does not generate an equitable entitlement to buy the other side out of a decree; it is the underlying facts — whether readiness and willingness was proved, whether the resisting party's defence was genuine — that determine whether specific performance follows, and an appellate court that agrees with the trial court on those facts has correspondingly little room left to select a different remedy on free-standing equitable grounds.

For buyers who have obtained a favourable specific performance decree that is under appeal, the case underscores the practical value of promptly seeking execution where no stay has been granted. The Supreme Court's readiness to treat the 1979 court-executed sale and the buyer's uninterrupted possession as effectively decisive, even after multiple rounds of appellate reversal on the merits, illustrates that Indian courts are reluctant, once a conveyance has been completed and long possession has followed it, to treat that outcome as provisional simply because litigation continued around it. Conversely, sellers resisting specific performance should recognise that success in delaying a final decision does not, without more, translate into leverage to convert the buyer's entitlement into a cash payment; an appellate court that finds the seller's defence to be without merit is unlikely to reward that defence indirectly through a discretionary damages award.

For counsel drafting or opposing substantial questions of law in a second appeal, the judgment reinforces the limited scope of interference available once a High Court has affirmed, rather than disturbed, the trial court's findings of fact on the statutory ingredients of specific performance. A High Court that reaches that conclusion has, in substance, exhausted its remit under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to revisit the merits, and any further recasting of the relief itself needs an independent legal basis — not an unstructured appeal to equity — particularly where doing so would require unwinding a transaction already carried into effect.

Finally, for advocates citing comparative authority on delay-linked adjustments to specific performance relief, the case is a reminder to examine closely what stage a cited precedent's transaction had reached. An authority permitting enhanced consideration as a condition of completing a sale that has not yet occurred does not automatically support displacing a sale that has already been finally executed; the two situations call for different analyses, and conflating them risks exactly the kind of ad hoc "equity" reasoning the Supreme Court declined to endorse here.

## 5. Conclusion

*Sobaran Singh v. Gordhan Singh* is a short order resolving what might look, on its face, like a routine dispute over an old agreement to sell, but its significance lies in disciplining a form of appellate reasoning that appears with some regularity in specific performance litigation: an appellate court agrees that the plaintiff proved his case, yet reaches for compensation instead of performance in the name of equity, without confronting whether anything remains, procedurally or factually, for that discretion to operate on. Where a sale has already been executed through the court's own process and possession has followed for decades without interruption, the Supreme Court has now made clear that "equity" cannot be invoked to reopen and monetise what has, in substance, already been completed. For a legal system that continues to carry specific performance suits filed under a regime the 2018 amendment has since replaced, the case offers a template for testing whether a later appellate court's resort to equity is a genuine exercise of discretion at the threshold of relief, or an unexplained departure from findings it has itself just affirmed.

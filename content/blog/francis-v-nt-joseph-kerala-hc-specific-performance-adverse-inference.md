**Quick answer:** In *Francis @ Kuruvila v. N.T. Joseph*, decided on 23 September 2026, the Kerala High Court dismissed a defendant's first appeal against a decree for specific performance of a 2007 agreement to sell 56.5 cents of land for Rs. 23 lakh. The defendant admitted his signature on a 2009 endorsement recording part-payment of Rs. 15 lakh but claimed the underlying agreement itself was fabricated from blank stamp papers he had handed over years earlier to secure an unrelated loan. The Court held that this defence could not survive three facts on the record: the stamp paper bearing the agreement was, undisputedly, purchased only in 2007 — years after the loan the defendant said explained it; the payment recital in the plaint went entirely undenied; and the defendant, a bank employee, never replied to a lawyer's notice that set out the agreement and both payments in detail. On that combination, the trial court's finding of due execution, and its exercise of discretion to decree specific performance, called for no interference.

## 1. Introduction

Suits for specific performance of agreements to sell immovable property are a staple of Indian civil litigation, and they turn, more often than not, not on any unsettled question of law but on a single contested fact: did the defendant actually sign the agreement the plaintiff is suing to enforce? *Francis @ Kuruvila v. N.T. Joseph*, decided by a learned Single Judge of the Kerala High Court on 23 September 2026, is a compact illustration of how that question gets answered when a defendant does not deny his signature outright but instead advances an origin story designed to drain it of legal effect.

The appellant, Francis @ Kuruvila, had executed — on the plaintiff's case — an agreement dated 3 January 2007 to sell 56.5 cents of land in Alangad village, Ernakulam district, for Rs. 23,00,000, with two years allowed for completion. Two tranches of the price, totalling Rs. 20,00,000, were said to have been paid, the second by an endorsement made directly on the agreement's stamp paper in March 2009, extending time to March 2010. When the sale did not go through and a lawyer's notice went unanswered, the plaintiff sued for specific performance. The defendant's response was not a bare denial: he admitted his signature appeared on the document but said it had been obtained years earlier, on blank stamp paper, as security for an unconnected loan, and had since been "misused and fabricated" into an agreement he never made.

The trial court rejected that account and decreed specific performance. On appeal, the defendant pressed two evidentiary points — that the loan story was corroborated by his own witnesses, and that the plaintiff had failed to prove the second, larger payment through the one attesting witness he did produce. The High Court's reasons for rejecting both, discussed below, turn on how far a plea of fabrication can survive when the defendant's own signature, the dates on the record, and his own silence all point the other way. This article sets out the facts, the Court's reasoning, the statutory framework the case sits within, and what it means for how agreements to sell — and disputes over them — are drafted, evidenced and litigated.

## 2. Case summary and background

The suit, O.S. No. 270 of 2011, was filed before the Additional Sub Court, North Paravur, by N.T. Joseph, then 68, against Francis @ Kuruvila, then 58, both residents of villages in Ernakulam district. Joseph's case was that on 3 January 2007 Kuruvila executed an agreement for sale — marked at trial as Ext.A1 — agreeing to convey 56.5 cents of property (roughly 0.565 acres, or about 2,286 square metres) for a total consideration of Rs. 23,00,000, with two years fixed for performance. An advance of Rs. 5,00,000 was paid on the date of the agreement itself. A further Rs. 15,00,000 was paid on 30 March 2009, at which point the period for performance was extended to 30 March 2010, by an endorsement made on the reverse of the same stamp paper. When the extended period lapsed without a sale deed being executed, Joseph issued a lawyer's notice on 27 April 2011 (Ext.A2) calling on Kuruvila to complete the sale. Receiving no reply, he filed the suit.

Kuruvila's written statement did not deny that the signatures on Ext.A1 were his. Instead, he denied that he had ever executed an agreement for sale. His case was that he had borrowed Rs. 5,00,000 from Joseph on 14 March 2002 — five years before the date the agreement itself bears — and had, at that time, handed over blank signed stamp papers as security for the loan. Those blank papers, he said, were later "misused and fabricated" to manufacture Ext.A1 and the endorsement on it. He added that he had separately agreed to sell the same property to a third party under a different agreement (Ext.B2) dated 31 March 2011, and that Joseph — a close relative who knew Kuruvila had an encumbrance over the property that was not cleared until 2012 — had fabricated Ext.A1 only after learning of that encumbrance, to pre-empt the third-party sale.

At trial, the plaintiff examined himself and one Renoy Prakash — PW2, one of the two attesting witnesses named on Ext.A1 (the other being the plaintiff's wife, who was not examined) — and marked Exts.A1 to A6. The defendant examined himself and two other witnesses, DW2 and DW3, and marked Exts.B1 to B3, presumably including the competing Ext.B2 agreement. The Additional Sub Court, North Paravur, found that Ext.A1 had been duly executed and decreed specific performance on 30 November 2012. Kuruvila's first appeal, delayed in reaching final hearing until 2026, argued the point on two limited fronts: first, that the 2002 loan was itself admittedly funded by Joseph out of a loan he had taken from the co-operative society where Kuruvila worked, which the appellant said corroborated the blank-stamp-paper story; and second, that since only Renoy Prakash — and not the plaintiff's wife — was examined to prove Ext.A1, and since Renoy Prakash's evidence said nothing about the Rs. 15,00,000 payment made in 2009, that payment, and by extension the case for specific performance, stood unproved.

The Court, per Mohammed Nias C.P., J., rejected both contentions and dismissed the appeal, holding that the trial court's finding on due execution — and its exercise of discretion to decree specific performance — called for no interference. The reasoning is worth setting out because it shows a first appellate court doing exactly what Order XLI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 asks of it in a suit of this kind: re-examining the whole evidentiary record, not merely the two narrow points the appellant chose to argue.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 What the defendant did not deny, and why that mattered

The single most consequential feature of this case is what the appellant's written statement left unsaid. Under Order VIII Rule 5 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, every allegation of fact in a plaint that is not specifically denied, or stated to be not admitted, in the written statement, is taken to be admitted — subject to a proviso protecting persons under disability, which had no application here. The plaint set out, in terms, that Rs. 15,00,000 had been paid on 30 March 2009 and that time for performance had been extended to 30 March 2010 by an endorsement to that effect. Kuruvila's defence addressed the origin of Ext.A1 as a whole but did not, on the judgment's own account, traverse this specific averment. The Court treated that omission as significant in its own right, independent of the oral evidence on either side — a plaintiff's uncontroverted pleading is not merely persuasive, it stands admitted as a matter of procedure.

Layered on top of that was the appellant's own admission that the signature below the 30 March 2009 endorsement — the one recording the Rs. 15,00,000 payment and the extension — was his. This is where the "blank stamp paper" defence ran into a problem of physical plausibility rather than mere credibility: it required the Court to accept that a defendant would sign not just the face of a stamp paper as security for a loan, but also its reverse, at a date years after the loan was supposedly taken, and would do so blank, trusting entirely to the lender's good faith to fill in whatever recital he later chose. The judgment records the Court's own scepticism on this point — noting that it was "not likely for anyone to put a signature on both sides of the stamp paper" and that the endorsement "appears to be natural" rather than a later insertion. That is a finding about the physical document, not merely about which witness to believe, and it is precisely the kind of finding an appellate court re-appraising documentary evidence under Order XLI is entitled to make afresh.

### 3.2 The stamp paper date as an objective check on a fabrication defence

The second load-bearing fact is one that neither party's oral testimony could shift: the stamp paper on which Ext.A1 was written had, on the record, been purchased only in 2007. Under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899, stamp paper is typically purchased shortly before the document it is used for is executed, and the vendor's endorsement or the paper's own printed particulars ordinarily fix the date of purchase independently of anything either party later says about it. If Kuruvila's account were correct — that he handed over blank signed stamp paper in 2002 to secure a loan taken that year — the stamp paper itself should have been of 2002 vintage, or close to it. That it was instead purchased in 2007, the same year Ext.A1 bears as its date of execution, directly contradicts the timeline his defence depended on, and does so through a fact no oral witness could talk around. This is the kind of objectively verifiable detail — akin to checking a registration date, a survey number, or a khata entry against a party's narrative — that recurs across property litigation and that this judgment illustrates well: a fabrication defence that requires the tribunal to disregard an undisputed document date rarely survives scrutiny.

### 3.3 Silence in the face of a lawyer's notice, and the standing of a bank employee

The Court also drew an adverse inference from Kuruvila's failure to reply to the lawyer's notice of 27 April 2011, which had set out the agreement, the two payments and the extension in specific terms. Indian courts have long treated a party's silence in response to a notice detailing a transaction as a relevant circumstance bearing on the probability of that party's later, litigation-stage denial — not because silence amounts to an admission as a matter of substantive law, but because ordinary human conduct would expect a person genuinely the victim of fabrication to say so at the first opportunity, particularly where the allegation, if left unanswered, exposes him to a decree affecting immovable property. The judgment expressly weighs the fact that Kuruvila was an employee of a co-operative bank — a detail that would ordinarily suggest more than usual familiarity with financial documents and legal correspondence — against his claimed conduct of leaving signed blank stamp papers with a lender and then ignoring a detailed legal notice for years. The Court's own phrase, that this "cannot be taken as normal human conduct in the circumstances of the case," is a finding on probability rather than law, but it is precisely the sort of probability-based reasoning a court of first appeal, re-weighing oral and documentary evidence together under Order XLI, is expected to undertake.

### 3.4 The exercise of discretion under the pre-2018 Specific Relief Act regime

It bears note that this agreement, dated 2007, and this suit, filed in 2011, fall to be governed by the Specific Relief Act, 1963 as it stood before the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 — which came into force on 1 October 2018 and substituted Section 10 to make specific performance the rule, subject to defined exceptions, rather than a matter of judicial discretion. Under the unamended Section 10, read with Section 20, specific performance remained an equitable remedy that the court was not bound to grant merely because it was lawful to do so; Section 20 catalogued circumstances — such as an unconscionable bargain or disproportionate hardship — in which a court could properly withhold the decree even after due execution was proved. The judgment records that Rs. 20,00,000 of the Rs. 23,00,000 consideration stood paid by the time of suit, leaving little room for any argument that specific performance would work an unconscionable result against the seller; if anything, the scale of part-payment is itself among the strongest indicators of a plaintiff's continuing readiness and willingness to perform his own obligations, a precondition for the remedy under Section 16(c) of the Act in both its old and current form. The Court's brief, unelaborated affirmance of the trial court's "exercise of discretion" is consistent with this: once due execution was established on the facts set out above, there was nothing on this record capable of tipping the equitable balance against the plaintiff.

### 3.5 What the judgment does not decide

It is worth being precise about the limits of what was actually before the Court. The appeal, by the appellant's own framing, was confined to two evidentiary contentions — the credibility of the loan-and-blank-paper story, and the sufficiency of proof for the Rs. 15,00,000 payment through a single attesting witness. The judgment does not engage with, and should not be read as authority on, the separate agreement (Ext.B2) the defendant claimed to have executed with a third party in 2011, the effect (if any) of the encumbrance the defendant said existed over the property until 2012, or any question of priority between competing agreements to sell the same land — issues that a suit for specific performance can raise but that this particular appeal did not require the Court to resolve. Readers should also note that the judgment cites no reported precedent; its reasoning proceeds directly from the pleadings, the documents and the oral evidence, applying settled statutory principles rather than developing or distinguishing any line of authority. It is, in that sense, a case study in evidence rather than a source of new doctrine — but no less instructive for that.

## 4. Practical significance

For conveyancing and litigation practice, the judgment offers several concrete lessons. First, the date on which stamp paper is purchased is an objectively checkable fact that can make or break a fabrication defence years after the event; a party who genuinely intends to raise such a defence should verify the stamp vendor's records before pleading a specific date for the alleged blank-paper transaction, and a party seeking to prove an agreement should routinely plead and, where possible, independently establish the stamp paper's purchase date as part of the primary case, not merely in rebuttal. Second, an agreement to sell that contemplates payments in instalments should record each instalment by a dated, signed endorsement on the agreement itself, exactly as was done here in March 2009 — the practice created a discrete, physically anchored point of admission that proved decisive, and drafting counsel should build extension-of-time and part-payment clauses into agreements to sell with this evidentiary function in mind, rather than relying on separate receipts that are easier to disown.

Third, the choice of attesting witnesses matters more than it may appear at the time of execution: an agreement witnessed by a party's spouse creates an obvious difficulty in producing that witness at trial without inviting suggestions of interestedness, and where a second, independent witness is available, that witness's evidence should be led in a manner that covers every material fact pleaded — including subsequent endorsements — rather than only the original execution. Fourth, for anyone served with a legal notice concerning immovable property, this judgment is a reminder that silence carries a real evidentiary cost: a party who disputes the transaction described in a notice should say so, in writing, at the earliest opportunity, if only to avoid the notice becoming, years later, a plank in the opposing party's case for adverse inference. Finally, for sellers structuring a sale where part performance has already occurred — as here, with 87 per cent of the consideration paid — the case confirms that heavy part-payment materially strengthens both the readiness-and-willingness requirement under Section 16 and the equitable case for specific performance under Section 20 (or, for post-2018 agreements, the narrower exceptions under Section 11(2) and Section 14), a factor worth documenting carefully in any transaction expected to proceed over an extended period.

## 5. Conclusion

*Francis @ Kuruvila v. N.T. Joseph* will not appear in any digest of leading authorities on the Specific Relief Act; it develops no new test and cites none of its own. Its value lies instead in showing, with unusual clarity, how a court actually goes about testing a fabrication defence to an agreement for sale: not by weighing which witness sounds more convincing, but by triangulating a defendant's own admissions, an objectively dated document, an unrebutted pleading, and a documented silence, and asking whether they can all be true together with the story the defendant now tells. Here they could not. For a legal system in which so much property litigation turns on exactly this kind of fact-finding — did this person sign this document, and if so, on what terms — the judgment is a useful, if modest, reminder that the paper trail, properly read, often does most of the work the advocates argue over.

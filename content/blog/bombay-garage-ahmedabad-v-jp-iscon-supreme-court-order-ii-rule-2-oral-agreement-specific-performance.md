**Quick answer:** In *Bombay Garage Ahmedabad Limited v. J P Iscon Private Limited*, decided on 29 September 2026 (2026 INSC 1066), J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, JJ. set aside concurrent decrees for specific performance of an oral agreement to sell land. The second suit was barred by Order II Rule 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, because the plaintiff's earlier injunction suit omitted specific performance without leave, although that relief was then available; a liberty inferred from the withdrawal *pursis* did not cure this. For completeness, the Court also held that no concluded oral contract was proved, and that the defendants' evasive evidence could not fill the gap. The judgment is marked reportable.

## 1. Introduction

Two recurring problems in property litigation meet in this judgment. The first is procedural: a litigant who fears that property will be sold away files a quick suit for an injunction, then discovers that the real remedy is specific performance. The second is evidentiary: a litigant who never reduced the bargain to writing must prove it through witnesses, and the burden is heavy. The Supreme Court had spoken on both before. What *Bombay Garage Ahmedabad Limited v. J P Iscon Private Limited* adds is how it resolves each, and how it treats a three-Judge Bench precedent that had softened the procedural rule.

The plaintiff company claimed that it had agreed orally to buy land from the appellant company, first at Rs. 18.51 crore and then, after the defendants insisted, at Rs. 20.50 crore. It had earlier sued only to restrain alienation, withdrawn that suit, and filed a second suit for specific performance. Both the Trial Court and the High Court decreed it. The Supreme Court held that neither the form of the second suit nor the proof offered in it could stand.

This article sets out the facts and holdings, examines the reasoning on Order II Rule 2 and on proof of an oral contract, identifies the questions left open, and closes with the consequences for anyone who sues on, or defends against, an unwritten agreement to sell immovable property.

## 2. Case summary

### 2.1 Facts and procedural history

The plaintiff company's case, as the judgment records it, was that in 2006 and 2007 it negotiated the purchase of the first defendant company's property in a series of meetings. At the first, a price was fixed and a token advance paid. A second, in the presence of the parties and a solicitor, was to prepare the title and the sale deed. At a further meeting the defendants demanded a higher price, Rs. 20.50 crore was agreed, and a further cash payment and a cheque for Rs. 5 crore were handed over in January 2007. By letter of 25 November 2006 the first defendant had sent ownership documents to the ninth defendant, its person in charge, for handing to the plaintiff. On 8 May 2007, when the plaintiff insisted on executing the sale deed, the defendants allegedly refused and challenged the plaintiff to contest (paras 24, 28–30).

The first suit, filed at Mirazapur and admitted in the second plaint, sought only a permanent injunction against alienation, change of the property's nature or construction. It was withdrawn on a *pursis* citing settlement talks, with a request to file afresh; the withdrawal order recorded no express liberty (paras 24–25, 27). The plaintiff then sued for specific performance, amending the plaint in 2011 (para 5). The Trial Court decreed the suit and the High Court affirmed. The Supreme Court granted leave.

### 2.2 The contentions

The appellants argued that the second suit was barred by Order II Rule 2 CPC, that the letter of 25 November 2006 showed only the communication of documents, that the plaint was internally inconsistent, and that the negotiator had no authority from the company (para 5). The respondents answered that the withdrawal *pursis* sought liberty to file afresh, so that Order XXIII Rule 1(3), not Order II Rule 2, governed, and that the evidence, including the defendants' evasive answers, established a concluded contract (paras 6–7).

### 2.3 The holdings

The Court dealt with maintainability first. It noted that a suit on an oral agreement to sell is permissible, citing *Brij Mohan v. Smt. Sugra Begum* and *K. Nanjappa v. R.A. Hameed*, but that the plaintiff bears a heavy burden to prove consensus *ad idem* on the vital and fundamental terms, with strict proof the norm and mere inference impermissible (paras 2–3).

On Order II Rule 2, it surveyed *Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal* (Constitution Bench), *Gurinderpal v. Jagmittar Singh*, *Vimlesh Kumari Kulshrestha v. Sambhajirao*, *Cuddalore Powergen Corporation Ltd. v. Chemplast Cuddalore Vinyls Ltd.*, *Virgo Industries (Eng.) Pvt. Ltd. v. Venturetech Solutions Pvt. Ltd.*, *Vurimi Pullarao v. Vemari Vyankata Radharani*, *Inbasagaran v. S. Natarajan*, *Rathnavati v. Kavita Ganashamdas* and *Sucha Singh Sodhi v. Baldev Raj Walia*. It concluded that the earlier plaint showed a clear refusal by the defendants on 8 May 2007 and a challenge to the plaintiff to do what it could, so that specific performance was "entitled and available" when the first suit was filed. Neither the relief nor leave to sue for it later was sought. The second suit therefore attracted the bar (paras 24–27).

On the merits, it held that the plaintiff had "failed to establish a concluded contract" and that the concurrent findings "borders on perversity" (para 39). The appeal was allowed, the decrees were set aside, the suit was dismissed and the parties bore their own costs (para 40).

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Order II Rule 2 and the injunction-then-specific-performance sequence

Order II Rule 2 requires every suit to include the whole of the claim the plaintiff is entitled to make in respect of the cause of action. A plaintiff who omits to sue for, or intentionally relinquishes, a portion of the claim cannot afterwards sue for it, unless the earlier suit was brought with the leave of the court. The Court applied the three-part test from *Gurbux Singh*: the second suit must be on the same cause of action as the first; the plaintiff must have been entitled to more than one relief on it; and the plaintiff must have omitted, without leave, to sue for the relief now claimed (para 8).

The judgment's central step is factual and, in that sense, faithful to the Court's own caution that application of the rule "would be on facts" (para 23). It reads the plaintiff's own first plaint. That plaint pleaded the meetings, the increased price, the cheque, the delivery of documents and, above all, the refusal of 8 May 2007 accompanied by a challenge to do whatever the plaintiff liked. On those pleadings a claim for specific performance had accrued. The plaintiff sought only an injunction. Gurbux Singh warns that the bar is a technical one that must be "established satisfactorily" and not presumed by inference, and that a defendant cannot invite the court to speculate about an unproduced earlier plaint (paras 9–10). The Court distinguished that concern on the simple ground that here the plaintiff itself produced the earlier plaint, which was on record as Annexure P19 and admitted in the second plaint. The identity of cause of action was therefore proved from the plaintiff's own document.

This part of the reasoning is convincing. The line of authority from *Gurbux Singh* through *Virgo Industries* and *Vurimi Pullarao* separates cases in which the plaintiff at the earlier stage faced only a threat to possession from cases in which the defendant had already repudiated the bargain. *Inbasagaran*, *Rathnavati* and *Sucha Singh Sodhi* fall in the first group, since a sale to a third party surfaced only in a written statement. *Cuddalore Powergen* involved a government ban on registrations that made specific performance unavailable when the first suit was filed. The present plaintiff fits none of these exceptions.

### 3.2 Leave at institution versus liberty at withdrawal

The more consequential point is the Court's treatment of the withdrawal *pursis*. The plaintiff's argument had a surface plausibility. The *pursis* asked for liberty to sue afresh; the withdrawal was ordered on it; the second suit was filed at once. The Court accepted that liberty "definitely it can be inferred" from the *pursis* (para 25). It nevertheless held that this did not cure the defect. Sub-rule (3) of Order II Rule 2, as the Court reads it, requires leave "at the time of filing of the suit", meaning the first suit, so that the reserved relief is preserved from the outset. Liberty sought at the stage of withdrawal is a different thing.

The Court then separated the two provisions. Order XXIII Rule 1(3) applies where a plaintiff withdraws a suit to file a fresh one on the same "subject-matter" or "part of the claim". Leave under it is granted on being satisfied that sufficient grounds exist, and it extends to the subject-matter or part of the claim, "not a new relief" (para 26). Since the second suit added specific performance, which the plaintiff had omitted and which Order II Rule 2 treats as relinquished, Order XXIII could not rescue it. And the observation that the plaintiff could have sought an amendment of the first suit to add the relief cuts against the plaintiff, since it "was not done" (para 27).

This is a strict reading, and it makes procedural sense. If a liberty obtained at withdrawal could revive relief that Order II Rule 2 has already extinguished, the sub-rule requiring leave from the court hearing the first suit would be largely redundant, because every plaintiff could wait until withdrawal and then ask. A fair counter-argument is that a withdrawal order made on a *pursis* seeking liberty to file afresh is, functionally, the court's leave to litigate again. The Court's answer is that liberty under Order XXIII covers what was claimed, not what was never claimed. The judgment does not address a case in which the court, told that the plaintiff wished to sue afresh on the agreement, granted liberty in express terms, and it should not be read as deciding it.

### 3.3 The treatment of *Gurinderpal*

The most noteworthy doctrinal move is the treatment of *Gurinderpal v. Jagmittar Singh*. There, in the description the Court gives of it, a suit for injunction against alienation had been withdrawn with liberty to file a fresh suit on the strength of the plaintiff's statement, and this Court inferred that the plaintiff "wanted to seek a decree of specific performance and not confine it to a mere injunction" (para 11). The Bench called that an inference that was "impermissible and running contrary to the Constitution Bench decision", and, although *Gurinderpal* is a decision of three Judges, it would "bow down to the proposition as laid down in the Constitution Bench" (para 11).

Two observations follow. The point of principle is sound: where a Constitution Bench has said that the bar is proved by identity of cause of action and not by inference, a smaller bench is not bound by a later decision that appears to run the other way. But the judgment does not use the language of overruling, so *Gurinderpal* should be treated as of doubtful authority on inferring liberty from a withdrawal request, not as formally set aside. Lawyers who relied on it will need another footing.

### 3.4 Proof of a concluded oral contract

Having found the suit barred, the Court did not stop. "For completeness" it examined the facts (para 28). That examination is instructive because it is a compact statement of what proof of an oral agreement to sell looks like when it fails.

The Court began with the pleadings: "there can be no proof offered without pleadings" (para 32). The first suit pleaded three meetings; the second plaint initially pleaded two; an amendment restored three, and a later paragraph digressed again. A first meeting at a witness's residence, spoken of in evidence, was pleaded nowhere. The second plaint omitted the date of refusal that the first had fixed, and said nothing of the settlement talks after withdrawal. The Court declined to resort to "nit picking", but held that small discrepancies "assume massive proportions" where an oral contract is alleged (para 32).

Then it looked at the money. The Rs. 5 crore cheque was pleaded as an advance, yet neither plaint said that it was ever presented to the drawee bank, and there was a gap of months between its delivery and the first refusal. An advance that is not encashed is, the Court said, "a clear indication that there is no concluded contract" (para 33). The Court then went on to refuse to accept even the token advance as proved (para 39).

The plaintiff's own witness was interested and uncorroborated. The second witness, a person of high public standing, was introduced without pleading, and his account of a telephone confirmation was hearsay. On his status the Court added that there is "no presumption that a person having some political status would only speak truth before Court" (para 35). The third witness had no direct knowledge. The letter of 25 November 2006 showed only the transmission of documents (para 37). And the intermediary negotiating for the company, the son-in-law of its person in charge, showed no authority to bind it; a plaintiff who trusts such a person does so "to the peril of the plaintiff" (para 38).

### 3.5 Adverse inference from evasive defendants

The most useful holding for litigators is probably the treatment of the courts below's reliance on the evasive answers of defence witnesses. The Court held that however recalcitrant the defendants' conduct in the box, it "cannot lead to an inference of a concluded contract", because the proof must be furnished by the plaintiff and "established unequivocally" (para 36). Evasion by a defence witness may damage credibility; it does not stand in for the plaintiff's own evidence. Together with the criticism that the courts below placed emphasis on the "dishonest and evasive stance" of the defendants (para 39), this reaffirms that a decree for specific performance on an oral agreement must rest on affirmative proof of the plaintiff's case.

The Court was in substance re-appreciating evidence in an appeal by special leave and described the concurrent findings as perverse to justify doing so. Its reasons concern failures of pleading and proof rather than a mere difference on weight, but the lesson is that concurrent findings will not shield a decree where the pleadings, the money trail and the witnesses do not line up.

### 3.6 Questions left open

The Court did not decide whether the ninth respondent had authority to deal with the company's assets, nor the Section 46 Companies Act argument, because the suit was not maintainable and the contract not proved (para 39). Its remark that an amendment could have been sought (para 27) is an observation, not a holding.

## 4. Practical significance

**Choice of relief at the outset.** A plaintiff holding an agreement to sell, oral or written, who has met a refusal or evasion from the seller should assume that the cause of action for specific performance has accrued. If only an interim injunction is urgently needed, plead specific performance from the start or obtain express leave under Order II Rule 2(3) on institution. Withdrawing later "with liberty" is not an adequate substitute; if an injunction suit is pending, apply to amend the plaint before thinking of withdrawal, subject to limitation.

**Clients whose first suit is already withdrawn.** The second suit is now exposed. The first plaint will be examined for whether a refusal had occurred; the position is safest where it shows that none had, as in the cases the Court treats as exceptions.

**Defendants.** The bar must be pleaded and proved, but it is easily proved when the plaintiff's earlier plaint is on record. Obtain certified copies of the earlier plaint and withdrawal order and plead the bar in the written statement.

**Oral agreements.** The Court repeats that no rule of law requires a sale of immovable property to be in writing (para 2). But the plaintiff must show consistent pleadings on the meetings and terms, a date for execution of the deed, an advance actually paid and, if by cheque, encashed, a witness with direct knowledge, and a counterparty with authority. Against a corporate seller, the negotiator's authority is part of the plaintiff's case, and kinship with the company's controller does not supply it (para 38).

**Conveyancing and litigation strategy.** Buyers who pay large sums on understandings with company representatives should insist on a written agreement, board authority where required, and traceable payments that are presented. In court, do not plan to win on a defendant's poor cross-examination, and settle pleadings with care, since a late amendment adding a meeting or date will be tested against the earlier pleading.

## 5. Conclusion

*Bombay Garage Ahmedabad Limited v. J P Iscon Private Limited* is a judgment about discipline in pleading, in the choice of reliefs, and in proof. On Order II Rule 2, it holds that a plaintiff who could have sued for specific performance and sued only for an injunction has relinquished the larger relief unless leave was obtained when the first suit was filed, and that liberty inferred at withdrawal does not restore it. By preferring the Constitution Bench in *Gurbux Singh* to the three-Judge Bench in *Gurinderpal*, it removes an inference of liberty some litigants had relied on. On proof, it restates the heavy burden on a plaintiff relying on an oral agreement and refuses to let the defendants' evasiveness stand in for evidence.

The procedural holding is the more durable, since the merits discussion is tied closely to the record. The open questions, the effect of express leave, the availability of amendment and the standing of *Gurinderpal* on its own facts, will be litigated in later cases. Until then, the sound practice is to claim every relief the facts justify at the first opportunity.

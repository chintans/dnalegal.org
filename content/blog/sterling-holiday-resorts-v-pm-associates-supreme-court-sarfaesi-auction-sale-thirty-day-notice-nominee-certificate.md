**Quick answer:** In *Sterling Holiday Resorts Limited v. M/s P.M. Associates*, decided on 30 September 2026, a Bench of Justices Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Alok Aradhe (the judgment being authored by Justice Aradhe) quashed a High Court judgment that had upheld a sale certificate issued to a purchaser of a mortgaged resort at Ooty. The Court held that Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 are mandatory, and that the auction was vitiated by five distinct infirmities: a bid received while a tribunal's restraint was in force, a sale concluded before the thirty-day notice period (after excluding the period of restraint) had run, no notice to the borrower when the bids were finally opened, a sale certificate issued to a non-bidder "nominee" that did not exist on the auction date, and the absence of any record of the auction. The Court declined to decide whether a secured creditor can cancel its own sale certificate, or whether such a certificate requires registration. The judgment is marked non-reportable.

## 1. Introduction

Auction purchasers at SARFAESI sales are routinely told that their title is secure. Courts have said so repeatedly: the finality of a confirmed auction sale protects bidders who pay full price, and lenders who must be able to sell secured assets at a fair value without litigation hanging over every purchaser. *Sterling Holiday Resorts Limited v. M/s P.M. Associates*, decided by the Supreme Court on 30 September 2026, tests how far that assurance extends. The answer is that it extends exactly as far as the auction's own legality.

The Court's opening line describes the dispute as concerning "the fate of a hill resort which has been the subject of litigation for well over a decade". The resort, 'The Fernhill' at Ooty in the Nilgiris, was mortgaged to two financial institutions. After the borrower's default, one of them, IFCI Limited, invoked the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (the SARFAESI Act). A sale for Rs. 20 crore followed, a sale certificate issued, and the lender then cancelled the certificate after the borrower paid off its dues. The purchaser contested the cancellation and won before the High Court. The Supreme Court reversed, but on a ground neither side had treated as central: whether the auction was lawful at all.

This article sets out the facts and the long procedural history, explains the Court's five findings against the auction and its treatment of the finality doctrine, and then examines what the judgment leaves unresolved, including the questions it expressly declined to decide. It closes with the practical consequences for secured creditors, auction purchasers, borrowers and their advisers.

## 2. Case summary and background

### 2.1 The facts

The borrower, Sterling Holiday Resorts Limited, created a joint equitable mortgage over the resort in favour of IFCI and another institution, TFCI, as a first charge. On default, the two institutions filed O.A. No. 277 of 2000 before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, Delhi under the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993. During the pendency of that application, IFCI issued a demand notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act on 30 October 2007, and on 21 August 2009 its Authorised Officer issued a notice under Section 13(4) stating that symbolic possession of the resort had been taken.

The Debts Recovery Tribunal, Delhi decreed the original application on 23 October 2009; the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal later modified the decree to provide interest at 16 per cent per annum. TFCI's claim was settled under a one-time settlement. Meanwhile, the Debts Recovery Tribunal, Chennai, in a securitisation application by the borrower, set aside IFCI's attempt to take physical possession but allowed it to proceed from the stage of symbolic possession. Acting on that liberty, IFCI issued an auction notice on 25 March 2010, with a reserve price of Rs. 20 crore and an auction date of 28 April 2010.

### 2.2 The restraint and its aftermath

On 7 April 2010, thirteen days into the notice period, the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal, Chennai directed the borrower to deposit Rs. 1 crore and, on that deposit, restrained the Authorised Officer from "in any way proceeding further under the provisions of the SARFAESI Act". The borrower deposited the sum on 8 April 2010. IFCI received bids but did not open them. On 9 March 2011 the Appellate Tribunal allowed the borrower's appeal; IFCI challenged that order in the High Court, which allowed its writ petition on 6 September 2011, holding that there had been substantial compliance with Section 13(3A) of the Act.

Within six days, on 12 September 2011, IFCI opened the bids and declared one Ms. Rukmani Khemchand the successful bidder. The same day, the full price of Rs. 20,00,10,000 was remitted not by her but by M/s P.M. Associates, a partnership of Ms. Khemchand and her brother that, as the judgment records, was constituted on 12 September 2011. A sale certificate bearing the date 16 September 2011 was issued to the firm.

The borrower then challenged the High Court's judgment in the Supreme Court and, by an order of 5 January 2012 in that petition, was permitted to deposit the balance dues. It paid Rs. 8.80 crore and, after an intimation from IFCI, a further Rs. 3.72 crore in full and final settlement. On 8 February 2012, IFCI cancelled the sale certificate and refunded the Rs. 20 crore with interest, which the purchaser encashed. The purchaser then petitioned the High Court to quash the cancellation and to direct registration of the certificate.

### 2.3 The High Court and the appeals

By judgment dated 23 August 2013, a Division Bench of the High Court held, among other things, that the sale certificate had been validly issued; that the Authorised Officer had no power to cancel it unilaterally, such power lying only with a court or tribunal; that accepting the settlement amount was impermissible; and that the certificate required registration under Section 17(2)(xii) of the Registration Act, 1908. Both sides appealed. This Court directed status quo on 16 September 2013, and the matter was decided only on 30 September 2026. The purchaser also filed contempt petitions and a special leave petition against the sanction of the borrower's merger with Thomas Cook (India) Limited.

The parties argued the power to cancel, registration and redemption at length. The Court identified instead "the pivotal question which goes to the root of the controversy": whether the auction process begun by the notice of 25 March 2010 and ending in the sale certificate of 16 September 2011 "was conducted in accordance with law". It held that it was not, allowed the borrower's appeals, dismissed the purchaser's, and quashed the High Court's judgment. The contempt petitions were not pursued, and the special leave petition on the merger was dismissed as no longer surviving.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Mandatory procedure and the logic of the safeguards

The Court begins from the proposition that where a statute prescribes a manner of doing a thing, it must be done in that manner or not at all, citing *Taylor v. Taylor* and *Ramchandra Keshav Adke v. Govind Joti Chavare*. It then ties that rule to the character of the SARFAESI Act, which lets a secured creditor take possession of and sell a borrower's property without court intervention. Relying on *Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India*, the Court observes that the Act's validity was upheld because the power is hedged by procedural safeguards: "The safeguards are thus the very condition upon which the power exists."

This is the judgment's most useful move. Rather than treating each procedural rule as a separate technicality that a purchaser might argue was waived or cured, it treats the Rules as constitutive of the power to sell. Rules 8 and 9 are held "mandatory and must be scrupulously followed", and the thirty days' notice under Rules 8(6) and 9(1) is described as the borrower's "real and final opportunity" to redeem under Section 13(8) as it then stood. A sale in breach cannot be sustained, the Court says, relying on *Mathew Varghese* and *J. Rajiv Subramaniyan*.

### 3.2 The five infirmities

**A bid received under restraint.** The Appellate Tribunal's order barred the Authorised Officer from proceeding "in any way" under the Act. The Court held that receiving a bid and earnest money is itself a step in the sale process. That IFCI did not open the bids was no answer, because "the solicitation and receipt of the bid was itself the forbidden step". Citing *Surjit Singh v. Harbans Singh* and *Delhi Development Authority v. Skipper Construction Co.*, the Court held that an act done in violation of an order of a court or tribunal "is not merely irregular but is bereft of legal effect". Nor did the High Court's later judgment in IFCI's favour retrospectively validate what had been done under the restraint.

**Truncation of the thirty-day period.** This is the most doctrinally interesting finding. Thirteen days of the thirty had run when the restraint issued, and it lasted until 6 September 2011. Applying the maxim *actus curiae neminem gravabit*, the Court excluded the entire period of restraint from the computation. The borrower, it reasoned, was entitled to proceed on the footing that the process was arrested, and counting that time against it "would be to convert the protection granted by the Tribunal into an instrument of prejudice". On that footing seventeen days remained, expiring on 23 September 2011. The bids were opened on 12 September and the certificate issued on 16 September, both within the protected window. The Court added that this was not a technical lapse, since the window is the period in which the borrower may exercise the right of redemption.

The reasoning is persuasive, but it extends Rule 9(1) beyond its text. The Rule fixes a period running from publication of notice or service on the borrower; it says nothing about exclusion during a stay. The Court filled that gap by equity, and no earlier authority is cited for the exclusion itself, only for the proposition that a truncated window defeats Section 13(8). Lenders should treat it as settled for practical purposes, but it is the point on which the judgment is most exposed to being confined to its facts.

**No notice of the bids' opening.** The borrower had no notice of the opening on 12 September 2011, seventeen months after the date originally fixed for the auction. The Court held that the object of Rules 8(6) and 9(1) is to keep the borrower informed so that it can tender dues, participate or bring better offers, and that "A sale conducted behind the back of the borrower, after such an interregnum, defeats that object". The Court rests this on fairness in the exercise of a statutory power, not on a specific textual requirement.

**A nominee certificate.** Rule 9(2) confirms the sale in favour of "the purchaser who has offered the highest sale price in his bid", and Rule 9(6) directs the certificate to issue to that purchaser. The auction notice itself defined the "Purchaser" as the successful bidder. Ms. Khemchand bid in her individual capacity, yet the certificate issued to the firm "at her request and as her nominee". Neither the Rules nor the notice contemplate nomination. The defect was compounded because the firm did not exist when bids were invited or received, so it could not have been assessed for eligibility under Clause 3.1(a) of the notice, which required a partnership bidder to be a registered firm. Allowing the certificate "is to permit, indirectly, what could not have been done directly".

**No auction record.** IFCI described the sale first as one by private treaty and then as one by public tender, and despite orders of 25 November and 10 December 2011 never produced the original records. If there were several eligible bidders, Clause 2.10 required inter se bidding, and no record of it exists; if the sale was by private treaty, Rule 8(8) required written terms, of which there was "no trace".

### 3.3 Finality of auction sales

The purchaser relied on the line of authority that protects confirmed auctions, including *Valji Khimji and Company v. Official Liquidator of Hindustan Nitro Product (Gujarat) Limited* and *Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) (P) Ltd.* The Court acknowledged those decisions and then limited them. Finality "presupposes an auction conducted in accordance with law", and the same line of authority, citing *Divya Manufacturing Co. Pvt. Ltd. v. Union Bank of India* and *J. Rajiv Subramaniyan*, recognises that a sale vitiated by material irregularity, fraud or non-compliance with mandatory procedure may be set aside even after confirmation. The Court put it thus: "Sanctity is the reward of legality, not a substitute for it."

This does not displace the finality principle. A purchaser invoking finality must be able to show that the statutory steps were followed; where the record is silent, as here, the principle offers no shelter.

### 3.4 The equities, and what was left undecided

The Court also noted, as reinforcing considerations and not as independent grounds, that the borrower had paid all of IFCI's dues while the certificate was unregistered and possession remained with it; that the plea of collusion between borrower and lender lacked substance because the settlement had been disclosed to the High Court; that the purchaser's money had been returned with interest within five months, whereas the borrower's undertaking depended on the resort; and that, under Article 300A of the Constitution, deprivation of property requires authority of law, which a sale ignoring the mandated procedure does not supply.

The Court then expressly refused to decide the issues the parties had argued most: whether the Authorised Officer can cancel a sale certificate once issued (an argument that, it noted, the purchaser pressed "with some force", set against the borrower's reliance on Section 21 of the General Clauses Act, 1897), whether the certificate required registration, and precisely when the right of redemption under the pre-2016 Section 13(8) was extinguished. Because the auction was illegal, "no right, therefore, accrued in favour of the Purchaser", and those questions did not arise. Consequently, the High Court's holdings on them are quashed along with the rest of its judgment, but no contrary view is laid down, and the question of cancellation power remains open for a case where the auction is lawful.

## 4. Practical significance

**For secured creditors and Authorised Officers.** The judgment is a compliance checklist. Keep the valuation and reserve-price record, the thirty-day notice and newspaper publications, the bids, the confirmation and any inter se bidding record in the file, because the file is what will be produced if the sale is challenged. An unproduced record was itself one of the infirmities. Where a tribunal or court restrains further steps, stop soliciting and receiving bids as well as opening them, and expect that the notice period will be recomputed to exclude the restraint. After a restraint lifts, give the borrower fresh notice before bids are opened. The conduct of IFCI here, which opened bids within six days of a High Court judgment and concluded the sale days later, is the pattern to avoid.

**For auction purchasers and their lenders.** Due diligence on a SARFAESI purchase cannot stop at the sale certificate. The questions to ask are whether any order restrained the sale at any point after the notice, whether the notice period, properly computed, had run, whether the certificate issues to the identical person or entity that bid, and whether the auction record can be inspected. A bidder who wishes to acquire through a firm or company should say so in the bid and, where the notice permits, bid in that entity's name from the start. Nomination after the event is not contemplated by the Rules. Purchasers should also register the certificate promptly where the law requires it, though this judgment does not say whether it was required here.

**For borrowers.** The decision shows that procedural defects can be raised long after the event if the sale has not been perfected by registration and possession, and that full payment of dues to the secured creditor, even late, is treated as a weighty circumstance. It does not mean that a borrower can reopen a lawful sale by tendering dues afterwards. The pre-2016 Section 13(8) applied in this case, and the Court noted that the Section was substituted by Act 44 of 2016 with effect from 1 September 2016. Advisers on sales conducted under the current Section should not assume that the redemption analysis carries over.

**For litigators.** The case is a reminder to plead the root defect. The parties spent years on cancellation, registration and redemption, but the borrower prevailed on the legality of the auction itself. Counsel should put the whole auction record in issue, not only the questions the lower court decided.

A caution on weight. The judgment is marked non-reportable, and by its own terms it rests on the facts: a restraint, an unregistered certificate, possession remaining with the borrower, and full payment of dues. Counsel should cite it for its reading of Rules 8 and 9 and its treatment of finality, and should not overstate it as a binding ruling on the cancellation or registration questions it left open.

## 5. Conclusion

*Sterling Holiday Resorts* is a decision about the conditions on which a non-judicial sale can bind a borrower. Its core holding is that the SARFAESI Rules are not procedural courtesies but the legal basis of the secured creditor's power of sale, and that an auction conducted in breach of them confers no right on a purchaser, however much money has been paid. Its supporting holdings are practical: a bid taken during a restraint is a forbidden step, the notice period does not run while the restraint lasts, bids revived after a long freeze require fresh notice, and a certificate can issue only to the person who bid.

The judgment's strength lies in its insistence that finality is earned. Its weakness, if there is one, lies in what it leaves unsettled. The exclusion of the period of restraint from Rule 9(1) is justified by maxim and not by text, and the cancellation of a sale certificate by an Authorised Officer, the registration of such a certificate and the exact moment of extinction of the right of redemption under the old Section 13(8) all remain for another day. Lenders and purchasers should plan on the basis that the Court will read the Rules strictly when the record is thin, and borrowers should not read it as licence to defeat lawful sales.

The most durable lesson is evidentiary. In the end the sale failed not because anyone proved fraud, but because nobody could show that the process had been followed. For a lender, that is a reason to keep the file; for a purchaser, a reason to read it before paying.

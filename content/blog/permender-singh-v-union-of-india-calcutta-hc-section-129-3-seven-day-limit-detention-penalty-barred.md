**Quick answer:** In *Permender Singh v. Union of India and others*, WPA No. 3350 of 2026 (neutral citation 2026:CHC-AS:1856), decided on 14 October 2026 (hearing concluded on 12 October 2026), Justice Aryak Dutt of the Calcutta High Court quashed two orders in FORM GST MOV-09 dated 15 January 2026. They confirmed penalties of Rs. 12,67,640 and Rs. 12,55,600 under Section 129(1)(b) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 on two goods vehicles detained on 14 July 2025. Section 129(3) requires the proper officer to issue a notice within seven days of detention and to pass an order within seven days of service. The notices were served on 17 July 2025, so the orders were due by 24 July 2025. They came 175 days later and were held "barred by limitation and without jurisdiction". The Court rejected the State's explanation that a High Court case brought by the supplier was pending, and it held that the availability of an appeal under Section 107 did not stand in the way of the writ.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The period in Section 129(3) is a hard limit.** The Court treated the second seven-day period, for passing the order, as fatal when missed, following Division Bench decisions of the Patna, Gujarat and Tripura High Courts (¶¶17, 23–25).
- **Time ran from service of the notice.** The MOV-07 notices were served on 17 July 2025, which fixed 24 July 2025 as the last date for the order (¶¶10, 17).
- **A proceeding elsewhere does not stop the clock.** The supplier's writ petition in the Gauhati High Court did not stay the Section 129 proceedings, and the petitioner was not a party to it (¶¶19–22).
- **The appeal route did not oust the writ.** Where an order is passed without jurisdiction, relegating the petitioner to an appeal under Section 107 "would serve no purpose" (¶26).
- **Nothing was decided on the merits.** The Court did not rule on whether the invoices and e-way bills were invalid, and it preserved any proceedings that are "otherwise permissible" against the supplier or the recipient (¶29).
- **Weight.** This is a single-judge decision on a point where the State offered no contrary authority (¶15). It is persuasive outside the Calcutta High Court and its reasoning is short.

## 1. Introduction

Detention of goods in transit is the most immediate enforcement power in Goods and Services Tax (GST) law. A vehicle stopped on a highway stops earning, the goods stop moving, and the consignor, consignee and transporter are all affected before any adjudication has taken place. Parliament balanced that urgency by writing short time limits into Section 129 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (the CGST Act). The proper officer must issue a notice within seven days of detention and must then pass an order within seven days of service of that notice.

The question in *Permender Singh* was what follows when the second period is missed by a very wide margin and the Department says the delay was caused by litigation brought by someone else. The petitioner owned two lorries and hired them to a transporter. The vehicles were detained at Mohitnagar on NH-27, in the Jalpaiguri district of West Bengal, on 14 July 2025. The penalty orders were passed on 15 January 2026. The Court held the orders out of time by 175 days, quashed them and directed release of both vehicles within seven days of communication of its order.

The decision matters for three reasons. It applies the time limit where the petitioner was only the owner of the conveyance, the position of many lorry owners who suffer detention for documentary defects not of their making. It shows how a court treats a justification for delay resting on a parallel proceeding in another State. And it repeats that a limitation-based jurisdictional defect is a recognised exception to the rule of alternative remedy.

Section 2 sets out the facts and holding, Section 3 analyses the scheme and reasoning, Section 4 turns to practice, and Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

**The transaction.** The petitioner owned vehicles WB172949 and WB172944 and hired both to Bhagwat Roadlines on 9 July 2025 to carry MS scrap from M/s. P. S. Enterprise, Guwahati, Assam (the consignor) to M/s. Rudraksh Enterprises, Purnea, Bihar (the consignee). The goods moved under two e-tax invoices numbered PS/123/25-26 and PS/124/25-26, both dated 9 July 2025, and two e-way bills valid up to 12 July 2025 (¶¶1–2).

**Interception.** On 11 July 2025 officers of the Bureau of Investigation (North Bengal), Siliguri intercepted both vehicles at Mohitnagar. Driver statements were recorded in FORM GST MOV-01, physical verification was ordered in FORM GST MOV-02 and carried out on 13 July 2025, and recorded in FORM GST MOV-04 (¶3).

**The alleged irregularity.** The officers found on the GST portal that the supplier's registration had been suspended with effect from 10 July 2025 after a notice in FORM GST REG-17 issued that day. A report from the CGST authorities at Guwahati stated that a visit on 12 July 2025 had found the supplier non-functional at its declared place of business. The invoices and e-way bills were therefore treated as invalid, and the movement was held to contravene Section 68 of the West Bengal Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and the CGST Act, read with Rules 138 and 138A (¶4).

**Detention and notice.** On 14 July 2025 the second respondent detained the vehicles and goods under Section 129(1) by orders in FORM GST MOV-06. Notices in FORM GST MOV-07 proposing penalty under Section 129(1)(a) and (b) were issued and, according to the demand orders, served on 17 July 2025, with appearance fixed on or before 24 July 2025 (¶¶5–6).

**The Gauhati proceedings.** The supplier's petition, WP(C) No. 4700 of 2025, was disposed of by the Gauhati High Court on 11 December 2025. The order dated 30 July 2025 cancelling its registration was set aside and the matter remitted. On the seizure the point was not pressed, and liberty was granted to approach the appropriate authority. The Gauhati High Court did not stay the Section 129 proceedings (¶7).

**The orders challenged.** On 15 January 2026 the second respondent passed orders under Section 129(3) in FORM GST MOV-09, confirming penalty under Section 129(1)(b) of Rs. 12,67,640 for vehicle WB172949 and Rs. 12,55,600 for vehicle WB172944 (¶8).

**Submissions.** The petitioner argued that the notice was served on 17 July 2025, so the order was due by 24 July 2025, and an order passed on 15 January 2026 was barred and without jurisdiction (¶10). He relied on three Division Bench decisions: *Kedia Enterprises v. State of Bihar*, 2024 (90) G.S.T.L. 129 (Pat.), *Khatu Enterprises v. State of Gujarat*, (2025) 36 Centax 242 (Guj.) and *Satguru Impex v. State of Tripura*, 2022 (64) G.S.T.L. 214 (Tripura) (¶11). He added that he was only the owner, had no role in the invoices or e-way bills, and that the vehicles, hypothecated and his only livelihood, had been detained since July 2025 (¶12). The State filed an affidavit report setting out the chronology, denying the allegations generally and asserting that the petition was not maintainable (¶13). The Court noted that the report gave no explanation for the gap of nearly six months and "does not refer to Section 129(3) at all", and that no authority was cited to distinguish the petitioner's cases (¶¶14–15).

**Holding.** The Court framed one question: whether the orders were passed within the time fixed by Section 129(3) (¶16). The notices were in time, the orders were passed 175 days after the period expired on 24 July 2025, and they were therefore "barred by limitation and without jurisdiction" (¶17). The Court rejected the Gauhati proceedings as an excuse, followed the three Division Bench decisions and held the writ maintainable despite the appeal under Section 107 (¶¶18–26). It quashed both MOV-09 orders, directed release of the vehicles within seven days "without insisting on payment of any alleged duty", preserved any permissible proceedings against the supplier or recipient, and made no order as to costs (¶¶27–30).

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The statutory scheme

Section 129 governs goods and conveyances in transit that are said to move in contravention of the Act or the rules. Sub-section (1) makes the goods, conveyance and documents liable to detention or seizure and fixes the amounts on which they may be released; clause (b), under which penalty was confirmed here, applies where the owner of the goods does not come forward. Sub-section (3), as the Court describes it in paragraphs 10 and 17, requires the proper officer to issue a notice within seven days of detention and to pass an order within seven days of service. A later sub-section gives the person concerned fifteen days from receipt of the order to pay before the goods or conveyance become liable to sale or disposal.

Section 129 is a summary mechanism. It lets the Department hold the conveyance while tax and penalty are determined, and the short periods are the counterweight to that power. A procedure measured in days does not sit easily with an order that arrives after six months. The judgment applies the provision as it stood in July 2025 and January 2026. It refers to no later amendment, and nothing in its reasoning depends on the quantum of penalty under clause (a) or (b).

### 3.2 When did time start and end?

The arithmetic is simple. Detention was on 14 July 2025. The MOV-07 notices, served on 17 July 2025, were within seven days of detention. The second period ran from 17 July and ended on 24 July 2025. The order of 15 January 2026 came 175 days later (¶17).

The Court did not need to choose between competing starting points, because the demand orders themselves recorded the date of service. Nor did it need to consider adjournments or late replies, the kind of question in *Kedia Enterprises*. There, as the Court summarises it, the vehicle was detained on 30 March 2024, notice was served on 4 April 2024, the reply was filed on 12 April 2024 and the order was passed on 18 April 2024. The Patna High Court held that the authority should have passed the order on the date of the reply, and that having delayed it had not followed the mandate of Section 129(3). The Calcutta High Court drew the contrast directly: in *Kedia Enterprises* "the delay was about six days" and here "it is 175 days" (¶23).

A court unwilling to excuse six days is unlikely to excuse 175. But the holding is tied to its facts, and the Court was not asked to fix any boundary of tolerance. It should not be read as ruling on how many days of delay, if any, a court would overlook.

### 3.3 The pending Gauhati proceedings

The orders explained the delay by saying that time had passed "owing to the fact that the matter was sub-judice" before the Gauhati High Court (¶18). The Court answered in steps. The Gauhati case was brought by the supplier, mainly on suspension and cancellation of its registration, and the petitioner was not a party (¶19). No order of that Court stayed the Section 129 proceedings (¶20). The statutory period had expired on 24 July 2025 (¶21), before even the cancellation order of 30 July 2025 was made. And the governing proposition was stated in one sentence: "A pending proceeding elsewhere does not stop a statutory period from running unless a competent court has ordered so" (¶22).

That is the right answer. A statutory time for decision is displaced by a stay, by an extension the statute allows, or by a court order to that effect. It is not displaced by the pendency of related litigation, which may complicate the merits but does not remove the officer's duty to decide within the time given. The chronology reinforces the point. The Gauhati order setting aside the cancellation was made on 11 December 2025 and the Section 129(3) orders followed on 15 January 2026, about five weeks later, and the State's affidavit offered no explanation (¶14). If the Department was waiting for the Gauhati outcome, it was waiting by choice, which is inconsistent with the time limit.

### 3.4 The three Division Bench decisions

The Court treated three authorities, all cited by the petitioner and none contested (¶15), as supporting its view. It described *Kedia Enterprises* as holding that "the limitation is clear and definite" (¶23), *Khatu Enterprises* as holding that notice and order must each be issued within seven days and that non-compliance is fatal and makes the detention illegal (¶24), and *Satguru Impex* as releasing a detained vehicle and goods on the footing that the Revenue must act sensibly where parties are genuine (¶25).

They support the result in different ways. *Kedia Enterprises* and *Khatu Enterprises* go to the mandatory character of the periods. *Satguru Impex*, as described, concerns the approach to release and is more a statement of policy than a construction of the text. The judgment does not separate them. Nor does it discuss the mandatory-directory distinction that usually decides what follows a missed statutory time, because the delay was so long and the State did not argue it. The decision is best read as the application of settled High Court authority to an extreme case rather than a fresh construction of Section 129(3).

### 3.5 Alternative remedy

The State argued that an appeal lay under Section 107 of the CGST Act. The Court answered that an alternative remedy does not bar a writ where the impugned order was passed without jurisdiction, and that relegating the petitioner to an appeal "would serve no purpose" (¶26). The finding of limitation and want of jurisdiction in paragraph 17 settled the point, and the practical considerations pointed the same way: the vehicles had been detained since July 2025 and were the petitioner's only livelihood (¶12). The judgment should not be overread. It does not say that every Section 129 order may be challenged by writ. It says that a defect visible from the dates alone removes the objection. Where the challenge turns on facts the officer has not examined, such as the validity of the invoices, the appellate authority remains the usual first forum.

### 3.6 What was left open, and a critique

Four matters are undecided. *First, the merits of the detention.* The invoices were dated 9 July 2025 and the suspension took effect on 10 July 2025. The Court did not consider whether a later suspension can make an earlier invoice invalid for transit purposes, and by preserving proceedings "otherwise permissible" against the supplier or recipient (¶29) it neither upheld nor rejected the alleged contravention. *Second, the effect on the detention itself.* The Court quotes *Khatu Enterprises* as holding the detention illegal (¶24), but the operative direction is release of the vehicles and quashing of the penalties (¶¶27–28). It does not say whether it adopts the wider proposition. *Third, the petitioner's status as owner.* Counsel stressed it (¶12), but the Court did not rest the decision on it. *Fourth, the outer limit of delay.* A delay of a few days, a stay order on the record, or a State that argued the point properly would present a different case.

My assessment is that the result is correct and the route is thin. The result follows from the text as the Court describes it, three consistent High Court decisions and the State's failure to answer. The route is thin because the Court paraphrased the statute rather than analysing it, did not address mandatory versus directory, and did not say how it would treat a short delay. Beyond paragraph 22, a later court will find little to draw on. As a GST decision on GST text, it raises no question of carrying over a legacy-regime principle.

## 4. Practical Significance

**Vehicle owners and transporters.** Calculate both periods on the day of detention. The first runs from detention and the second from service of the notice. Record the dates on the MOV-06 and MOV-07 forms and in correspondence. If no order has been passed by the last date, write to the proper officer, record the lapse and ask for release. A hired-out vehicle often belongs to someone who is neither consignor, consignee nor transporter, and the owner is well placed to make that complaint.

**Consignors and consignees.** The judgment does not protect against later proceedings on the merits, which the Court left open against the supplier or recipient (¶29). A recipient whose supplier is suspended or cancelled retrospectively should treat relief on a detention as separate from the credit and demand consequences that may follow, and should keep evidence that the supply took place, that the registration was active on the invoice date, and that the e-way bill matched the movement.

**Choice of remedy.** A writ is well founded where the defect is plain from the notice, the order and the detention record without factual inquiry. Where the objection involves the validity of invoices, the identity of the owner of the goods or their nature, the Section 107 appeal is the safer forum, and the appeal period should be watched while any writ is pending.

**Reply strategy.** File the reply within the date fixed and ask for a hearing, but record its date. In *Kedia Enterprises*, as the Calcutta High Court summarises it, the date of the reply was the date by which the order should have been passed (¶23).

**Parallel proceedings.** A pending case about the supplier's registration does not suspend Section 129 timelines unless the court orders a stay. A party that wants the proceedings paused must ask that court for an order, and an owner who is not a party cannot rely on the case for delay.

**The Department.** Waiting for another forum is not a basis for missing the statutory date. Where related litigation is pending, the better course is to pass a reasoned order on time and leave the aggrieved party to its remedy. The report here did not mention Section 129(3) and offered no explanation for the delay (¶14).

**Audit and recovery.** An order quashed on limitation does not end the matter for a supplier or recipient. Compliance teams should expect separate notices on registration, credit or demand, and should reconcile e-way bills and invoices against the supplier's registration history before a notice arrives.

**Precedential weight.** This is a single-judge decision of the Calcutta High Court, 2026:CHC-AS:1856, with no reporter citation yet. It binds authorities within West Bengal and persuades elsewhere, where the stronger authority will be the Division Bench decisions on which it rests. Cite them together.

## 5. Conclusion

*Permender Singh* applies a simple rule to a stark set of dates. Section 129(3) fixes seven days after service of the notice for the order, the notices were served on 17 July 2025, the orders came on 15 January 2026, and nothing in the record paused the period. The Court held the orders barred by limitation and without jurisdiction, quashed penalties of Rs. 12,67,640 and Rs. 12,55,600, and ordered release of both vehicles.

Its value lies in three points of practice. A statutory time limit for a detention order is a real limit, and the Department cannot explain a missed date by someone else's litigation. A jurisdictional defect visible from the dates alone justifies a writ even where an appeal exists. And an owner who is not responsible for the documents can seek release on the strength of the Department's delay without proving that the invoices were valid.

Its limits are equally clear. The Court did not decide whether the invoices were invalid, whether a later suspension affects an earlier invoice, whether the detention itself becomes unlawful when time is missed, or how a short delay would be treated. It also preserved proceedings against the supplier and recipient. For the petitioner the outcome is complete. For the wider question of what happens when a detention is justified on the merits but the order is late, the judgment gives an answer on one set of facts, supported by three Division Bench decisions, and invites fuller treatment when a contested case arrives.

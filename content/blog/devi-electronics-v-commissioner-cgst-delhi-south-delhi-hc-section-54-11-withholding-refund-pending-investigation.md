**Quick answer:** In *Devi Electronics Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner CGST Delhi South and Ors.*, W.P.(C) 15557/2024, decided on 1 October 2026 (reserved on 18 September 2026), a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Anil Kshetarpal and Shail Jain, JJ.; judgment by Kshetarpal, J.) dismissed an exporter's petition against an order of 24 September 2024 withholding a refund of Rs. 3,15,30,605 under Section 54(11) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The refund had been sanctioned by an Order-in-Appeal of 19 June 2024 for IGST paid on exports between November 2023 and January 2024. The Court held that Section 54(11) has two cumulative ingredients: a pending appeal, further proceeding or other proceeding, and the Commissioner's opinion, formed after a hearing, that releasing the refund is likely to adversely affect the revenue on account of malfeasance or fraud. A merely contemplated appeal is not enough, but a pending anti-evasion investigation supported by contemporaneous material of fraud is. The Court left the merits of the underlying allegations to the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Two ingredients, both required.** The power to withhold depends on (i) a pending appeal or proceeding and (ii) the Commissioner's opinion of likely adverse effect on revenue "on account of malfeasance or fraud", formed after hearing the taxable person (¶¶13–14).
- **A proposed appeal does not suffice.** The Court accepted the principle in *Mandy Enterprises* and *Alex Tour & Travel* that the Revenue cannot hold back a refund on the strength of a decision to appeal alone, but distinguished them because there was no independent material of fraud in those cases (¶¶16–17).
- **A pending investigation can be "other proceedings".** The Court read "any other proceedings under this Act is pending" as deliberately wide, covering any proceeding of whatever nature that bears on the correctness or finality of the order giving rise to the refund (¶15, ¶26).
- **No stay is needed.** Section 54(11) is a self-contained statutory safeguard and operates independently of any stay by a court or tribunal (¶15).
- **The show cause notice came later, but the investigation did not.** The investigation began by 26 March 2024; the show cause notice of August 2026 merely crystallised it (¶¶18, 27).
- **The Commissioner's order is not appealable under Section 107, but is reviewable under Article 226.** The Court rejected the argument that the "administrative" character of the order takes it outside judicial review (¶28).
- **The Court decided no question of fact.** Genuineness of the suppliers and the movement of goods were left for the Tribunal, with liberty to the petitioner to raise Section 54(11) there (¶¶29–30).

## 1. Introduction

A refund that an appellate authority has ordered in a taxpayer's favour is ordinarily expected to be paid. Section 54(11) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (the "CGST Act") is the exception the Act itself carves out. It allows the Commissioner to withhold a refund that flows from an order, where that order is under appeal or other proceedings are pending, and where the Commissioner forms the opinion that granting the refund is likely to harm the revenue in those proceedings because of malfeasance or fraud. Earlier Delhi High Court decisions protected taxpayers from its reflexive use; this judgment marks where that protection ends.

The petitioner, Devi Electronics Pvt. Ltd., exported electronic goods on payment of integrated tax (IGST) and won five appeals against the rejection of its refund claims. The Department then reviewed the appellate order, resolved to challenge it before the Appellate Tribunal, and withheld the money under Section 54(11). The petitioner argued that a contemplated appeal and an unadjudicated investigation are not pending proceedings, and that without a judicial stay the Department had no business sitting on an appellate order. The Division Bench disagreed. It treated the provision as resting on two limbs, found the second limb independently satisfied by an investigation that pre-dated both the appellate order and the withholding order, and dismissed the petition.

This article sets out the facts, analyses the Court's reading of Section 54(11), and considers what the decision means for exporters and revenue officers. The judgment concerns the GST regime only and is tied to the text of Section 54(11) as reproduced at paragraph 13 of the judgment. The judgment does not discuss any amendment to that provision, so nothing in it should be assumed to survive a later change in the wording.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

**The transactions and the claims.** The petitioner, registered under GSTIN 07AAACD0720F2ZB, exports microphones, telephone sets, smartphones, monitors, projectors, automatic data processing machines and related parts. Between November 2023 and January 2024 it exported goods on payment of IGST and filed five refund applications for Rs. 1,35,69,527.58, Rs. 43,11,670.36, Rs. 34,92,046.08, Rs. 67,97,817 and Rs. 33,59,544.01, aggregating to Rs. 3,15,30,605.03 (¶4).

**Rejection and appeal.** The proper officer issued show cause notices alleging that the applications lacked supporting documents. Orders-in-Original dated 1 March 2024, 27 March 2024, 22 April 2024, 25 April 2024 and 7 June 2024 rejected the claims, primarily for non-submission of complete documents (¶5). On appeal, the petitioner relied on screenshots from the GST portal showing what it had uploaded. By an Order-in-Appeal dated 19 June 2024, the Appellate Authority allowed all five appeals, holding that the officer had erred in rejecting the claims without appreciating the petitioner's submissions (¶6).

**Review and withholding.** On 23 June 2024 the Competent Authority reviewed that order and recorded that it was liable to be challenged before the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal under Section 112(3) of the CGST Act read with Rule 111(1) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017, with an appeal to be filed once the Tribunal became functional (¶7). In the meantime, on 25 June 2024, the petitioner filed a consolidated refund claim for Rs. 3,15,30,605.03 (ARN AA070624070785W). The Commissioner examined it under Section 54(11), gave the petitioner hearing notices on 27 August 2024 and 3 September 2024 for hearings listed on 30 August, 5 September and 9 September 2024, and passed the impugned order withholding the refund (¶¶7–9). The Department's pleadings place that order on 23 or 24 September 2024 (¶11.5); the Court refers to it as the order of 24 September 2024.

**The investigation.** The Anti-Evasion Wing of the Delhi South Commissionerate received a case concerning the petitioner from the Okhla Division on 26 March 2024, nearly three months before the Order-in-Appeal and nearly six months before the impugned order (¶18). The show cause notice that later quantified the allegations proposed to treat Rs. 6,53,08,612 of input tax credit as inadmissible (¶18). The Court recorded the following material, all drawn from the show cause notice and the impugned order:

- Physical verification reports and communications from State authorities that suppliers such as Kanpur Mobiles, Almec Trading and Balaji Mobile Hub were bogus, non-existent or not functioning at their registered premises (¶19).
- A report that Ziavlo India Private Limited was not found at its registered premises, the owner of the premises saying that it had never been let to that company, supported by a panchnama prepared by officers of the Directorate General of GST Intelligence, Surat (¶19).
- Statements under Section 70 of the CGST Act by vehicle owners named in e-way bills, who denied transporting the goods (¶19).
- Responses from Apple India Private Limited, after the petitioner supplied IMEI numbers for 3,697 mobile phones: Apple gave details of 3,623 phones, all sold business-to-business to nine companies, and the seven companies that responded did not show the petitioner anywhere in the supply chain (¶19).
- The Commissioner's own tier-wise (L1, L2 and L3) examination of suppliers, several shown as "Cancelled suo moto" on the GST portal (¶22).

**Issues and holding.** The Court framed the "principal and limited question" as whether the Commissioner rightly invoked Section 54(11) to withhold the refund (¶2). It decided four things. First, Section 54(11) has two cumulative ingredients (¶14). Second, the earlier decisions barring withholding on a proposed appeal were distinguishable because they turned only on the first limb (¶¶16–17). Third, an investigation pending since 26 March 2024, together with the Commissioner's reasoned opinion of malfeasance or fraud, satisfied the section (¶¶18–27). Fourth, the absence of a Section 107 appeal against a Commissioner's order does not exclude Article 226 review, and the factual disputes belong before the Tribunal, where an appeal was admittedly already filed (¶¶28–30). The petition was dismissed with liberty to raise all contentions before the Tribunal "uninfluenced by any observation made herein" (¶30).

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The statutory text and its two ingredients

Section 54(11) of the CGST Act, as reproduced by the Court at paragraph 13, permits the Commissioner to withhold a refund "[w]here an order giving rise to a refund is the subject matter of an appeal or further proceedings or where any other proceedings under this Act is pending and the Commissioner is of the opinion that grant of such refund is likely to adversely affect the revenue in the said appeal or other proceedings on account of malfeasance or fraud committed", after "giving the taxable person an opportunity of being heard", and "till such time as he may determine".

The Court broke this into two cumulative ingredients (¶14). The first is a trigger: the refund-giving order must be the subject of an appeal or further proceedings, or other proceedings under the Act must be pending. The second is a safeguard: the Commissioner must independently form an opinion, after hearing the taxpayer, that releasing the refund is likely to adversely affect the revenue on account of malfeasance or fraud. Both must be present. This is the analytical core of the judgment and is, in my view, a sound reading. The section contains no word that would allow the first ingredient to carry the whole weight.

### 3.2 The earlier authorities, and why they were distinguished

The petitioner relied on *Mandy Enterprises v. Deputy Commissioner* ((2024) 22 Centax 534 (Del.)) and *Alex Tour & Travel v. Asst. Commissioner CGST* (W.P.(C) No. 5722/2023). The Court also referred to *Truth Fashion*, giving no citation for it in the text. It read these as establishing that the Revenue cannot withhold a refund sanctioned by an appellate authority merely on the strength of a proposed or contemplated appeal with no stay, and that "the mere decision to prefer or institute an appeal" does not engage Section 54(11) (¶16). The Court accepted that principle and did not doubt it.

It then distinguished the cases on a single ground: they "turned on the singular fact" that the Revenue's case rested only on the first limb, a proposed appeal, "with no independent material of malfeasance or fraud placed before the Court" (¶17). That distinction does real work in the judgment. It reconciles the decision with the earlier line without overruling or qualifying it, and it tells practitioners that the proposed-appeal argument remains good law where the Department has nothing more.

### 3.3 "Any other proceedings": is an investigation a proceeding?

The most consequential holding is at paragraph 15. The Court described the width of "any other proceedings under this Act is pending" as "deliberate", and said that it "is not confined to a formally instituted appeal; it takes within its fold any proceeding, of whatever nature, arising under the Act of 2017, that has a bearing on the correctness or finality of the order from which the refund flows". On that footing, an Anti-Evasion Wing investigation under way on 24 September 2024 was enough (¶26).

The petitioner had argued the opposite, citing *Liberty Oil Mills v. Union of India*, *G.K. Trading Company v. Union of India* (2021 (51) G.S.T.L. 288 (All.)) and *Srico Projects (P.) Ltd. v. Telangana State Authority for Advance Ruling* ([2022] 142 taxmann.com 5 (Telangana)) for the proposition that an investigation is not a proceeding pending under the Act (¶10.3). The judgment records those citations but does not discuss them. There is no explanation of why they do not apply. That is the most obvious gap in the reasoning, and the point is likely to be argued afresh.

The Court's reading is also open to a textual challenge. The statute asks whether the refund will adversely affect the revenue "in the said appeal or other proceedings". An investigation that has not yet produced a show cause notice has no adjudicated claim in which the revenue's position can be harmed. The Court answered this indirectly by observing that the show cause notice "subsequently crystallised the allegations arising from that investigation" (¶27), so that the notice and the investigation are one continuous process. That is a defensible functional view, but it makes the scope of "proceedings" turn on how mature the investigation was, and the Court set out no criteria.

### 3.4 The Commissioner's opinion and how closely the Court examined it

On the second ingredient, the Court did not take the opinion on trust. It listed three findings that the record established before the impugned order: six suppliers found non-existent or non-operational by the jurisdictional authorities, categorical denials by transporters that goods had moved, and the manufacturer's sales records not showing the petitioner in the supply chain (¶20). It stressed that the Commissioner "did not act on a mere apprehension" but examined the supply chain by tiers and the portal status of each supplier (¶22). It linked this to Section 16(2)(b) of the CGST Act, which makes receipt of the goods or services a condition of input tax credit: the circumstances were "relevant to the requirement under Section 16(2)(b) … that the recipient must have actually received the goods or services" (¶24).

Two observations follow. First, the Court plainly subjected the opinion to a record-based check, even though it did not name a standard of review. That is the right approach: a power to withhold a sanctioned refund on the Commissioner's opinion cannot sensibly be unreviewable. Second, the Court's statement that each verification report in the show cause notice "pre-dates or coincides with the passing of the Impugned Order" (¶19) sits awkwardly with the extract it reproduces, which includes a communication from the State tax office at Kanpur dated 24 December 2024, three months after the order. The Court's summary at paragraph 20 is narrower and rests on material that was before the Commissioner, so the result is unaffected, but the tension shows why a challenger should map each item of material against the date of the opinion. An opinion must stand on what the Commissioner had when he formed it, not on what the investigation later gathered.

### 3.5 Other points decided

**Timing of the show cause notice.** The petitioner said the show cause notice, issued in August 2026, could not be a proceeding pending on 24 September 2024. The Court distinguished the pendency of an investigation from the later issue of a notice, and relied on the Commissioner's own record that the investigation was "still under process" on that date (¶27). The judgment dates the notice 21 August 2026 (¶¶18, 27) and, in the petitioner's submissions, 24 August 2026 (¶10.5); nothing turns on this.

**The "administrative" order.** The petitioner contended that the Commissioner passes an order under Section 54(11) in an administrative capacity, distinct from the "Adjudicating Authority" defined in Section 2(4), and that Section 107 therefore gives no appeal. The power is conferred on the "Commissioner", is "distinct from the adjudicatory mechanism under Sections 73 and 74" and from the appeal under Section 107, and the absence of an appeal "does not exclude judicial review under Article 226" (¶28). The Court did not decide whether the order is in fact appealable under Section 107, since it recorded that an appeal against the impugned order was admittedly already before the Tribunal (¶29).

**Alternative remedy.** The respondents objected that the writ was not maintainable because the Department had resolved to appeal the Order-in-Appeal (¶11.1). The Court entertained the petition and decided the Section 54(11) question, but declined to adjudicate "disputed questions concerning the genuineness of the suppliers, actual movement of goods, or the correctness of the findings" because of the pending appeal (¶29).

### 3.6 Critique and unresolved questions

The reasoning is persuasive on the structure of Section 54(11) and the distinction from *Mandy Enterprises* and *Alex Tour & Travel*, but less satisfying in three respects.

First, the treatment of "proceedings" is conclusory. The Court holds that an investigation is a proceeding but does not engage with the authorities cited against it, and does not draw any line between a routine verification inquiry and the kind of investigation that qualifies.

Second, the duration of the withholding is not addressed. The section allows withholding "till such time as he may determine". The order was made on 24 September 2024 and the petition was decided on 1 October 2026. The judgment says nothing about whether an open-ended withholding is subject to any limit of reasonableness, or whether interest is affected.

Third, the Court's own usage of "first limb" and "second limb" is not perfectly consistent with its enumeration at paragraph 14, where the first ingredient itself has three alternatives. In paragraphs 25 and 26 the "second limb" is described as the pendency of other proceedings coupled with the Commissioner's opinion. This blurs the clean two-part test the Court had just stated.

## 4. Practical Significance

**For exporters and refund claimants.** A favourable Order-in-Appeal is not the end of the refund. If the Department has reviewed the order and an investigation touching the claimant or its suppliers is open, expect a notice of hearing under Section 54(11). The hearing is a real opportunity and the only forum before the order is made. Claimants should use it to put on record, in documentary form, the movement of goods, payment trail and supplier credentials for each transaction, because the Commissioner's opinion will be judged on the material that existed at that point. A response that only argues that a proposed appeal is not a pending proceeding will fail if the Department has also recorded an investigation.

**For input tax credit hygiene.** The Court's reasoning ties the fraud inquiry to Section 16(2)(b). In practice, supplier non-existence at the registered premises, cancellation "suo moto" on the portal, transporters disowning e-way bills and a manufacturer's records not showing the buyer are the types of facts that were enough here. Exporters who pay IGST out of credit, and then seek refund, should maintain supplier-verification files, transport records and proof of receipt.

**For choice of remedy.** The decision illustrates that a writ will be entertained against a Section 54(11) order, since Article 226 review is not excluded by the absence of a Section 107 appeal (¶28). It also shows the likely outcome when the Department has an appeal pending before the Tribunal and the dispute is factual: the Court will confine itself to the jurisdictional preconditions and refer the rest to the Tribunal. Petitioners should therefore frame a writ around a demonstrable absence of an ingredient, such as no pending proceeding or no recorded opinion, rather than around the merits of the supply chain.

**For the Department.** The decision gives a template: record the pending proceeding and when it began, the material before the Commissioner, a tier-wise examination where circular trading is alleged, a hearing and a reasoned opinion on fraud or malfeasance. An order resting on a decision to appeal alone remains exposed under *Mandy Enterprises* and *Alex Tour & Travel*.

**For litigation strategy.** Challengers should (a) establish when the investigation began relative to the order, (b) separate material that pre-dates the opinion from material that does not, (c) argue that routine verification is not a "proceeding" and invite the Court to engage the authorities it did not discuss, and (d) press for a time-bound determination rather than an open-ended withholding.

## 5. Conclusion

The judgment confirms that Section 54(11) of the CGST Act is a real power with real limits. A sanctioned refund cannot be held back because the Department intends to appeal, or merely because the Commissioner suspects wrongdoing. What the Court requires is a pending appeal or proceeding, which can include a live investigation, together with a hearing and a reasoned opinion that releasing the money is likely to harm the revenue through malfeasance or fraud, supported by material that existed when the opinion was formed.

On that test, Devi Electronics lost because the record showed an investigation under way since March 2024 and a detailed examination of its supply chain. The Court decided no question of fact: whether the suppliers were genuine, whether the goods moved and whether the refund is ultimately due remain for the Appellate Tribunal, which the Court directed to consider the petitioner's contentions, including those on Section 54(11), uninfluenced by this judgment.

Two matters await later cases: how far "proceedings" stretches to cover investigations, and how long a refund may be withheld on an opinion never finally tested. Until then, taxpayers should treat an open investigation as capable of suspending an appellate refund, and officers should treat a recorded, contemporaneous and reasoned opinion as the condition of the power.

## FAQ

**Can the Department withhold a refund just because it plans to appeal the order that granted it?**
Not on that ground alone. The Court accepted the principle in *Mandy Enterprises* and *Alex Tour & Travel* that a proposed or contemplated appeal, without more, does not justify withholding. It distinguished those cases because the Department here also relied on a pending investigation and independent material of fraud (¶¶16–17).

**Is an investigation a "proceeding" for the purposes of Section 54(11)?**
The Court held that it can be. It read "any other proceedings under this Act is pending" as covering any proceeding of whatever nature that has a bearing on the correctness or finality of the order giving rise to the refund, and treated an anti-evasion investigation under way on the date of the order as sufficient (¶¶15, 26). It did not discuss the authorities the petitioner cited for the contrary view.

**Does the show cause notice have to be issued before the refund is withheld?**
Not on this judgment. The notice issued in August 2026 was held to have crystallised an investigation that was already in progress when the order of 24 September 2024 was passed (¶27).

**Does the taxpayer have to be heard?**
Yes. The section requires "an opportunity of being heard", and the Court recorded that the petitioner received hearing notices dated 27 August 2024 and 3 September 2024 (¶¶8, 13).

**Does a Section 54(11) order have a statutory appeal under Section 107?**
The Court did not decide that. It held that the absence of an appeal under Section 107 does not exclude review under Article 226, and recorded that an appeal against the order was already before the Appellate Tribunal (¶¶28–29).

**Did the Court find that the petitioner's input tax credit was fraudulent?**
No. The Court expressly declined to adjudicate the genuineness of the suppliers, the movement of goods or the correctness of the findings, and left those to the Tribunal (¶¶29–30).

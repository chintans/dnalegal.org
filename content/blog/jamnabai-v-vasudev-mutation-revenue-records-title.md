**Quick answer:** In *Jamnabai and Others v. Vasudev and Others*, 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 843, decided on 20 August 2026, the Supreme Court restored a decree of co-ownership and partition in favour of the legal heirs of a deceased co-owner whose name had been removed from the revenue records of agricultural land at Village Kanadia, Indore. The Court held that a mutation entry is a fiscal record which neither creates nor extinguishes title, and that it cannot operate as a conveyance or as a relinquishment of proprietary rights however long it stands unchallenged. It further held that the Madhya Pradesh High Court had exceeded its jurisdiction under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 by reappreciating evidence and displacing concurrent findings of the trial court and the first appellate court without any finding of perversity.

## Key Takeaways

- **A revenue entry is a tax record, not a title document.** Mutation exists so that the state knows whom to assess and from whom to collect. It carries no presumption of ownership capable of defeating a documented devolution of title.
- **Silence is not surrender.** A co-owner whose name is absent from the record of rights for years does not thereby relinquish anything. Extinguishment of an interest in immovable property requires a legally effective instrument, not administrative inaction.
- **Relinquishment must be proved on its own terms.** An affidavit, a statement recorded before a revenue officer and a consent letter were held insufficient to displace concurrent findings that no valid relinquishment had occurred.
- **Section 100 CPC is a narrow gate, not a second look at the evidence.** Concurrent findings of two courts survive second appeal unless they are perverse or vitiated by an error of law, and the High Court must say so before disturbing them.
- **Title diligence cannot stop at the revenue record.** Where a chain of devolution shows two heirs and the current record shows one, the omission is a question to be answered, not a fact to be accepted.

## 1. Introduction

Few instruments in Indian property practice are as widely misunderstood as the mutation entry. Purchasers ask for it, lenders record it, and litigants routinely plead it as though the appearance of a name in the record of rights were a species of ownership. *Jamnabai and Others v. Vasudev and Others*, decided by the Supreme Court of India on 20 August 2026, is a firm restatement that it is nothing of the kind. Reported at 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 843, the judgment arose from a dispute over roughly 12.41 acres of agricultural land in Survey No. 307, together with a house, at Village Kanadia, Tehsil and District Indore, Madhya Pradesh.

The proposition the Court reaffirmed is not novel. What makes the decision worth close reading is the factual setting in which the proposition was applied, and the second, procedural holding that travelled with it. Here the mutation entry had not merely failed to create title in the person recorded; it had been pressed into service to *extinguish* the title of a person who was not recorded. The respondents argued that a co-owner had walked away from his share, and pointed to the revenue record and to a cluster of documents said to embody his consent. Two courts rejected that case on the evidence. The High Court, sitting in second appeal, accepted it. The Supreme Court set the High Court's judgment aside and restored the decree.

This article sets out the facts and procedural history, which matter here more than usual because the outcome turned on who was entitled to find them; analyses the two holdings and the questions they leave open; and considers what the decision means for conveyancing diligence, for drafting, and for litigation where the revenue record runs against the client.

## 2. Case summary and background

The land originally belonged to one Bhagwansingh. On his death it devolved upon his two sons, Ramprasad and Vasudev, and mutation was effected in the revenue records in their joint names. That much was common ground: the record itself, at that stage, reflected a joint entitlement in both brothers.

According to the appellants, the family's circumstances then deteriorated. Ramprasad suffered from alcoholism and, on their case, was subjected to humiliation and physical assault by his brother Vasudev, which led his wife Jamnabai to move him to her parental village. The family continued, on the appellants' pleadings, to receive agricultural produce from the land — a detail of some importance, because receipt of a share of the yield is consistent with continuing co-ownership and inconsistent with the abandonment the respondents alleged.

The dispute crystallised when the appellants obtained certified copies of the revenue records and discovered that Survey No. 307/01 now stood mutated in the name of Jaswant, a son of Vasudev, and Survey No. 307/02 in the name of Vasudev himself. Ramprasad's name had been removed altogether. Notably, the parcel had by then been subdivided in the record, so the entries did not merely substitute one name for another; they described a holding partitioned between father and son to the exclusion of the branch that had been recorded jointly a generation earlier. In 2008 the appellants — Jamnabai as Ramprasad's widow, together with their children — instituted a suit seeking a declaration of co-ownership, partition and separate possession of their share.

The defence was one of relinquishment. The respondents contended that Ramprasad had voluntarily given up his interest in the property, relying on an affidavit, a statement said to have been made before the Naib Tehsildar, and a consent letter exhibited as Ex.D5. The theory of the defence was therefore not that mutation had conferred title on Vasudev and his son, but that the mutation was the administrative footprint of a genuine, consensual exit by Ramprasad from the co-ownership.

The trial court rejected that defence and decreed the suit. The first appellate court agreed, and by its judgment and decree dated 2 May 2019 affirmed the declaration of co-ownership and the appellants' entitlement to partition and separate possession. There were, accordingly, concurrent findings of two courts on the twin factual questions of whether Ramprasad had relinquished his interest and what the revenue entries signified.

In second appeal, the High Court of Madhya Pradesh took a different view and, by judgment dated 9 May 2025, reversed those findings and set the decree aside. The appellants carried the matter to the Supreme Court, where it was heard as Civil Appeal No. 11173 of 2026, arising out of Special Leave Petition (Civil) No. 39 of 2026 (Diary No. 62675 of 2025). The appeal was allowed. The Court set aside the High Court's judgment of 9 May 2025 and restored the first appellate court's judgment and decree of 2 May 2019, with the result that the legal heirs of Ramprasad stand declared co-owners entitled to partition and separate possession of their share. According to reports of the judgment, the Bench comprised Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice Augustine George Masih.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 The statutory character of a mutation entry

The Court's first holding rests on what a mutation entry *is*. Mutation under a state land revenue enactment — in Madhya Pradesh, the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code, 1959 — is an administrative act by which the revenue authorities update the record of rights to reflect who is in possession and therefore who is liable to pay land revenue. The record is maintained for fiscal administration. It is not a register of title, and the officer who makes the entry conducts no adjudication of competing claims to ownership; that is the exclusive province of the civil court.

Two consequences follow, and the judgment states both. First, a mutation entry cannot *create* title in the person recorded. Second — and this is the limb the case turned on — it cannot *extinguish* the title of a person who is not recorded. The second proposition is the logical mirror of the first, but it is far more often forgotten in practice, because a party holding the record has every incentive to treat the omission of a rival's name as a species of proof.

The Court also addressed the temporal argument that usually accompanies such a defence: that an entry left unchallenged for many years must be taken to reflect reality. It held that lapse of time does not transmute a fiscal record into an instrument of transfer. A mutation entry, however long unchallenged, cannot operate as a conveyance or as a relinquishment of proprietary rights. This is the sharpest sentence in the decision, and the one most likely to be cited.

The reasoning is doctrinally orthodox. Section 9 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 permits an oral transfer only where a writing is not expressly required by law. Section 17(1) of the Registration Act, 1908 compulsorily requires registration of non-testamentary instruments which purport or operate to create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish any right, title or interest in immovable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards. A deed of release or relinquishment falls squarely within that description. An entry made by a revenue clerk is not such an instrument, and cannot achieve by administrative side-effect what the statute requires a registered document to achieve directly.

The devolution itself was governed by the Hindu Succession Act, 1956. On Bhagwansingh's death his two sons took as Class I heirs, and heirs of the same class take as tenants-in-common rather than as joint tenants. The share of each was therefore a distinct, alienable, heritable interest — capable of being transferred, but only in a manner the law recognises. On Ramprasad's death his interest devolved on his own heirs, the appellants, which is why they and not merely he could sue.

### 3.2 Relinquishment as a question of proof

The respondents' documents deserve separate consideration, because the judgment does not say that an affidavit or a statement before a revenue officer can never be relevant to a plea of relinquishment. What it holds is that the concurrent findings rejecting that plea were not shown to be perverse.

Two propositions are easily conflated here. The strong one is that documents of this kind are legally incapable of effecting a relinquishment of an interest in immovable property — which follows from Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 where extinguishment of a proprietary interest is the object. The weaker one is that such documents may be evidence of a family arrangement or an admission, but were not believed on these facts. The reported holding sits closer to the second: two courts disbelieved the defence, and the High Court could not substitute its own appreciation of that evidence.

That leaves a question the judgment does not squarely resolve, and practitioners should not read it as resolved. Where a family arrangement is genuinely proved, Indian law has long recognised that a memorandum merely recording an antecedent arrangement, rather than itself creating or extinguishing rights, may not require registration. A defence framed as a proved family arrangement, supported by conduct over decades, is a different animal from a defence resting on an unregistered consent letter plus a favourable revenue entry. *Jamnabai* forecloses the latter. It does not obviously foreclose the former.

### 3.3 The second appellate holding

The procedural holding is the one with the wider footprint, because it reaches beyond property law. Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 confines the High Court's second appellate jurisdiction to cases involving a substantial question of law. The provision does not license a re-hearing on the facts. The settled framework — developed in the line of authority running through *Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari* (2001) and *Hero Vinoth v. Seshammal* (2006) — requires the High Court to formulate the substantial question of law, and permits interference with findings of fact only where they are perverse, unsupported by evidence, or vitiated by an error of law in the approach to the evidence.

The Supreme Court found that the High Court had done none of this. It had reappreciated the evidence on relinquishment and on the effect of the mutation entries, and had departed from concurrent findings without establishing perversity. That is a jurisdictional error, not merely a disagreement about outcome, which is why the appropriate remedy was restoration of the first appellate decree rather than a remand.

There is an analytical knot here worth naming rather than smoothing over. The legal effect of a mutation entry is a question of law, not fact, so had the High Court held that the courts below misunderstood what a revenue entry can do, that would have been a substantial question of law properly within Section 100. What it appears to have done instead is accept a factual case of relinquishment that two courts had rejected, using the entries as corroboration. The Supreme Court's answer — that the entries could not bear that weight in the first place — is itself a proposition of law, and it is what allows the two holdings to fit together rather than pull against each other.

### 3.4 The line of authority

The judgment stands on well-worn ground. Reports of the decision indicate that the Court relied on *Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh* (1997), where it was observed that mutation of property in a revenue record neither creates nor extinguishes title and has no presumptive value on title, and on *Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner* (2007), where it was held that an entry in revenue records does not confer title on the person whose name appears in the record of rights. The same principle was applied in *Jitendra Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, decided on 6 September 2021, where the Court held that a mutation entry confers no right, title or interest and exists only for fiscal purposes, and that rights under a disputed will must be established before a civil court.

The frequency with which the Supreme Court has had to repeat this is itself the point. A principle restated across three decades has not failed to settle in the law reports; it has failed to settle in practice. On that view the contribution of *Jamnabai* is not doctrinal innovation but the disciplining of a recurring error — and its most useful sentence is the one addressed to duration, since the passage of time is what gives these defences their surface plausibility.

## 4. Practical significance

**For title diligence.** The immediate lesson is that a certified copy of the record of rights answers a narrower question than purchasers assume. It shows who the state currently assesses. It does not show who owns. Where the devolution history discloses more heirs than the current record names, the missing names are the diligence question, and the only satisfactory answers are a registered instrument of transfer or release, a decree of a competent court, or a properly executed and registered family settlement. An unexplained gap between the succession chain and the record should be treated as an outstanding claim, because on the authority of this judgment it very likely is one. Vendors' undertakings and indemnities are not a substitute; a co-owner who never signed anything is not bound by a covenant given by someone who did.

**For drafting.** Where a family genuinely intends that one member should take the whole, the transaction must be documented as a transaction. A release or relinquishment deed, executed by the releasing party and registered, is the instrument the Registration Act, 1908 contemplates; a partition deed or a registered family settlement will serve where interests are being redistributed. Affidavits, consent letters and statements recorded before revenue officers are, at best, evidence of intention. They should never be the operative document, and applying for mutation on the strength of them creates a record that looks settled while resting on nothing.

**For litigation strategy.** For a plaintiff, the judgment is an answer to the delay-based defence that so often greets a late partition suit. Because the mutation entry effected no transfer, the plaintiff's title was never displaced, and the defendant must locate some other event — an ouster, a proved family arrangement, a registered instrument — capable of extinguishing it. Limitation and adverse possession remain live defences on their own terms, but they must be pleaded and proved as such, on evidence of hostile and exclusive possession to the plaintiff's knowledge, and not inferred from the shape of the revenue record. For a defendant, the corollary is that the record is corroborative at most, and a defence built on it alone is fragile.

**For appellate practice.** The Section 100 holding cuts both ways and should be used deliberately. A respondent holding concurrent findings should meet a second appeal by attacking the absence of a properly formulated substantial question of law before engaging with the merits. An appellant seeking to disturb such findings must identify a legal error in the approach to the evidence — for instance, that the courts below gave a revenue entry a legal effect it cannot bear — rather than inviting the High Court to weigh the evidence afresh.

**For revenue administration.** The decision is also a reminder to authorities effecting mutation on the strength of consent documents that they resolve nothing. Recording a transfer that no registered instrument supports does not confer title on the beneficiary; it merely creates a record that will be unwound, sometimes decades later and at considerable cost to everyone who relied on it in the meantime.

## 5. Conclusion

*Jamnabai v. Vasudev* is a short judgment about a long wrong. A co-owner's name disappeared from a revenue record; the record was then offered as proof that he had never really been an owner at all; and a second appellate court accepted the argument that two courts before it had rejected. The Supreme Court's response operates on both levels at once. Substantively, it holds that a fiscal entry cannot do the work of a registered conveyance, and that duration does not improve it — an entry unchallenged for twenty years extinguishes no more than an entry made yesterday. Procedurally, it holds that the finality of concurrent findings is not a courtesy but a jurisdictional limit, and that a High Court which reweighs evidence in second appeal has exceeded its authority whatever it concludes.

Neither proposition is new, and the judgment does not pretend otherwise. Its value lies in the pairing. Defences of this kind almost always combine a weak legal premise about the record with a request that the appellate court re-read the evidence; addressing only one leaves the other intact. Whether the restatement holds where a defendant proves a genuine family arrangement rather than merely asserting consent is a question for another case, and practitioners should not treat *Jamnabai* as having closed it.

For anyone advising on Indian immovable property, the operative instruction is unchanged but newly reinforced: verify title through the chain of registered instruments and the law of succession, treat the revenue record as evidence of assessment rather than of ownership, and document any intended relinquishment in the form the Registration Act, 1908 requires. The alternative is the position the respondents found themselves in — holding a record, and nothing else.

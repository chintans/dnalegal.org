**Quick answer:** In *Mohammed Luthfur Rahman v. The Union of India and 2 others*, WP(C) No. 3857 of 2026 (neutral citation 2026:GAU-AS:15401), pronounced on 12 October 2026, Justice Devashis Baruah of the Gauhati High Court quashed the cancellation of a Guwahati trader's GST registration. The registration had been cancelled under Section 29(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 because the trader did not furnish a rent agreement for an *additional* place of business, which was the subject of a landlord–tenant suit. The Court held that this ground does not fit Section 29(2)(a) read with Rule 21 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017, and that Form GST REG-01, prescribed under Rule 8, asks for proof of the *principal* place of business only. It set aside the show cause notice and the cancellation order and restored the registration from 18 June 2026, the date of suspension.

## Key Takeaways

- **The ground must fit the statute.** Section 29(2)(a) allows cancellation for contravention of provisions "as may be prescribed"; the Court looked to Rule 21 to find what is prescribed and found nothing covering a missing rent agreement for an additional place (¶¶12–14, 19).
- **Form REG-01 asks for proof of the principal place only.** The documents listed in the form relate to the principal place of business and say nothing about the additional place (¶16).
- **Even for the principal place, a rent agreement is not essential.** An affidavit with a document showing possession, such as an electricity bill, is an accepted alternative (¶¶16, 19).
- **Rule 21(a) is about conduct of business, not paperwork.** The Court read it as concerned with a person who no longer carries on business at the declared location, which Rule 25 allows the officer to verify physically (¶¶14–15).
- **The relief was complete.** The notice and both orders were quashed and the registration restored from the date of suspension, 18 June 2026 (¶20).
- **Weight.** This is a short oral judgment of a single judge that cites no precedent. It is persuasive outside the Gauhati High Court and its force lies in the statutory reading.

## 1. Introduction

A GST registration is the licence to trade in the tax system. Without it a supplier cannot issue tax invoices and its customers cannot claim credit on its supplies. The power to cancel or suspend a registration is therefore among the most consequential an indirect tax officer holds, and High Courts hear a steady volume of writ petitions against cancellations on grounds the statute does not obviously support.

*Mohammed Luthfur Rahman* is one such case, and a clean one. The petitioner carried on business as Euro Fashion in Guwahati from a principal place and an additional place. The additional place was in the middle of a tenancy dispute. When the State tax officer asked for the rent agreement and the petitioner could not supply it for that premises, the officer cancelled the registration. The Gauhati High Court asked a simple question: where in the Act, the Rules or the registration form is a registered person required to hold a rent agreement for an additional place of business, and is the lack of one a ground for cancellation? It found no such requirement.

The decision matters for three reasons. First, it ties the cancellation power back to its textual source, Section 29(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (the CGST Act), and the prescribed grounds in Rule 21 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 (the CGST Rules), rather than allowing a documentary default to be treated as a general ground. Second, it reads the registration form, Form GST REG-01, as part of the Rules and treats its list of documents as the measure of what an applicant must provide. Third, it deals with a recurring situation in which a trader's premises are held under a contested tenancy.

This article sets out the facts and procedural history, analyses the reasoning on Section 29, Rule 21 and Form REG-01, and notes what the Court did not decide. It then considers the consequences for registered persons and the Department.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

**The parties and the business.** The petitioner, Mohammed Luthfur Rahman, carries on business under the name and style of M/s Euro Fashion. His principal place of business is on S.S. Road, Lakhtokia, Guwahati. He also declared an additional place of business, located opposite Sikh Mandir on S.S. Road, Fancy Bazar, Guwahati. He is registered under both the CGST Act and the Assam Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, and the registration certificate records both places (¶¶3–4). The respondents were the Union of India, the State of Assam and the Assistant Commissioner of State Tax, Guwahati, who passed the order under challenge (¶2).

**The tenancy dispute.** The petitioner and the landlord of the additional place were in litigation. A suit, Title Suit No. 101/2026, was pending, and in Misc. (J) Case No. 195/2026 an injunction directed the parties to maintain status quo as to the suit premises. The petitioner had been depositing the rent in respect of that premises under Section 5(4) of the Assam Urban Area Rent Control Act, 1972, and the deposit challans were annexed to the writ petition (¶¶5–6).

**The show cause notice and suspension.** On 18 June 2026 the proper officer issued a show cause notice asking why the registration should not be cancelled for failure to submit the rent agreement. The registration was put under suspension when the notice was issued. The petitioner replied in detail. For the principal place, he said there was no dispute and the rent agreement had been furnished. For the additional place, he explained the pending suit and injunction. No decision followed, and the petitioner approached the High Court in WP(C) No. 3519/2026 (¶7).

**The first writ petition.** A coordinate Bench disposed of that petition on 26 June 2026. It directed the officer to consider the reply and pass an order under Section 29 of the CGST Act read with Rules 21A and 22 of the CGST Rules, as expeditiously as possible and within an outer limit of 15 days from service of a certified copy of the order with the petition and annexures (¶7).

**The cancellation.** By an order dated 14 July 2026 the registration was cancelled "on the ground that the Petitioner failed to submit the rent agreement" (¶8). A speaking order of the same date was annexed to the respondents' affidavit-in-opposition (¶10). The petitioner filed the present petition, WP(C) No. 3857 of 2026, and notice was issued on 10 August 2026 (¶9).

**The hearing.** Mr. S. Das appeared for the petitioner, Mr. B. Gogoi, Additional Advocate General, Assam, for the State respondents, and Mr. K. Gogoi, Central Government Counsel, for the Union. The judgment was pronounced orally on 12 October 2026.

**The issue.** The judgment frames no issues formally, but one question runs through it: whether failure to furnish a rent agreement for an additional place of business is a ground on which a registration can be cancelled under Section 29(2) of the CGST Act and Rule 21 of the CGST Rules. No tax, interest or penalty was in issue; the case concerns the registration alone.

**The holding.** The Court held that cancelling the registration for not submitting the rent agreement for the additional place "appears to be contrary to the provisions of the CGST Act of 2017, the Rules of 2017 as well as the Form REG-01" (¶19). It quashed the notice of 18 June 2026, quashed the speaking order and the cancellation order of 14 July 2026, restored the registration with effect from the date of suspension, 18 June 2026, and made no order as to costs (¶20).

## 3. Legal Analysis

### 3.1 The statutory scheme: Section 29(2) and its prescribed grounds

The Court began with Section 29(2) of the CGST Act, which it reproduced with its provisos (¶12). The proper officer may cancel a registration from a date he deems fit, including a retrospective date, in five situations: contravention of provisions of the Act or Rules "as may be prescribed" (clause (a)); failure of a composition taxpayer to furnish the annual return beyond three months of the due date (clause (b)); failure of any other registered person to furnish returns for the continuous tax period prescribed (clause (c)); failure of a voluntarily registered person to commence business within six months (clause (d)); and registration obtained by fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts (clause (e)). The first proviso bars cancellation without an opportunity of being heard. The second allows suspension while the proceedings are pending.

The Court dismissed clauses (b) to (e) in a single sentence: they had "no application" on the facts (¶13). The Court treated clause (a) as the only provision that could "at best" apply.

### 3.2 Clause (a) and Rule 21

Clause (a) does not itself say which contraventions suffice. It delegates that to the Rules. The Court therefore went to Rule 21 of the CGST Rules, which it set out in full: a registration "is liable to be cancelled" if the person does not conduct any business from the declared place of business; issues invoices without supply; violates Section 171 on anti-profiteering; violates Rule 10A; avails input tax credit in violation of Section 16; furnishes outward supply details in FORM GSTR-1 in excess of those in the return; violates Rule 86B; violates the third or fourth proviso to Rule 23(1); or fails to file returns for six continuous months (monthly filers) or two continuous tax periods (quarterly filers) (¶13).

The Court then read the first limb of the Rule in terms of its purpose. If a person "has declared a particular place of business and upon inspection being carried out, it is found that the person is no longer carrying on the business in the same location, the registration of the person can be cancelled" (¶14). That reads Rule 21(a) as a rule about conduct of business at a location. A failure to produce a document is a different thing from a failure to trade.

The Court also drew support from Rule 25, which deals with physical verification of business premises, as showing that the scheme contemplates the officer verifying on the ground whether business is carried on at the declared place (¶15). The Court did not say, in terms, that Rule 21 is an exhaustive list or that it supplies the only "prescribed" contraventions for clause (a). Its conclusion was narrower: nothing in Rule 21 covers non-submission of a rent agreement for an additional place of business.

### 3.3 Form REG-01 and the principal–additional distinction

The decisive step is the Court's treatment of Form GST REG-01. Rule 8 of the CGST Rules provides for the application for registration and the form requires documents to be uploaded. The Court observed that the list of documents under the heading "Proof of Principal Place of Business" covers owned premises (property tax receipt, municipal khata copy or electricity bill), rented or leased premises (a valid rent or lease agreement with proof of the lessor's ownership), premises covered by neither (a consent letter with ownership proof), and rented or leased premises where no agreement is available (an affidavit with a document showing possession such as an electricity bill) (¶16). It then made the point that matters: "the documents which are to be uploaded only pertains to the proof of principal place of business and has no relation as regards the proof of additional place of business" (¶16).

From this the Court drew two conclusions. First, a registered person is not required by the form to prove the additional place of business by a rent agreement, so cancelling for not producing one has no footing in the Rules, of which the form is a part (¶19). Second, even for the principal place, "there is no requirement of a rent agreement if an affidavit to that effect along with any document in support of possession of the premises like copy of the electricity bill is submitted" (¶19). The demand was therefore doubly misplaced: the agreement for the principal place had in fact been furnished, and the dispute concerned only the additional place (¶¶7, 15).

The reasoning has practical force. The form states what may be required at registration. When an officer later demands a document the form never asked for, as a condition of keeping the registration, the officer adds a requirement the rule-maker left out.

### 3.4 Remedy, limitation and the questions the Court did not reach

The Court decided one question only. It did not address limitation, jurisdiction or whether an alternative remedy barred the writ. The statute provides remedies against a cancellation order: an application for revocation of cancellation under Section 30 of the CGST Act, which the CGST Rules require within 30 days of service of the cancellation order, and an appeal under Section 107. The judgment records no objection on these lines and does not discuss them, so it cannot be cited as authority that a writ lies against a cancellation order despite those remedies.

The Court also did not decide:

- whether the petitioner was in fact carrying on business at the additional premises, since no inspection report is discussed;
- whether the registration could be cancelled on a different, properly framed ground after a fresh notice; or
- the effect of the suspension on the petitioner's supplies during the suspension, beyond restoring the registration from 18 June 2026.

### 3.5 Precedent, amendment and critique

The judgment cites no authority, and the text records none cited by counsel. The only earlier decision mentioned is the coordinate Bench order of 26 June 2026 in WP(C) No. 3519/2026, a procedural direction that decided nothing on the merits. The analysis rests entirely on the statutory text and the form.

That makes the reasoning easy to apply but tied to the text as it stood. The Court quoted Section 29(2) as amended, Rule 21 as it then read, and the form's list of documents. If the rule-maker later amends Form GST REG-01 to require proof of additional places of business, or adds a limb to Rule 21 dealing with premises documentation, the analysis would need to be revisited. The holding is a statement that, on these provisions, the ground was not available. It is not a statement that the Department may never require proof of an additional place of business.

Three observations on the strength of the reasoning are fair. First, the form-based argument is strong and verifiable from the face of the form. Second, the reading of Rule 21(a) as concerned with conduct of business is persuasive but briefly stated. A stronger judgment would have said whether Rule 21 exhaustively lists the prescribed contraventions under Section 29(2)(a), because similar cases will turn on that point. Third, the Court did not engage with the argument the Department might have made, that a registered person who cannot show a right to occupy a declared place may not be conducting business from it. The reply is that this is a matter of fact to be established on inspection under Rule 25, not by a demand for a document the form does not require. The judgment points that way without saying it in terms.

## 4. Practical Significance

**For registered persons with additional places of business.** The decision gives a clear line of defence against a show cause notice that asks for a lease or rent agreement for an additional place. The reply should identify the ground relied on in the notice, show that it does not correspond to any limb of Section 29(2) or Rule 21, and refer to the documents Form REG-01 asks for. If the notice concerns the principal place, the reply should point to the alternatives in the form: an affidavit with proof of possession such as an electricity bill, where a rent agreement is unavailable.

**For traders in disputed premises.** A landlord dispute does not by itself end the right to hold a registration. The petitioner's rent deposits under the rent control statute and the status quo injunction were part of the factual background (¶¶5–6). They should keep the court record, deposit challans and any interim order at hand to show possession and continued business.

**For suspension and the response window.** The registration here was suspended on the date of the notice, and the petitioner had to go to the High Court for a direction to decide his reply. Suspension stops a business from issuing tax invoices in the ordinary way, so the practical response is to reply within time and, if the officer does not decide, to seek a time-bound direction as the petitioner did in WP(C) No. 3519/2026. Restoration from 18 June 2026 treats the registration as continuing from suspension, though the judgment does not address credit questions for that period.

**For the choice of remedy.** The Court's willingness to decide the merits in a writ should not be taken as a licence to skip the statutory route. The judgment does not discuss Section 30, Section 107 or the 30-day period for a revocation application. A prudent practitioner will consider filing the revocation application or appeal within time, in parallel with or instead of a writ, unless the facts show, as here, a prior High Court direction and an order that fails on the face of the statute.

**For the Department.** An order of cancellation must rest on a ground that corresponds to Section 29(2) and, under clause (a), to a prescribed contravention. Where the real concern is that the registered person no longer carries on business at a declared location, the show cause notice should say so, and the officer should verify under Rule 25 and record the findings. A notice that complains only that a document has not been furnished, when the form does not require the document, is likely to be quashed. The Department remains free to act on a properly framed ground after fresh notice.

**For citation.** The judgment was delivered orally and the text carries no reportable designation. It is a single-judge decision, persuasive only outside the Gauhati High Court, and should be cited for the proposition it decides: on Section 29(2)(a), Rule 21 and Form REG-01 as set out in the judgment, failure to furnish a rent agreement for an additional place of business is not a ground for cancellation.

## 5. Conclusion

*Mohammed Luthfur Rahman* decides a narrow point and decides it correctly. A GST registration may be cancelled only on a ground that Section 29(2) of the CGST Act supplies. For contraventions under clause (a), the ground must be a prescribed one, and Rule 21 of the CGST Rules lists what is prescribed. Nothing in that list, and nothing in Form GST REG-01, makes a rent agreement for an additional place of business a condition of holding a registration. The form asks for proof of the principal place of business, and even for that place it accepts an affidavit with proof of possession. The Court restored the registration from the date of suspension.

The judgment is more useful for its method than its facts. It reads the Act, the Rules and the form together, asks what the rule-maker actually required, and declines to let a documentary default stand in for a statutory ground. That method serves registered persons challenged on paper compliance, and officers who want their orders to survive scrutiny.

Its limits are clear. It cites no authority, it does not say whether Rule 21 is an exhaustive list for clause (a), it does not discuss the statutory remedies of revocation and appeal, and it quashes the notice without barring a fresh and properly framed one. The reasoning depends on the text of the provisions as it stood on the date of the judgment, and an amendment to the form or the Rule could change the result. The likely next question is whether a person who cannot show any right to occupy a declared place can be said to conduct business from it. Until then, *Mohammed Luthfur Rahman* is a sound and citable authority for the proposition that a missing rent agreement for an additional place of business does not justify cancelling a GST registration.

**Quick answer:** In *Commissioner, Customs, Noida v. Siddhi Vinayak Importers*, decided on 14 October 2026 by a two-member Bench of the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal ("CESTAT"), Regional Bench, Allahabad (P.K. Choudhary, Member (Judicial), and K. Anpazhakan, Member (Technical)), the Department's appeals against an Order-in-Appeal dated 25 April 2019 were rejected. That order had set aside the enhancement of declared values on 29 consignments of polyester fabric imported from China. Applying Rule 12 of the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007 and Section 17 of the Customs Act, 1962, the Tribunal held that a letter in which the importer accepts a higher assessable value neither waives the mandatory Rule 12(2) intimation of the grounds for doubting the declared value nor takes away the importer's right to question the reassessment, and that an enhancement resting on bare statements about "contemporaneous imports", or on National Import Database ("NIDB") data without independent corroboration, cannot stand.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Written consent narrows the speaking order, not the right of challenge.** Following the Delhi High Court in *Niraj Silk Mills v. Commissioner of Customs (ICD) Patparganj*, the Tribunal treated an importer's written acceptance of reassessment under Section 17 of the Customs Act, 1962 as relieving the officer only of the duty to pass a speaking order. It does not extinguish the statutory right to contest the reassessment on formation of opinion or on merits.
- **The Rule 12(2) intimation cannot be bypassed or waived.** Relying on *Century Metal Recycling Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India*, the Tribunal held that forming a reasonable doubt about declared value and communicating the grounds to the importer is mandatory, and that the practice of circumventing it is unacceptable.
- **A consent letter that merely asserts lower declared value proves nothing.** The acceptance letters in this case did not set out any contemporaneous import data. The Tribunal held that a bare statement that the declared value is "liable to be rejected" does not meet the Valuation Rules.
- **Comparison with identical or similar goods has to be real.** The Tribunal noted that even Rule 5 requires attention to quantity in comparable commercial transactions, GSM, quality and time of placement of the order.
- **NIDB data alone is not enough.** Quoting the Delhi High Court, the Tribunal confirmed that valuation additions must be shored up by independent and cogent evidence.
- **Persuasive, not binding, authority.** This is a Tribunal order that follows higher authority; its value lies in the reasoning it adopts and its application to coerced-consent facts.

## 1. Introduction

Customs clearance is time-sensitive. Containers incur demurrage and ground rent, buyers wait, and importers have a practical incentive to accept almost any assessment that releases the goods. The Department has sometimes obtained a short letter agreeing that the declared transaction value is too low and may be raised, and then argued that, having consented, the importer cannot appeal.

*Siddhi Vinayak Importers* confronts that argument on a large record: twenty-nine bills of entry, a single Order-in-Appeal that allowed every importer appeal, and twenty-nine departmental appeals listed together. The Tribunal's answer is brief but consistent with the two authorities it follows. The first is the Supreme Court's decision in *Century Metal Recycling Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India*, reported at 2019 (367) E.L.T. 3 (S.C.), which makes the Rule 12(2) intimation mandatory. The second is the Delhi High Court's judgment dated 27 November 2024 in *Niraj Silk Mills v. Commissioner of Customs (ICD) Patparganj* (CUSAA 26/2022), which holds that consent narrows only the speaking order and not the importer's remedies.

This article reads the order closely. Section 2 sets out the facts, the procedural history and the Tribunal's holding. Section 3 analyses each limb of the reasoning, the statutory scheme it engages, and the questions the order leaves open, including how a ruling described as prospective bears on assessments made before it. Section 4 considers what importers, customs brokers and the Department should change in practice. Section 5 draws the threads together.

## 2. Case summary and background

**The importer and the consignments.** The respondent, Siddhi Vinayak Importers of Rohtak, Haryana, is described in the order as a regular importer of different kinds of fabrics. It imported 29 consignments of fabrics from China. The goods were declared as a "Mixed Lot of 100% Polyester Knitted Fabrics (Rolls of Assorted Colors and Weight)". Duty was self-assessed on the invoice values. The order states neither the duty in dispute nor the dates of import.

**The importer's account.** According to the importer, as recorded by the Tribunal, no "Out of Charge Orders" were passed on the consignments. The importer made written requests to the proper officer to clear the goods provisionally, paying duty on the enhanced value under protest, in order to avoid delay. It contended that no cognizance was taken of those requests. It was "coerced" into submitting letters of consent agreeing to assessment and valuation by the customs authorities. The importer's case was that the authorities discarded the declared transaction value, and enhanced the value on the strength of the consent letters, without observing the mandate of Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962.

The Tribunal accepted that the acceptance letters "were not voluntary" (para 5). It recorded that the importer then wrote several letters requesting a speaking order and received none, and so filed appeals before the Commissioner (Appeals) against the assessment on each bill of entry.

**The Department's account.** The Department's position before the Commissioner (Appeals), as the Tribunal records it, was that reassessment had been made under Section 17(4) of the Customs Act on the basis of written consent, so that no speaking order was needed. Its memorandum of appeal before the Tribunal told a partly different story. It asserted that the adjudicating authority had passed three speaking orders under Section 17(5) on the reassessment of 24 bills of entry after show cause notices, and that Order-in-Original No. 205/AG-3/ICD-DD/17 dated 14 March 2018 covered 17 bills of entry. On that footing it argued that the 17 individual appeals before the Commissioner (Appeals) duplicated the appeal against the speaking order and were infructuous.

**The Commissioner (Appeals).** By Order-in-Appeal Nos. NOI-CUSTM-000-APP-29 to 57-19-20 dated 25 April 2019, the Commissioner (Appeals), CGST, Noida allowed the importer's appeals. He held that the rejection of the declared value and the enhancement for assessment of duty were legally unsustainable, and set aside the assessment orders on all the bills of entry. The Department's own grounds record that the Commissioner (Appeals) found no reasons to doubt the truth or accuracy of the declared value and nothing to infer additional remittance.

**The departmental appeals.** The Department appealed to the Tribunal (Customs Appeal No. 70604 of 2019, with Customs Appeal Nos. 71120 to 71147 of 2024). Its grounds were that: the Commissioner (Appeals) overlooked Rule 12 of the Valuation Rules, under which the officer "may" ask for further information and must intimate the grounds for doubt only "at the request of" the importer; that nothing showed the importer made any such request or protested the reassessment as Section 17(5) contemplates; that the speaking orders did record comparative data of contemporaneous imports; that the individual appeals were duplicative; and that the "mixed lot" description of the comparison goods matched the importer's own declaration.

**Issues and holding.** The Tribunal framed the primary issue as whether the Commissioner (Appeals) was correct in holding that rejection of the declared value and enhancement of value were not sustainable. It answered yes. It held that the letters of acceptance did not evidence contemporaneous imports and could not by themselves satisfy the Valuation Rules; that Rule 12(2), as read by the Supreme Court in *Century Metal Recycling*, cannot be ignored or waived; and that the appeals were "squarely covered" by the Delhi High Court's judgment in *Niraj Silk Mills* (para 8). It concluded that the Orders-in-Appeal were upheld and the Department's appeals rejected (para 12).

## 3. Legal analysis

### The statutory scheme

Three layers of law interact. First, Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962 directs that the value of imported goods is the transaction value, adjusted as the Valuation Rules provide. Second, Rule 12(1) of the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007 permits the proper officer who has "reason to doubt the truth or accuracy of the value declared" to ask for further information; if doubt persists after the response, the transaction value is deemed incapable of determination under Rule 3(1). Rule 12(2) then adds that, "at the request of an importer", the officer "shall intimate the importer in writing the grounds for doubting the truth or accuracy of the value declared" and must give a reasonable opportunity of being heard before a final decision. Only after valid rejection of transaction value may the officer move to the sequential methods in Rules 4 to 9, beginning with identical goods.

Third, Section 17 governs assessment. Self-assessment may be verified and, under Section 17(4), the proper officer may reassess. Section 17(5), as quoted in the order, provides that where reassessment "is contrary to the self-assessment done by the importer or exporter regarding valuation of goods, classification, exemption or concessions of duty", and "in cases other than those where the importer or exporter, as the case may be, confirms his acceptance of the said re-assessment in writing", the officer "shall pass a speaking order on the re-assessment, within fifteen days from the date of re-assessment". The Department built its case on the text of these two provisions: Rule 12(2) is triggered only on request, and Section 17(5) speaks of acceptance in writing.

### Issue one: does written acceptance waive the right to challenge?

The Tribunal's answer is no, and its reasoning is borrowed entirely from *Niraj Silk Mills*. The order reproduces the Delhi High Court's reading of Section 17(5): the concession it contemplates "is at best confined to the speaking Order which the proper officer is obliged to frame in affirmation of the provisional opinion that it may have formed under Section 17(4)". Nothing in Rule 12(2) contemplates any concession or waiver "at least in explicit terms". And "the right to question the correctness of the decision of the proper officer, be it with respect to the formation of opinion or even on merits, is one which is protected by statute" (as quoted at para 10).

The reading is textually sound. Section 17(5) lifts one obligation, the speaking order, when the importer accepts the reassessment in writing. It says nothing about appeals. A waiver of a statutory right of appeal is not lightly inferred from silence, and a consent letter obtained to secure release of cargo is a weak foundation for one. The Tribunal added a factual finding that the acceptance letters were not voluntary (para 5), which leaves the Department with no consent to rely on even if waiver were available in principle.

The Tribunal also noted that the Department had earlier succeeded before the Tribunal on the opposite view in the *Hanuman Prasad* group of matters, where it was held that once a letter of acceptance was submitted, importers could not question the assessment later (para 8). The Delhi High Court reversed that approach in *Niraj Silk Mills*. This history matters for readers who find older Tribunal decisions in databases: the *Hanuman Prasad* line, as described in the order, no longer reflects the law.

### Issue two: is the Rule 12(2) intimation mandatory despite the words "at the request of an importer"?

The Department's best textual point was that Rule 12(1) says the officer "may" ask for information and Rule 12(2) operates only on request. The Tribunal did not engage with the text directly. It relied on *Century Metal Recycling*, which, as the Tribunal reads it, "held that the mandate of sub Rule (2) of Rule 12 cannot be ignored or waived", that "[f]ormation of opinion regarding reasonable doubt as to the truth or accuracy of the valuation and communication of the said ground to the importer is mandatory", and that the Supreme Court "deprecated the practice of by passing and circumventing the statutory mandate as un-acceptable" (para 7). The Tribunal added that this requirement was made mandatory "by invoking the doctrine of prospective application".

Two observations follow. First, the Tribunal drew a factual parallel: in *Century Metal Recycling*, as in this case, the importer's requests for provisional assessment were ignored and a letter of acceptance was extracted (para 9). Second, the Tribunal did not say how the prospective character of that ruling bears on the present assessments. On the Department's own grounds, the speaking order covering 17 bills of entry is dated 14 March 2018, and *Century Metal Recycling* is reported in the 2019 volume of the E.L.T. The order does not discuss whether assessments made before that decision can attract the Rule 12(2) mandate. The Tribunal appears to have treated the Delhi High Court's later application of the principle as sufficient. A more careful order would have addressed the point, because a Department seeking a reference or an appeal would begin there.

### Issue three: what must a valuation enhancement contain?

Independently of waiver, the Tribunal found that the substance of the enhancement was missing. The acceptance letters merely stated that the declared value was liable to be rejected and re-determined on the basis of contemporaneous import data; they did not set out that data. The Tribunal held that "merely mentioning that the declared value is liable to be rejected and the same is liable to be assessed at a particular price does not meet the requirement of Customs Valuations Rules, 2007", because even for applying Rule 5 "several parameters like quantity in comparable commercial transactions, GSM, quality, time of placement of Order for import etc. has to be fulfilled" (para 6). It noted, too, that although the letters recited that details of contemporaneous imports had been shown to the importer, "no such details of alleged contemporaneous import data have been mentioned" (para 9).

On NIDB data, the Tribunal adopted the Delhi High Court's statement that the Tribunal has consistently found a valuation addition based solely on NIDB data unwarranted, and that any such reassessment must be shored up by "independent and cogent evidence". It added that mere reliance on external data "without corroborative evidence or clear justification would fail to meet the tests and principles" of the Valuation Rules (para 11). The NIDB is the Department's compilation of declared import values. It is a useful alert for risk management, but, as the Delhi High Court reasoned, it is not itself evidence of what a comparable importer paid in a comparable transaction.

### What the Tribunal did not decide

The order is silent on several points the Department raised. It did not rule on whether the 17 individual appeals duplicated the appeal against the speaking order dated 14 March 2018, so no precedent on maintainability of duplicative appeals emerges. It did not reconcile the Department's two accounts of whether speaking orders were passed. It did not address limitation, interest, penalty or any consequential refund, and it does not discuss amendments to Section 17 or Rule 12 after the assessments in question. Readers should not read the order as authority on those questions.

### A critique

The result is right, and the route is conventional: a Tribunal following a High Court that construed the statute and a Supreme Court that made a procedural step mandatory. Two weaknesses deserve notice. The first is the unaddressed prospectivity point. The second is the thin treatment of the Department's textual argument on Rule 12(2): the order relies on authority rather than explaining why a requirement framed around a "request" is mandatory whether or not a request was made. Neither undermines the outcome, because the acceptance letters lacked data and the consent was found involuntary. They do limit the order's utility where consent was genuine and the assessment order was reasoned.

## 4. Practical significance

**For importers and customs brokers.** The first lesson is to treat any request for a letter of acceptance as a decision with consequences. The order helps importers who signed under pressure, but it did not hold that consent letters are always ineffective; it found coercion on the facts and found the letters empty of evidence. Where an importer signs to obtain clearance, the contemporaneous record matters. Written requests for provisional clearance on payment under protest, letters asking for a speaking order, and any request for the Rule 12(2) grounds should be sent in writing and kept on file. They were central here, and they answer the Department's argument that no request under Rule 12(2) was shown.

Second, an importer who has already signed should not assume the right to appeal is gone. The Delhi High Court's reading, applied here, treats the right to question both the formation of opinion and the merits as statutory. An appeal against the assessment on each bill of entry was entertained in this case, and the Commissioner (Appeals)'s order allowing them was upheld.

**For the Department.** The order sets a practical standard for a defensible enhancement: a recorded reason to doubt, communication of the grounds, comparison data that identifies quantity, quality, GSM and timing, and a response to the importer's objections. NIDB values may support the opening of an inquiry but cannot be the whole reasoned order. Where the Department relies on a consent letter, it should ensure the letter records the grounds and data on which the importer is said to have agreed, since a letter that does not cannot answer Rule 12(2).

**Choice of forum and process.** The case went through the ordinary statutory route: reassessment, an appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals), and a departmental appeal to the Tribunal. It was not a writ. An importer in this position generally needs the appellate record: the bills of entry, the acceptance letters, and the correspondence asking for provisional release and a speaking order. Writ jurisdiction is not engaged by this order and nothing in it suggests otherwise.

**Precedential weight.** The order is a decision of a two-member Bench of the Tribunal and carries no reportability designation. It binds the parties and is persuasive in other proceedings. Its authority is derived from the Supreme Court and Delhi High Court decisions it applies. Within the Delhi High Court's territorial jurisdiction, *Niraj Silk Mills* is the controlling authority; elsewhere it is persuasive but weighty, and the Supreme Court's decision in *Century Metal Recycling* binds all. Counsel should cite those decisions directly and use this order as an illustration of their application to coerced-consent facts.

**Litigation strategy.** An importer relying on this order should plead the three strands together: absence of voluntary consent, absence of Rule 12(2) intimation, and absence of comparable evidence. The Department, for its part, should expect the Rule 12(2) point to be taken in every enhancement matter and should prepare to show the request, the grounds and the data.

---

## 5. Conclusion

*Siddhi Vinayak Importers* is a modest order with a useful message. The Tribunal did not announce a new rule. It applied the Supreme Court's insistence in *Century Metal Recycling* that the Rule 12(2) intimation is mandatory and the Delhi High Court's holding in *Niraj Silk Mills* that written acceptance of a reassessment relieves the officer only of the speaking order, not the importer of the right to question the decision. To those it added an evidentiary point on the facts: a consent letter that does not disclose any contemporaneous import data, and a valuation addition that rests on departmental database values alone, cannot displace declared transaction value.

The order's weaknesses are those of brevity. It does not explain why a rule framed around a "request" should operate when no request is shown, it does not address the prospective character of *Century Metal Recycling* in relation to assessments made before it, and it leaves the Department's maintainability and merits points undecided. Those gaps matter most where the importer's consent was genuine or the assessing officer's reasoning was documented, and the order should not be stretched to those cases.

For practitioners, the direction of travel is clear. Customs valuation disputes are moving away from a regime in which a short letter closes the file and toward one in which the proper officer must form a recorded doubt, communicate it, and support any enhancement with comparable evidence. Importers who have signed acceptance letters should review their position with that framework in mind, and the Department's field formations should review their forms.

## FAQ

**Does signing a letter accepting an enhanced customs value stop an importer from appealing?**
Not according to this order. Following the Delhi High Court in *Niraj Silk Mills*, the Tribunal treated written acceptance under Section 17(5) as relieving the officer of the duty to pass a speaking order only. The importer's statutory right to question the reassessment remains.

**Is the officer bound to give reasons for doubting declared value even if the importer does not ask?**
The Tribunal, relying on *Century Metal Recycling*, held that forming a reasonable doubt and communicating the grounds under Rule 12(2) is mandatory and cannot be ignored or waived, even though the rule is framed around a request by the importer.

**Can the Department enhance value using NIDB data alone?**
No. The Tribunal quoted the Delhi High Court's position that a valuation addition resting only on NIDB data requires independent and cogent evidence and a clear justification.

**Does this order decide whether duplicate appeals against bills of entry and a speaking order are maintainable?**
No. The Tribunal did not separately rule on the Department's objection that the 17 individual appeals duplicated the appeal against the speaking order.

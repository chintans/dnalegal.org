**Quick answer:** In cross-appeals arising from a common Order-in-Original, CESTAT's East Regional Bench at Kolkata (Ashok Jindal, Member Judicial, and K. Anpazhakan, Member Technical) held, by Final Order Nos. 76212-76213/2026 pronounced on 25 September 2026, that a show cause notice dated 31 August 2018 — which asserted that Rule 3 of the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012 applied to foreign currency payments made by a shipping company, without explaining why the deductions the company had claimed under Rules 4, 9 and 10 were wrong — could not sustain either the reverse-charge demand of Rs. 143,01,41,936 that the Revenue sought to revive on appeal, or the invocation of the extended period of limitation for October 2012 to June 2017. The Tribunal separately set aside a confirmed demand of Rs. 1,26,16,689 raised on demurrage charges paid by the company for June 2016 to March 2017, holding that demurrage is a penal charge for delay, not consideration for a taxable service. The result: the Revenue's appeal against the dropped Rs. 143.01 crore was dismissed, and the assessee's appeal against the confirmed Rs. 1.26 crore was allowed — disposing of the entire Rs. 144,27,58,625 demand in the assessee's favour.

## Key Takeaways

- **A show cause notice must say why the taxpayer's own claim is wrong, not merely assert the contrary.** Where an assessee has claimed a deduction under specific rules in its returns, a notice that simply invokes a different, default rule — without stating why the rules actually claimed do not apply — is vague, and an adjudicating authority who accepts the taxpayer's explanation on that basis cannot be faulted for want of a "speaking order."
- **Audit-detected facts already available to the Revenue defeat the extended period.** Because the demand traced to scrutiny of the assessee's own ST-3 returns and an audit, and the assessee's claim to deductions was visible on the face of those returns, the Tribunal held the extended period of limitation was not invokable for want of suppression.
- **The burden of proving taxability, and the inapplicability of a claimed exemption or deduction, rests on the Revenue.** The Tribunal held the Revenue had not discharged that burden merely by asserting, without documentary engagement, that the adjudicating authority's order was "non-speaking."
- **Verification of the place of actual performance matters only for a performance-based deduction.** The Tribunal accepted the Revenue's own submission that scrutiny of where a service was physically performed is relevant to Rule 4 of the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012, but not to Rules 9 or 10, and confined its analysis accordingly.
- **Demurrage is not consideration for a service.** Following its own earlier order in the assessee's case and a coordinate Delhi bench's reasoning on container detention charges, the Tribunal held that a charge compensating for delay in loading, unloading or returning equipment is a penal charge — liquidated damages for breach, not a return promise under a contract — and falls outside the service tax net altogether.

## 1. Introduction

Cross-border service transactions occupy an uneasy corner of Indian indirect tax law. Under both the erstwhile service tax regime and the Goods and Services Tax that replaced it, a recipient of services from a supplier located outside India can be made liable to pay tax on a reverse-charge basis — but only if the transaction is treated, under a specific set of place-of-supply rules, as having been received within the taxable territory. Where a business claims that a particular payment falls under a rule that looks to the place of performance, the nature of the service, or the location of goods being transported, and the Revenue instead insists on a default territorial rule, the dispute is rarely about facts alone; it is about which of several textually plausible rules governs, and — just as often — about whether the notice raising the demand said enough to put the taxpayer to its defence in the first place.

This appeal, decided by the East Regional Bench of the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal at Kolkata, presents both problems at once, wrapped around a demand large enough that its outcome mattered on its own terms: Rs. 144,27,58,625, spread across fifteen categories of imported services procured by a shipping company. The adjudicating authority dropped nearly all of it, accepting the company's reading of the place-of-supply rules; the Revenue appealed nearly the entire drop, arguing that the adjudicating authority's reasoning was too thin to stand; the company cross-appealed the small residual demand that had been confirmed against it, arguing that what the Revenue had taxed as freight was, in substance, an unrelated penalty payment. This article works through the Tribunal's reasoning on the adequacy of the show cause notice, the extended period of limitation, the burden of proof, and the demurrage question, before turning to what the decision means for compliance and litigation strategy in reverse-charge disputes — under both the surviving service tax legacy and, with appropriate care about regime differences, the place-of-supply architecture that now governs the same commercial questions under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017.

## 2. Case Summary and Background

The appellant/respondent, M/s India Steamship, is a division of Chambal Fertilisers and Chemicals Limited engaged in the shipping business through two principal revenue streams: voyage chartering, transporting petroleum products by vessel for freight, and time chartering, providing vessels on a time basis for a fixed rent. The company discharged service tax on these activities under both the forward charge and the reverse charge mechanism. As a global shipping operation, it also incurred substantial foreign currency expenses procuring services — including steamer agency services, maintenance and repair, business auxiliary services, technical inspection and certification, and the transportation of goods by coastal shipping and by air — from suppliers located outside India. In its ST-3 returns, the company disclosed these payments and claimed deductions or abatement on the footing that the relevant services fell under Rule 4 (performance-based services), Rule 9 (specified services, including intermediary services) or Rule 10 (goods transportation services) of the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012 ("POPS Rules"), rather than under the default territorial rule, Rule 3.

The company had faced a materially similar demand before, for the earlier period 2007-08 to 2011-12, under the since-superseded Taxation of Services (Provided from Outside India and Received in India) Rules, 2006 — the "positive list" predecessor to the POPS Rules. That demand was set aside by the same Tribunal by Final Order Nos. 76064-76065/2024 dated 11 June 2024, a decision the Revenue subsequently accepted.

For the period October 2012 to June 2017, the Revenue issued a fresh show cause notice on 31 August 2018, based on scrutiny of the company's ST-3 returns and an audit, invoking the extended period of limitation. The notice proposed a reverse-charge service tax demand under Section 68(2) of the Finance Act, 1994, read with the reverse-charge Notification No. 30/2012-ST, treating Rule 3 of the POPS Rules as applicable across all fifteen categories of imported service — without addressing, on its face, why the deductions the company had claimed under Rules 4, 9 and 10 were not available.

The Commissioner of CGST & Central Excise, Kolkata, in Order-in-Original No. 50/Commr./CGST & CX/Kol/North/2024-25 dated 31 December 2024, examined the company's fifteen categories of service individually. The other fourteen categories — steamer agency services, maintenance and repair, business auxiliary services, and others of smaller value — were held to be correctly covered by Rules 4, 9 or 10 in their entirety, and their demand was dropped in full. Within the largest category, transport of goods by coastal shipping (total proposed demand of Rs. 1,07,83,53,384), the adjudicating authority dropped the demand for October 2012 to May 2016 — while coastal shipping remained on the negative list of services under Section 66D(p) of the Finance Act, 1994 — but confirmed a demand of Rs. 1,26,16,689 for the later period, June 2016 to March 2017, after coastal shipping had been removed from the negative list, treating certain foreign currency payments made during that window as consideration for coastal shipping transportation received on reverse charge. In all, Rs. 143,01,41,936 was dropped and Rs. 1,26,16,689 was confirmed, together with interest.

Both sides appealed. The Revenue's appeal (Service Tax Appeal No. 76110 of 2025) challenged the dropping of Rs. 143,01,41,936, contending that the adjudicating authority had accepted the company's submissions wholesale without independently examining invoices, agreements or payment records for each category of service, and had therefore passed a "non-speaking" order. The company's appeal (Service Tax Appeal No. 75621 of 2025) challenged the confirmed Rs. 1,26,16,689, contending that the underlying payments were not consideration for coastal shipping transportation at all, but demurrage — charges paid to overseas vessel owners for delay in loading or unloading cargo when the company chartered ships on a spot basis — and therefore fell outside the scope of taxable consideration altogether. The Tribunal heard both appeals together and disposed of them by a common Final Order.

## 3. Legal Analysis

**The adequacy of the show cause notice.** The Tribunal's starting point was that the 2018 notice invoked Rule 3 of the POPS Rules "without assigning any reason" for why Rules 4, 9 and 10 — the rules the company had claimed in its returns — did not apply. It treated this as more than a pleading nicety. Relying on the Supreme Court's holding in *Commissioner of Central Excise, Bangalore v. Brindavan Beverages (P) Ltd.* that a show cause notice is "the foundation on which the department has to build up its case," and on its own coordinate bench's reasoning in *Venkatesh Films Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner of CGST & Central Excise, Kolkata*, the Tribunal held that a notice which is silent on why a taxpayer's specific, disclosed claim is incorrect is vague, and that vagueness has a direct consequence for the adjudication that follows: an adjudicating authority who accepts the taxpayer's explanation, in the face of a notice that never engaged with it, cannot be said to have passed a legally deficient order for that reason alone. This reasoning did double duty against the Revenue's central appellate ground — that the Order-in-Original was "non-speaking" — because if the notice itself never framed a case against the specific deductions claimed, the adjudicating authority had nothing more specific to answer.

**The extended period of limitation.** The notice invoked the extended period for a five-year-plus span, October 2012 to June 2017, issued only in August 2018. The Tribunal found this unsustainable on two independent bases. First, the demand arose from scrutiny of the company's own ST-3 returns and an audit; since the relevant documents were with the Revenue during that audit, and the company's claim to deductions under Rules 4, 9 and 10 was apparent on the face of the returns, there was no concealment to found an allegation of suppression. Second, and reinforcing the point, the company had already been through a substantively similar dispute for the earlier 2007-08 to 2011-12 period, which the Tribunal had resolved in its favour by its 2024 order — a fact the Tribunal treated, applying the Supreme Court's decision in *Nizam Sugar Factory v. Collector of Central Excise, Andhra Pradesh*, as further undermining any suggestion that the company had suppressed facts material to a subsequent notice covering an overlapping subject matter. The Commissioner had sought to distinguish the earlier round on the footing that it arose under the "positive list" regime while the present notice arose under the negative list regime introduced from July 2012 — a distinction the Tribunal implicitly treated as insufficient to rescue the extended period, given that the underlying facts relevant to suppression (disclosure in returns, availability of documents on audit) did not turn on which regime applied.

**The burden of proof, and the Rule 4/Rule 9/Rule 10 distinction.** The Tribunal accepted the general proposition, pressed by the company, that the burden of establishing taxability — and of showing that a claimed deduction or exemption does not apply — lies on the Revenue. It held that the Revenue's appellate case, which catalogued, service category by service category, the adjudicating authority's supposed failure to examine invoices, contracts and payment records line by line, did not discharge that burden; a submission that the adjudicating authority ought to have done more does not itself establish that the underlying deductions were wrongly allowed. On the substantive classification question, the Tribunal drew a distinction of some practical utility: verification of the place where a service was actually performed, it held, is relevant to a claim for deduction under Rule 4 of the POPS Rules (the performance-based rule), but has no bearing on claims under Rule 9 (specified services, including intermediary and business-auxiliary-type services) or Rule 10 (goods transportation services), which turn on different criteria — the location of the intermediary or service provider, and the destination of the goods being transported, respectively. Because the company had furnished documentation supporting its claims under all three rules, and the notice had not engaged with the specific rule-by-rule basis for those claims, the Tribunal held the adjudicating authority was entitled to accept them, and affirmed the dropping of Rs. 143,01,41,936 in its entirety. The Revenue's appeal was accordingly dismissed.

**Demurrage as not consideration for a service.** On the company's own appeal, the Tribunal addressed the confirmed demand of Rs. 1,26,16,689 for June 2016 to March 2017 — the period after coastal shipping's removal from the negative list under Section 66D(p) of the Finance Act, 1994. The company's case was that these payments were not freight for transportation at all, but demurrage: sums paid to overseas owners of vessels chartered on a spot basis, on account of delay in loading or discharging cargo. The Tribunal accepted this characterisation and held demurrage to be a penal charge rather than consideration for a service, following its own reasoning in the company's earlier 2024 order — which had held that "such penal charges were not for any services received by the Appellant" and were accordingly outside the service tax net — and a coordinate Delhi bench's decision in *Tiger Logistics (India) Ltd. v. Commissioner of Service Tax-II, Delhi*, which had reached the same conclusion for container detention charges by reasoning that such payments are liquidated damages for breach of a return obligation, not consideration under the contract, and by relying on the Central Board of Excise and Customs' Circular No. 121/2/2010-ST dated 26 April 2010, which had clarified that container detention charges are "penal rent" rather than a taxable business support or business auxiliary service. On this reasoning, the Tribunal set aside the confirmed demand of Rs. 1,26,16,689 in its entirety, together with the consequential interest and penalty, and allowed the company's appeal.

**What the decision leaves open.** The Tribunal did not need to, and did not, decide whether demurrage would be treated identically in every factual configuration — its holding rests on the specific characterisation, accepted on the documents before it, that the disputed payments compensated for delay rather than for carriage. Nor did it resolve, because the point was not argued that way, whether the Commissioner's attempt to distinguish the company's own 2024 precedent on positive-list/negative-list lines was correct in principle; the Tribunal's limitation holding proceeded on the narrower and more directly dispositive ground that the relevant facts were already before the Revenue on audit, making the regime label immaterial to the suppression enquiry. Practitioners should also note what the decision does not say: it does not hold that a show cause notice invoking a default rule is invariably vague — only that a notice which is silent on why a taxpayer's specific, disclosed alternative claim is wrong fails to meet the foundational standard the Supreme Court set in *Brindavan Beverages*.

## 4. Practical Significance

For businesses importing services from overseas suppliers — shipping, logistics, and any sector with material cross-border service spend — this decision is a marker on how to defend a reverse-charge demand raised years later on audit. A taxpayer who has disclosed the payments and identified the specific rule relied on for a deduction, at the time of filing returns, has a strong answer to any later suppression allegation: if the claim was visible in the return and the documents were available on audit, the extended period becomes difficult to sustain whatever the classification outcome. Taxpayers who claim deductions without documenting the specific rule relied on in contemporaneous records are correspondingly worse placed to resist an extended-period notice on the same facts.

The judgment is also a template for responding to a vague notice. A taxpayer facing a notice that asserts a default rule without addressing a claimed alternative should record, in its reply, that the notice fails to engage with the specific basis for the claim — a foundational objection that can dispose of both the demand and the extended-period question together, without needing every classification dispute resolved on the merits.

For entities facing demurrage, detention or similar delay charges — a recurring feature in shipping, logistics and equipment leasing — the decision confirms that such payments fall outside consideration for a service, provided the contractual and commercial documents support that characterisation. Taxpayers should keep the same documentary support that succeeded here: contracts identifying the charge as a delay penalty, invoices with a discrete demurrage or detention line item, and consistency with their own prior orders or comparable Tribunal authority.

On departmental strategy, the decision cautions the Revenue that an appeal reciting, service by service, that the adjudicating authority ought to have examined more documents does not itself discharge the Revenue's burden of proof; an appellate ground must show what the documents actually establish, not merely that a fuller recitation would have been preferable.

Finally, the classification framework here — Rule 3 against Rules 4, 9 and 10 of the POPS Rules, framed under the Finance Act, 1994 — was superseded when that levy was subsumed into GST from 1 July 2017; tellingly, interest on the confirmed demand was itself computed with reference to Section 174(2) of the CGST Act, 2017, the savings clause keeping legacy liabilities alive. The equivalent place-of-supply questions — is a service performance-based, does it involve an intermediary, does it concern goods transportation — now arise under Sections 12 and 13 of the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, which are textually distinct from the POPS Rules. A favourable POPS Rules classification does not transfer automatically to GST; reliance on this decision in a GST dispute should be confined to its general principles — the standard for an adequate notice, the treatment of the extended period, and the burden of proof — not to the specific rule numbers construed.

## 5. Conclusion

*India Steamship* resolves a Rs. 144.27 crore reverse-charge dispute almost entirely on procedural and evidentiary footing rather than on a fresh classification ruling: the Revenue's notice never explained why the taxpayer's disclosed, rule-specific claims were wrong, the extended period could not be invoked where the relevant facts were before the Revenue on audit, and the burden of displacing a considered adjudication order lay with the Revenue, which did not discharge it merely by cataloguing what more the adjudicating authority might have examined. On the residual demurrage question, the Tribunal reaffirmed a settled line of authority — its own and a coordinate bench's — that a penal charge for delay is not consideration for a service. None of this announces new law so much as it applies, with some care, principles that recur across excise, service tax and GST litigation alike: a show cause notice is the department's case, not a placeholder for one to be filled in later; suppression requires concealment, not merely an unfavourable classification the Revenue later regrets accepting; and the party alleging taxability carries the burden of proving it. For businesses with material cross-border service transactions — in shipping and well beyond it — the practical lesson is to make the classification basis for a deduction visible at the time of filing, and to test any belated, broadly worded show cause notice against that disclosure before conceding the classification dispute the notice never actually made.

## FAQ

**Does this decision decide a new rule for classifying cross-border services under GST?**
No. The Tribunal construed the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012, framed under the Finance Act, 1994, which no longer apply to transactions after the introduction of GST from 1 July 2017. The equivalent questions under GST are governed by Sections 12 and 13 of the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, which are differently worded. The decision's value for GST disputes lies in its general reasoning on show cause notices, the extended period, and burden of proof, not in the specific rule numbers it applies.

**Can a taxpayer defeat an extended-period demand simply by having disclosed a transaction in its returns?**
Not automatically, but disclosure is central to the analysis. Here, the Tribunal found the extended period unavailable because the claim to deductions was visible in the ST-3 returns and the underlying documents were available to the Revenue during audit — facts that, together, were held inconsistent with the concealment that suppression requires.

**Is a demurrage or detention charge always outside the service tax or GST net?**
This decision holds that demurrage compensating for delay in loading, unloading or returning equipment is a penal charge, not consideration for a service, provided the commercial and contractual documents support that characterisation. The outcome in any given case still depends on how the charge is actually structured and documented.

**Quick answer:** In *Puneet Batra v. Union of India & Ors.*, decided on 18 September 2026, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Anil Kshetarpal and Shail Jain, JJ.) dismissed a writ petition filed by a practising advocate challenging a search conducted on 25 July 2025 at the office of his family's tax consultancy firm and the consequent seizure of a CPU, under an authorisation issued by the Additional Commissioner, Anti-Evasion, CGST Delhi East Commissionerate, under Section 67(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. GST authorities alleged the advocate was not merely representing a gaming company client but was personally involved in routing an estimated ₹1,306 crore of user deposits for an offshore online gambling platform, on which they claimed ₹365.68 crore in GST was payable. The Court held that a validly authorised search of a firm's premises is not rendered unauthorised merely because one cabin is used by an advocate, that advocate-client privilege under Section 132 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 protects client communications rather than an advocate's own conduct where he is alleged to be personally implicated, and that the appropriate remedy for protecting unrelated clients' confidential data lay in procedural safeguards over the seized electronic device, not in quashing the search itself.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Advocate-client privilege does not immunise an advocate's own premises from a validly authorised search merely because he is a lawyer.** Where a search authorisation under Section 67(2) of the CGST Act covers a firm's premises, the fact that one cabin within those premises is used by a practising advocate does not, by itself, render the search of that cabin unauthorised.
- **Privilege attaches to the client's communications, not to the advocate's person.** The Court held that the protection under Section 132 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 exists for the benefit of the client and cannot be invoked to shield material or conduct concerning the advocate's own independent affairs, where the Department has placed prima facie material suggesting the advocate was personally involved in the transactions under investigation.
- **CBIC's administrative instructions and the Master Circular regulate, but cannot override, the statutory search power.** Alleged non-compliance with Instructions No. 02/2022 or the Master Circular on Show Cause Notice, Adjudication and Recovery does not, without a demonstrated defect in the statutory authorisation itself, invalidate a search conducted under Section 67.
- **Absence of a show cause notice at the investigation stage is not a ground to quash a search.** The Court held that a SCN is a subsequent adjudicatory step, and its absence at the stage of investigation and summons does not affect the legality of a search conducted under Section 67(2).
- **The remedy for the risk of exposing unrelated clients' confidential data lies in cloning-and-filtering safeguards, not in invalidating the search.** Where an advocate's seized device may contain material belonging to clients unconnected with the investigation, courts are likely to prescribe supervised cloning, identification and restricted access rather than set aside the seizure altogether — and an advocate who declines a court-offered opportunity to identify unrelated client files risks losing the benefit of that protection.

## 1. Introduction

Section 67(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 empowers a proper officer to search premises and seize goods, documents, books or things where there is "reason to believe" that they are relevant to a proceeding under the Act. The provision is a powerful and increasingly used tool in GST enforcement, particularly in cases involving alleged fake invoicing, fraudulent input tax credit and, as this case illustrates, tax evasion routed through intermediary entities. A distinct and largely untested question arises when the premises searched belong to, or include, the office of a practising advocate: does the professional status of the occupant, and the possibility that privileged client communications may be swept up in the search, limit or condition the exercise of the statutory power?

*Puneet Batra v. Union of India*, decided by the Delhi High Court on 18 September 2026, confronts that question directly. GST intelligence officers searched the office of a tax consultancy firm run by an advocate's family, seized a CPU from the advocate's own cabin, and pursued him with a series of summons — all in connection with an investigation into an alleged scheme for evading GST on payments routed to an offshore online gambling website. The advocate argued that his professional status, and the advocate-client privilege attaching to material in his possession, rendered the search and seizure unauthorised. The Department countered that he was not merely a lawyer for the company under investigation but was himself a financial beneficiary of the scheme. This article sets out the facts, traces the eight-month procedural history that preceded the final judgment, and analyses how the Court resolved the tension between the statutory search power, advocate-client privilege, and the practical risk that an advocate's computer may hold confidential material belonging to entirely unconnected clients.

## 2. Case summary and background

The petitioner, Puneet Batra, is a practising advocate and a member of the Delhi High Court Bar Association, the Sales Tax Bar Association, and the New Delhi Bar Association at Patiala House. His family runs a tax consultancy, M/s Bass Legal LLP, from a roughly 900-square-foot office in Mayur Vihar, New Delhi, comprising a common staff area and two cabins — one used by the petitioner's father and the other by the petitioner himself. From 2023, the petitioner rendered professional services, including GST filings, Registrar of Companies compliance, income tax returns and cyber-crime assistance, to M/s Matkarma Technology Pvt. Ltd. ("MTPL"), a gaming company. On 4 and 5 September 2024, the GST Department searched MTPL's registered premises; the petitioner, as an authorised representative, was present. He states that MTPL later became non-responsive, and his team withdrew its vakalatnama by email on 6 September 2024. Despite this, he received and responded to a succession of summons from the Anti-Evasion Branch, CGST Delhi East, dated 22 September 2024, 1 October 2024, 10 June 2025 and 19 June 2025, appearing and furnishing a statement on 27 June 2025.

The dispute in the writ petition arose from a search conducted on 25 July 2025 at the Bass Legal office, pursuant to an authorisation dated 24 July 2025 issued by the Additional Commissioner, Anti-Evasion, CGST Delhi East Commissionerate, under Section 67(2) of the CGST Act. Officers seized documents relating to MTPL, the firm's partnership deed, and a CPU with 1,250 GB of storage from the petitioner's cabin, recorded in a panchnama of the same date. A fresh summons was issued the same day for the petitioner's appearance on 28 July 2025. He instead approached the Delhi High Court on an urgent basis.

The procedural history that followed spanned more than a year. On 28 July 2025, the Court restrained the Department from opening or downloading the CPU's contents without the petitioner's presence, pending an affidavit on the basis for investigating him. On 4 August 2025, the Department was directed to place supporting material in a sealed cover, along with any remand applications filed in respect of three arrests made in the investigation. By 1 September 2025, the Department had filed a note alleging the petitioner's role in MTPL's affairs, which it said involved an online gaming operation. In an order dated 9 September 2025, after the petitioner's senior counsel objected — relying on the Supreme Court's decision in *Madhyamam Broadcasting Limited v. Union of India* — to reliance on sealed-cover material without disclosure to him, the Court directed detailed safeguards for the CPU's examination, cloning and identification of relevant data, to be supervised by Local Commissioners. A Board Resolution of MTPL dated 29 September 2025 subsequently recorded the company's consent to hand over its own data held by the petitioner and Bass Legal to the CGST authorities. The matter was finally heard and reserved on 3 September 2026, and the judgment was pronounced on 18 September 2026.

The Department's case, as it stood at final hearing, was that MTPL had received approximately ₹1,306 crore from users for participation in gambling on an offshore website (referred to in the record as winner11.com), attracting GST at 28% amounting to ₹365.68 crore, and that the petitioner was not acting merely as MTPL's lawyer but was a direct financial beneficiary — allegedly entitled to 0.7% of every rupee deposited by a user — with access to the platform and to MTPL's bank account, and involvement in generating invoices without underlying supply of goods. The petitioner denied personal involvement, asserted advocate-client privilege over the seized material, and raised a series of procedural objections. The Court had to decide whether the search, the seizure of the CPU, and the summons issued to the petitioner were vitiated on any of these grounds.

## 3. Legal analysis

### 3.1 Validity of the search authorisation and the "advocate's cabin" objection

The Court's starting point was that the search authorisation of 24 July 2025 covered the premises of Bass Legal as a whole, not merely the cabin used by the petitioner's father. Examining the photographs on record, the Court found that the petitioner's cabin was physically part of, and opened into, the common office space of Bass Legal, and that the petitioner's own letterhead bore the same address. Having reviewed the "reasons to believe" recorded by the Additional Commissioner before authorising the search — a review the Court characterised as directed only at whether sufficient material existed for the exercise of the Section 67(2) power, not at the merits of the underlying allegations — the Court held the authorisation was validly issued and that a properly authorised search of the firm's premises could not become unauthorised merely because one cabin within those premises happened to be used by a practising advocate. The Court kept this issue analytically distinct from the separate question of what protection, if any, applied to material found in that cabin.

### 3.2 Advocate-client privilege: whose protection, and against what

On the privilege question, the Court's reasoning turned on locating the protection's proper beneficiary. It held that the privilege codified in Section 132 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 exists to protect the client, not the advocate personally: an advocate is barred from disclosing a professional communication without the client's consent precisely so that clients can communicate candidly without fear the communication will later be used against them. It follows, the Court reasoned, that the privilege attaches to the nature and origin of a communication or document — not to the mere fact that it happens to be found in an advocate's office. Material concerning an advocate's own independent affairs, transactions or conduct is not rendered untouchable simply because it sits in his cabin.

Applying that distinction, the Court accepted that the Department had placed prima facie material — statements of persons examined in the investigation, and evidence concerning an alleged financial benefit, access to the platform and to MTPL's bank account, and the deletion of files from the CPU during the search — indicating that the petitioner's own role went beyond providing professional services. The Court was careful to note that it was not returning any finding on the petitioner's ultimate guilt or liability; it was deciding only whether the Department had a sufficient basis to investigate beyond the petitioner's professional role, and whether that basis defeated a claim that the search should be treated as invalid in its entirety. It found that it did. As the Court put it, communications and material genuinely protected by advocate-client privilege remain entitled to protection, but that privilege "cannot operate as an absolute bar against an investigation into the conduct of the Advocate himself" once the Department has placed prima facie material suggesting the advocate acted beyond the role of legal adviser.

### 3.3 Disclosure of investigative material in sealed cover

The petitioner's reliance on *Madhyamam Broadcasting Limited v. Union of India* to resist reliance on undisclosed, sealed-cover material was rejected as inapposite: the Court noted that Madhyamam Broadcasting addressed a different set of issues framed in that case, and that the Union of India there had not contended the material formed part of an ongoing investigation or a police-style case diary — a characterisation squarely applicable to the material the GST Department had placed before the Court here. In its place, the Court drew on two Supreme Court decisions concerning police case diaries — *Balakram v. State of Uttarakhand & Ors.* and *Sidharth, Etc. Etc v. State of Bihar* — for the underlying principle that investigative records are not, as a matter of course, disclosed to a person under investigation merely because they have been placed before a court, since premature disclosure risks compromising an ongoing investigation. The Court also drew an analogy to Section 192(5) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which similarly restricts access to police case diaries, and to Section 130 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, which recognises protection for communications made to a public officer in official confidence. The Court was explicit that this reasoning was drawn by analogy from a different statutory context — criminal investigation, not a GST search — and that it was applying only the underlying principle, not a direct statutory rule, to the material before it.

### 3.4 Procedural objections: absence of SCN, alleged change of stance, and the Master Circular

The Court rejected each of the petitioner's remaining procedural objections in turn. The absence of a show cause notice against MTPL, it held, could not affect the legality of a search or summons issued at the investigation stage, since an SCN is a later, adjudicatory step that arises only if the investigation results in proceedings — a sequencing point the Court considered dispositive regardless of when, or whether, such a notice eventually issues. The petitioner's argument that the Department had "changed its stand" — first attributing the running of MTPL's affairs to another individual and later to the petitioner — was rejected on the footing that an investigation is not static, and that subsequent material implicating an additional person does not, without more, contradict an earlier statement about someone else's role. Reliance on Instructions No. 02/2022 and the Master Circular on Show Cause Notice, Adjudication and Recovery dated 10 March 2017, and on Clause 16.24 of the CBI Manual, 2020 governing seizure of digital evidence, fared no better: the Court held that administrative instructions and circulars regulate how a statutory power is exercised but cannot curtail or extinguish the power itself, so that an alleged procedural deviation from such guidance, absent a demonstrated defect in the statutory authorisation, does not render a search void.

### 3.5 Safeguarding third-party client data: the remedy the Court actually granted

Although the Court dismissed the writ petition and declined to hold the search or seizure illegal, it did not leave the petitioner's legitimate concern for unrelated clients' confidentiality unaddressed. It directed that the Department confine its further examination to the cloned copy of data prepared under the Court's earlier supervised procedure, restrict that examination to material relevant to the MTPL investigation, and refrain from accessing data belonging to any of the petitioner's other clients unrelated to MTPL's affairs — with liberty to approach the Court again if such data later became relevant. The Court noted that it had earlier offered the petitioner an opportunity to identify files belonging to his other clients so that they could be excluded from the material made available to the Department, an offer the petitioner declined. It also treated MTPL's own Board Resolution of 29 September 2025 — consenting to the handover of its data to the CGST authorities — as relevant to the present position regarding MTPL's own material, while expressly declining to treat that later consent as retrospectively validating a search that had already occurred on 25 July 2025.

### 3.6 A reasoned critique and what remains open

The judgment is carefully self-limiting. The Court expressly clarified that its conclusion should not be read as a general proposition diluting advocate-client privilege, and that it was confined to the specific circumstance where the Department had placed prima facie material suggesting the advocate's own, not merely his client's, involvement in the subject matter of investigation. That caveat is doctrinally important but leaves the threshold for what counts as sufficient "prima facie material" to displace a privilege claim largely unarticulated beyond the facts of this case — an alleged direct financial stake, access to bank accounts and platform credentials, and deletion of data during the search were treated cumulatively as sufficient, but the judgment does not indicate which of these, standing alone, might or might not have sufficed. Practitioners should also note that the Court reached its conclusion on the strength of sealed-cover material it examined but the petitioner never saw in full — a departure from ordinary adversarial disclosure that the Court justified by analogy to criminal case-diary protection, but which necessarily limits how far the reasoning on this specific point can be tested or relied upon in a future case with a different evidentiary posture.

## 4. Practical significance

For advocates and tax consultants who represent clients under GST investigation, this decision is a caution rather than a comfort. A validly authorised search of a firm's premises will not be defeated merely by pointing to the professional status of the person whose cabin is searched; and once the Department places even prima facie material suggesting an advocate's personal financial or operational stake in the client's affairs, courts are likely to treat the advocate's own conduct as outside the privilege's protective scope, leaving the underlying investigation to proceed. Advocates who represent clients in high-risk sectors — the judgment's facts concerned offshore online gaming, an area GST authorities are actively investigating — should maintain clear, contemporaneous documentation distinguishing fee-for-service professional engagement from any commercial or financial interest in the client's business, since the absence of such a record leaves room for the Department's characterisation of the relationship to prevail at the interim stage.

For firms facing a Section 67(2) search of shared premises, the practical lesson from the Court's eventual remedy is to seek supervised safeguards — cloning, restricted access, and independent identification of unrelated client files — promptly, and to cooperate with any court-offered opportunity to segregate unconnected client data, since declining such an offer, as the petitioner did here, forfeits the protection it would otherwise confer. Compliance teams should raise CBIC instructions and Master Circulars as objections at the earliest stage, while recognising that such instruments will not by themselves invalidate a search resting on a facially valid statutory authorisation.

For taxpayers and their counsel more broadly, the decision reinforces that an absence of a show cause notice is not a basis to resist search or summons at the investigation stage, and that inconsistency in the Department's evolving theory of a case — as further evidence comes in — is unlikely, by itself, to be treated as an abuse of process. Finally, corporate clients under GST investigation should be aware that a board resolution consenting to the release of company data to investigators, even if passed after a search, can materially affect a professional adviser's ability to resist disclosure of that company's material going forward, even though it cannot retrospectively cure a search already concluded.

## 5. Conclusion

*Puneet Batra* addresses, for what appears to be the first time in this reported form, the collision between the GST Department's search and seizure power under Section 67(2) of the CGST Act and the advocate-client privilege attaching to a tax practitioner's office. The Delhi High Court's answer is a calibrated one: the privilege protects the client's communications, not an automatic sanctuary around the advocate's own premises or conduct, and once the Department shows prima facie material suggesting the advocate's personal involvement in the transactions under investigation, the search will stand — subject to safeguards that protect the confidentiality of unrelated clients whose data may have been swept up incidentally. The judgment stops well short of a general dilution of privilege, expressly confining itself to its own facts, but it signals that advocates cannot expect an automatic shield merely from their professional status once the Department's case travels from "representing the client" to "running the client's business."

For the wider GST bar, the decision is likely to matter less for its result — the petition was dismissed — than for the procedural template it leaves behind: sealed-cover placement of investigative material, court-supervised cloning of seized devices, and a structured opportunity for the searched party to carve out genuinely unrelated client data. As GST intelligence investigations increasingly reach into the offices of the professionals who advise the taxpayers under scrutiny, this template, more than the privilege holding itself, may prove to be the case's most durable contribution.

## FAQ

**Does advocate-client privilege prevent GST authorities from searching an advocate's office?**
Not automatically. The Delhi High Court held that a validly authorised search under Section 67(2) of the CGST Act of a firm's premises is not rendered unauthorised merely because one cabin is used by a practising advocate; privilege protects the client's communications, not the advocate's premises as such.

**Can GST authorities examine material found in an advocate's office if he claims to be merely representing a client under investigation?**
Where the Department places prima facie material suggesting the advocate's own personal involvement in the transactions under investigation — rather than only a professional advisory role — the Court held that privilege does not operate as an absolute bar to investigating the advocate's own conduct, though genuinely privileged client communications remain protected.

**What happens to confidential data belonging to an advocate's other, unconnected clients if his computer is seized?**
The Court did not invalidate the seizure but directed that the Department work only from a supervised cloned copy of the data, confine its examination to material relevant to the specific investigation, and refrain from accessing data belonging to unrelated clients — with the advocate given an opportunity to identify and carve out such material in advance.
